## Executive Summary

Developments in April highlight the rapidly evolving landscape of digital assets, artificial intelligence (AI), and central bank digital currencies (CBDCS) across Europe, the United Kingdom, Australia, the United States, and internationally. Key themes include:

- **Regulation of Crypto-Assets:** Europe's Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) regulation is a central focus, with reports from ESMA and EBA detailing guidelines on market abuse prevention, central contact points, reporting templates, and suitability assessments. The UK is also advancing its regulatory framework for crypto assets. Australia is developing an innovative digital asset industry. Dubai has a blockchain policy.

- **Digital Market Developments:** Reports address the competitive landscape for blockchain-based market infrastructures, including proposals for fostering experimentation. The SNB and AMF offer insights on digital asset market structures and pilot regimes. Deloitte projects 2025 as a key year for payment stablecoins, and crypto derivatives are recognised for their growing importance.

- **Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCS):** The ECB is actively exploring consumer attitudes, the future of money with the digital euro, offline payments, and rulebook development. The BoE and BIS are collaborating on Project Pyxtrial, and the BoE has published a digital pound experiment report focusing on offline payments. Citi is also examining digital dollars.

- **Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Finance:** ESMA guides using AI for investing, and the BoE addresses AI's implications for financial stability. The Hong Kong Institute for Monetary and Financial Research (HKIMR) is focused on responsible AI adoption in financial services. The BIS examines the AI supply chain.

- **Financial Crime:** The EBA has issued a 2024 report on payment fraud, and the FCA provides guidance on countering financial crime risks related to crypto-asset service providers (CASPs). The BIS has also assessed the money laundering and terrorist financing risks of CASPs. Nasdaq Verafin provides insights into financial crime in Europe.

- **Tokenisation:** Multiple reports from the IMF, Congressional Research Service, Tokeny, Pwc, Ripple, BIS, and HSBC discuss the transformative potential of tokenisation for financial markets, including its role in payments, addressing market inefficiencies, and the need for standardisation and security (including post-quantum security). The BIS has also released a final report on the tokenisation of promissory notes (Project Promissa).

- **What it means for firms:** Several reports discuss the implications of these developments for financial institutions, including regulatory compliance, risk management, and strategic considerations.

Overall, these documents indicate a global trend towards increased regulation and standardisation of digital assets, a growing interest in the potential of tokenisation, active exploration of CBDCs, and a focus on the opportunities and risks presented by AI in the financial sector.

## Europe

### MiCA: ESMA final report on guidelines on preventing and detecting market abuse under

From Article 92(3) of MiCA and Article 16 of the ESMA Regulation, these guidelines aim to foster consistent supervisory practices among EU competent authorities to prevent and detect market abuse in crypto assets. This includes insider dealing, unlawful disclosure of inside information, and market manipulation, ensuring the uniform application of Title VI of MiCA.

**Key Implications and Requirements for Competent Authorities:**

- **Compliance and Reporting:** Competent authorities must try to comply with these guidelines by incorporating them into national frameworks. They must notify ESMA within two months of the guidelines' publication whether they comply, intend to comply, or do not comply (stating reasons for non-compliance).

- **Proportional and Risk-Based Approach:** Supervision should be proportionate to the risks posed by different actors in the crypto-asset market (CASPS, issuers, traders, miners, social media influencers, etc.). Authorities must adopt a forward-looking, risk-based approach, remain vigilant to new forms of market abuse and adapt their surveillance with technological advancements.

- **Integration of Existing Practices:** Authorities are encouraged to integrate market abuse prevention and detection measures for crypto-assets into their supervisory frameworks, adapting or extending practices used for traditional financial instruments. This includes monitoring manipulative practices specific to crypto (e.g., MEV strategies, token supply manipulation, stablecoin backing) and considering social media monitoring.

- **Common Supervisory Culture:** Competent authorities should actively contribute to a common EU supervisory culture by sharing information, best practices, and experiences related to market abuse in crypto-assets. This could potentially lead to ESMA adopting supervisory convergence tools. Voluntary dialogue with other relevant authorities (consumer protection, anti-money laundering) is also encouraged.

- **Adequacy of Resources:** Authorities are encouraged to allocate dedicated staff with expertise in crypto-assets and related technologies, and to develop specific market surveillance tools, including data-driven approaches. Ongoing staff training is also essential.

- **Open Dialogue with Stakeholders:** Proactively engaging crypto market participants, experts, academics, and technology providers is crucial for understanding technological developments and emerging market integrity risks.

- **Initiatives to Promote Market Integrity:** Authorities should implement educational initiatives to raise awareness among market participants about behaviours constituting market abuse and relevant sanctions, using accessible language and various communication channels. Encouraging best practices beyond legal requirements for issuers, PPAETS, and other CASPS is also recommended.

- **Monitoring and Surveillance:** NCAS should conduct data-driven market monitoring and surveillance, including publicly available data, regulatory data from CASPS, and reconciliation of on-chain, off-chain, and cross-market data. Monitoring relevant communications on various platforms is also essential, using a risk-based approach and potentially automated tools.

- **Supervision of PPAETS' Systems:** Competent authorities must ensure that PPAETS' arrangements, systems, and procedures for preventing and detecting market abuse remain appropriate, adopting a risk-based approach to their supervision.

- **Reaction to STORS:** Adequate and proportionate procedures for analysing Suspicious Transaction and Order Reports (STORS) received from PPAETS must be in place, including clear steps, responsibilities, and criteria for grading reported behaviours.

- **ESMA Coordination:** Authorities should consider requesting ESMA's coordination in cross-border cases of suspected market abuse to ensure effective investigations and avoid undue burden on market participants.

- **Addressing Third-Country Obstacles:** Competent authorities must inform ESMA and other relevant authorities about CASP business models or interactions with third-country authorities that may hinder adequate supervision of cross-border market abuse, striving for a common supervisory approach in such cases.

### MiCA: EBA final report on extending RTS on central contact points under MLD4 to cover CASPS

This EBA final report outlines draft Regulatory Technical Standards (RTS) amending existing rules to mandate the appointment of central contact points (CCPS) not only for electronic money issuers (EMIS) and payment service providers (PSPS) but also for cryptoasset service providers (CASPS) operating across EU member states without a branch. This extension is a direct consequence of the updated Fourth Money Laundering Directive (MLD4) by the Wire and Cryptoasset Transfer Regulation (WCTR), which brought CASPS under the scope of AML/CFT regulations.

**What it means for firms:**

- **New Compliance Burden for CASPS:** CASPS operating in EU member states other than where their head office is located will likely face a new requirement to appoint a CCP. This will involve costs associated with establishing and maintaining this function.

- **Risk-Based and Proportional Approach:** The specific requirement for a CCP will be determined using a risk-based approach and the principle of proportionality. A key criterion is exceeding the host member state’s EUR 3 million threshold. Still, member states can also mandate a CCP if they assess a high risk of money laundering or terrorist financing, regardless of this threshold.

- **Enhanced AML/CFT Supervision:** Host member states will gain a direct point of contact within CASPS operating in their jurisdiction, facilitating more effective AML/CFT supervision and ensuring adherence to local regulations.

- **Standardised Supervisory Framework:** By extending the CCP requirement to CASPS, the EU aims to create a more consistent AML/CFT supervisory framework across different cross-border financial service providers.

- **Implementation Timeline:** CASPS need to monitor the progress of these draft RTS through the European Commission, Parliament, and Council. Once endorsed and published, they must prepare to implement the requirements for appointing CCPS within the specified timeframe.

- **Potential for Divergent Application:** While the RTS aim for harmonisation, the discretion given to member states to impose the CCP requirement based on risk assessment could still lead to some divergence in application across the EU.

### MiCA: Official translations of EBA guidelines on reporting templates under MiCA

The European Banking Authority (EBA) has published official translations of its guidelines (EBA/GL/2024/16) on reporting templates to assist competent authorities in supervising issuers' compliance with Titles III and IV of the Markets in Crypto-assets (MiCA) Regulation (EU) 2023/1114. These guidelines are addressed to **competent authorities, asset-referenced tokens (ARTS) issuers of e-money tokens (EMTS)**.

The guidelines specify standard **templates and instructions** for:

- Issuers are to provide competent authorities and the EBA with the necessary information to cover identified reporting data gaps, particularly concerning **own funds and liquidity requirements**, and data needed to **assess the significance** of ARTS and EMTS.

- Issuers must collect necessary data from relevant **cryptoasset service providers (CASPS)**.

These guidelines aim to ensure a **common supervisory approach** with consistent and predefined templates across EU Member States, enhancing **supervisory convergence** and ensuring a **level playing field** within the internal market.

**Key implications and firm points:**

- **Mandatory Templates:** Issuers of ARTS and EMTS will be required to use the specified templates to report data related to their funds, reserves of assets, liquidity, and other information relevant to supervisory oversight and significance assessment.

- **Data Gap Coverage:** The templates address data gaps identified by the EBA that were not adequately covered by the initial reporting requirements under Article 22 of MICA. This will enable competent authorities and the EBA to monitor compliance and assess significance effectively.

- **Data Collection from CASPS:** Issuers are instructed to use specific templates to collect necessary data from CASPS, ensuring consistency in the information received. This includes data on token holders and transaction volumes.

- **Harmonised Reporting:** The guidelines aim to harmonise data collection across Member States, reducing the burden on issuers who might otherwise face differing requests from national authorities.

- **Significance Assessment:** The collected data will be crucial for the EBA to assess the significance criteria for ARTS and EMTS as outlined in Mica. This could lead to certain issuers being subject to more stringent requirements and direct EBA supervision.

- **Implementation Timeline:** The guidelines will apply from **26 May 2025**, two months after the official translations' publication date. National competent authorities must notify the EBA by this date whether they comply with or intend to comply with the guidelines.

- **Quarterly Reporting:** Issuers will generally be required to submit the information quarterly, with specific reference and remittance dates provided.

- **Proportionality:** While the core templates apply to issuers with an issue value higher than EUR 100,000,000, competent authorities may require smaller issuers to report the same data.

- **Data Formats:** Issuers must submit the information in the data exchange formats specified by competent authorities and according to the data point model and validation rules provided by the EBA.

- **CASP Involvement:** CASPS must provide issuers with the requested data using the specified templates and instructions, including a specific anonymisation process for sharing information on natural person token holders.

These guidelines establish a standardised framework for data reporting under Mica, empowering supervisors with the necessary information to effectively oversee ART and EMT issuers, assess their significance, and ensure compliance with crucial regulatory requirements across the European Union. Issuers and CASPS must prepare their systems and processes to meet these new reporting obligations by the specified implementation date.

### MiCA: CSSF - Guidelines on assessing the suitability of management members and shareholders in issuers of asset-referenced tokens and crypto-asset service providers

This Circular CSSF 25/875 informs entities under Luxembourg's regulatory purview regarding the **immediate application** by the Commission de Surveillance du Secteur Financier (CSSF) of the joint European Banking Authority (EBA) and European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) Guidelines on the suitability assessment of:

1. **Members of the management body** of issuers of asset-referenced tokens (ARTS) and crypto-asset service providers (CASPS) (EBA/GL/2024/09; ESMA75-453128700-10 - Part 1 of the Circular).

2. **Shareholders and members** (direct or indirect) with qualifying holdings in issuers of ARTS and CASPS (EBA/GL/2024/09; ESMA75-453128700-10 - Part 2 of the Circular).

These guidelines, published on 4 December 2024 and effective from 4 February 2025, establish standard reference parameters for competent authorities across the EU to assess the suitability of these key individuals and entities. The CSSF has integrated these guidelines into its administrative practice and regulatory approach to promote supervisory convergence at the European level.

**Summary of the Guidelines' Content:**

- **Suitability of Management Body Members:** The guidelines detail the criteria for assessing the good repute (including absence of relevant criminal records and sanctions), individual knowledge, skills, experience, and sufficient time commitment of management body members. They also emphasise the importance of collective suitability, ensuring the management body possesses the necessary expertise. The guidelines outline the responsibilities of ART issuers and CASPS in performing these assessments and the role of competent authorities in supervising them.

- **Suitability of Shareholders/Members with Qualifying Holdings:** These guidelines focus on assessing the good repute of proposed acquirers with qualifying holdings at the authorisation stage and the suitability (based on reputation, competence of those directing the business, financial soundness, compliance with regulatory requirements, and absence of ML/TF suspicions) of proposed acquirers of qualifying holdings in already authorised entities. They also address the specific considerations when the funds for acquisition involve crypto-assets.

**Firm Implications for Entities Under CSSF Jurisdiction:**

- **Immediate Compliance:** Applicant and authorised issuers of ARTS and CASPS in Luxembourg must immediately adhere to these EBA/ESMA guidelines as part of the CSSF's regulatory requirements. This necessitates reviewing and potentially updating their internal policies and procedures for assessing management body members and qualifying shareholders.

- **Enhanced Scrutiny:** The CSSF will apply these harmonised European standards when assessing the suitability of management body members during the authorisation process and on an ongoing basis. Similarly, the suitability of shareholders with qualifying holdings will be subject to this enhanced scrutiny during authorisation and when changes in ownership occur.

- **Detailed Assessment Criteria:** Entities must understand and implement the detailed criteria outlined in the guidelines regarding good repute, knowledge, skills, experience, time commitment (for management body members), financial soundness (for qualifying shareholders), and the absence of money laundering or terrorist financing concerns.

- **Proportionality:** While the guidelines establish standard parameters, the principle of proportionality should be applied, considering the size, internal organisation, and complexity of the ART issuer or CASP. Significant ART issuers and larger CASPS providing more critical services will likely face more sophisticated suitability assessment expectations.

- **Documentation and Reporting:** Entities must thoroughly document their suitability assessments for management body members and qualifying shareholders. They should also be prepared to provide this information to the CSSF during the authorisation process and ongoing supervision.

- **Impact on Governance:** The guidelines will directly impact the governance structures of ART issuers and CASPS, influencing the selection, appointment, and ongoing evaluation of their management bodies and the acceptance of shareholders with significant influence.

- **Specific Considerations for Crypto-Assets:** When assessing the source of funds for acquiring qualifying holdings, especially if they involve crypto-assets, entities and the CSSF must consider particular information related to distributed ledger addresses, crypto-asset account numbers, and the involved crypto-asset service providers to mitigate ML/TF risks.

- **Supervisory Convergence:** The CSSF's adoption of these guidelines signifies a move towards greater supervisory convergence across the European Union in the crypto-asset sector. This ensures consistent scrutiny and a level playing field for ART issuers and CASPS operating within the EU.

In conclusion, this CSSF circular has significant and immediate implications for entities dealing with ARTS and crypto-assets in Luxembourg. They must familiarise themselves with the detailed requirements of the EBA/ESMA guidelines and adapt their internal processes to ensure compliance with the CSSF's regulatory expectations regarding the suitability of their management and significant shareholders.

### Digital Markets: TOWARDS A MORE COMPETITIVE EUROPEAN PILOT REGIME: A PROPOSAL FOR FOSTERING EXPERIMENTATION BY BLOCKCHAIN-BASED MARKET INFRASTRUCTURES

The report focuses on the European Pilot Regime, designed to encourage using Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT) in market infrastructures. The Autorité des Marchés Financiers (AMF—France) and the Commissione Nazionale per le Società e la Borsa (Consob—Italy) argue that the regime's adoption has been limited and propose improvements to enhance its competitiveness.

Key proposed improvements include:

- **A More Flexible Regulatory Framework:**

  - Greater proportionality and adaptability to project size and nature.

  - Easier access to the "DLT Trading and Settlement System" (DLT TSS) status.

  - Expanded use of e-money tokens and tokenised deposits for settlement.

- **Broadening the Scope and Ensuring Long-Term Visibility:**

  - Increased admission and capitalisation limits for larger-scale projects.

  - Extension of eligible assets to include structured bonds and derivatives.

  - Admission of "Organised Trading Facility" status.

  - Clarification of the Pilot Regime's duration to secure investments.

- **Developing Interoperability and Raising Market Awareness:**

  - Better integration between DLT and traditional infrastructures.

  - Implementation of common technical standards.

  - Increased awareness among issuers and investors about tokenisation.

The overall goal is to foster the deployment of DLT within the European Union's financial ecosystem and strengthen its competitiveness.

**Firm Implications**

**For Financial Institutions (Banks, Investment Firms, Exchanges):**

- **Increased Opportunity for DLT Adoption:** The proposed changes would likely create a more favourable environment for experimenting with and implementing DLT solutions. This could lead to:

  - **New Products and Services:** Tokenisation of a broader range of assets (including structured bonds and derivatives) could enable the development of innovative financial products.

  - **Efficiency Gains:** DLT can streamline processes related to trading, settlement, and post-trade activities, potentially reducing costs and increasing efficiency.

  - **Competitive Pressure:** Firms that do not adopt DLT may face competitive pressure from those that do.

- **Need for Technological Investment:** Implementing DLT solutions requires significant technological infrastructure and development investments. Firms need to be prepared to allocate resources to these initiatives.

- **Focus on Interoperability:** To avoid market fragmentation, firms must prioritise the development of systems that can interoperate with traditional and DLT-based infrastructures.

- **Regulatory Adaptation:** Firms must stay informed about and adapt to the evolving regulatory landscape surrounding DLT and tokenisation.

**For Technology Providers:**

- **Growing Market for DLT Solutions:** Financial institutions’ increased adoption of DLT will create a growing market for technology providers that offer DLT-related products and services.

- **Importance of Standards:** The call for common technical standards highlights the need for technology providers to develop solutions that are interoperable and compliant with industry standards.

- **Collaboration Opportunities:** Technology providers must collaborate with financial institutions and regulators to develop and implement effective DLT solutions.

**For Issuers of Financial Instruments:**

- **New Funding and Capital Raising Options:** Tokenisation can provide companies new ways to raise capital and access funding.

- **Increased Transparency and Efficiency:** DLT can increase transparency and efficiency in issuing and managing financial instruments.

- **Need for Education:** Issuers must be educated about tokenisation's benefits and opportunities to encourage greater adoption.

**For Investors:**

- **Access to New Investment Opportunities:** Tokenisation can provide investors access to new types of financial assets and investment opportunities.

- **Increased Efficiency and Liquidity:** DLT can increase the efficiency and liquidity of financial markets, making it easier for investors to buy and sell assets.

- **Need for Investor Education:** Investors must know the risks and benefits of investing in tokenised assets.

**For Regulators (AMF, Consob, ESMA):**

- **Balancing Innovation and Risk:** Regulators face the challenge of fostering innovation in the financial sector while ensuring investor protection and market stability.

- **Developing Expertise:** Regulators must develop expertise in DLT and tokenisation to oversee this evolving market effectively.

- **Promoting Harmonisation:** Regulators need to work together to facilitate the harmonisation of regulations and standards across the European Union.

This report signals a push for a more dynamic and competitive European market for DLT-based financial instruments. This has wide-ranging implications, prompting firms to innovate, invest strategically in technology, and adapt to a changing regulatory environment.

### AI: ESMA - Using Artificial Intelligence for Investing: What you should consider

This report from the European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) warns the public about the **risks of using publicly available artificial intelligence (AI) tools for investment decisions**. The report highlights the increasing popularity of AI tools that offer investment advice and stock signals. While acknowledging their potential, ESMA emphasises that these tools have significant risks and limitations. Key concerns include the lack of regulatory oversight, the potential for inaccurate or misleading advice based on flawed or outdated data, the difficulty in understanding how these AI models function, and the absence of investor protection mechanisms typically offered by authorised financial firms. ESMA strongly advises against relying solely on these AI tools, urges users to seek multiple perspectives from authorised professionals, and cautions against "get-rich-quick" schemes promising high returns through AI. Furthermore, the document stresses the importance of protecting personal data when interacting with these tools due to potential security vulnerabilities.

**What it means for firms:**

ESMA's warning carries several firm implications for investors and the financial industry:

- **Increased Scrutiny of AI in Finance:** Regulatory bodies like ESMA are paying close attention to the growing use of AI in investment and are likely to increase scrutiny and potentially introduce regulations in the future to protect investors.

- **Need for Investor Education:** There's a clear need for greater public awareness regarding the limitations and risks of AI-powered investment tools. Investors must be educated to approach these tools sceptically and understand that they are not a substitute for professional financial advice.

- **Reinforcement of Traditional Advice Channels:** The document implicitly reinforces the value of regulated and authorised financial advisors obligated to act in the client's best interest and provide tailored advice. Investors should be encouraged to prioritise these channels for critical financial decisions.

- **Caution Regarding "Black Box" AI:** The warning about the lack of understanding of how some AI models work highlights a broader concern about the transparency and explainability of AI in finance. This could lead to pressure for more transparent and auditable AI systems.

- **Data Privacy Concerns:** The emphasis on protecting personal data when using these tools underscores the importance of data security and privacy in AI applications. Developers and users of these tools need to be mindful of data protection regulations.

- **Liability and Responsibility:** The lack of regulatory oversight for public AI tools raises questions about liability in case of financial losses resulting from their advice. This area may see legal and regulatory developments as the use of AI in investment grows.

- **Market Integrity:** The warning against inaccurate or misleading AI-generated advice points to potential risks to market integrity if investors make decisions based on flawed information. Regulators will be concerned about maintaining fair and efficient markets.

- **Competitive Landscape:** While cautioning against risks, the document acknowledges AI’s "innovative potential.” This suggests that the financial industry will likely continue to explore and integrate AI, but with a greater emphasis on responsible development and deployment, potentially under future regulatory frameworks.

In conclusion, ESMA's statement strongly cautions investors to exercise prudence when using public AI tools for investment. It also signals the financial industry that regulators know the potential risks and are likely to examine this evolving landscape more closely to ensure investor protection and market integrity.

### CBDC: ECB - Consumer attitudes towards a central bank digital currency

This working paper explores European consumer attitudes towards introducing a digital euro, examining communication effectiveness, fund allocation, and the impact of holding limits.

**Summary of Key Findings:**

- **Effective Communication:** A short video explaining the digital euro's features significantly increased consumer adoption likelihood by 12 percentage points. However, most consumers didn't seek additional information even when readily available, suggesting a need for repeated and targeted communication.

- **Habit Persistence:** Many consumers prefer existing payment methods, posing a broader digital euro adoption challenge.

- **Limited Wealth Allocation:** When presented with a hypothetical €10,000 windfall, consumers allocated only a small portion to the digital euro, with minimal impact on other liquid assets.

- **Minimal Impact of Holding Limits:** Holding limits between €1,000 and €10,000 had little effect on liquid asset allocation, with no significant bunching observed at higher thresholds.

**What it means for firms:**

- **Importance of Clear Communication:** Central banks must effectively communicate the practical benefits of CBDCS to encourage adoption and address consumer preferences for existing payment methods.

- **Limited Impact on Financial Stability:** The introduction of a digital euro is unlikely to cause a significant shift away from traditional bank deposits, based on current consumer intentions.

- **Flexibility in Holding Limits:** Holding limits within a reasonable range appear to have a minimal impact on consumer behaviour, providing policymakers with flexibility.

- **Need for Continued Research:** Further research is needed to understand long-term consumer behaviour and the potential impact of CBDCS on the financial system.

- **Consideration of Demographic Factors:** Communication strategies should account for demographic differences, as older and less financially literate consumers are less inclined to seek information about CBDCS.

- **Emphasis on Offline Functionality:** Highlighting the offline capabilities of a digital euro, similar to cash, may encourage adoption.

- **No Spillover to Crypto:** Introducing a digital euro is unlikely to significantly impact investment in other digital assets like cryptocurrencies.

- **Communication Timing:** The effects of communication about CBDCS fade relatively quickly, necessitating repeated messaging.

### CBDC: ECB - Digital euro: The future of money

The digital euro is intended as a digital version of central bank money, providing cash-like features in the digital space. It aims to complement existing payment methods and will be free for individuals. Key factors for its introduction include modernising the euro for the digital age, addressing challenges in the European payments sector (such as dependence on non-European providers and the absence of a pan-European solution), and maintaining Europe's strategic autonomy and monetary sovereignty.

The digital euro's design prioritises user choice in terms of use cases (P2P, POS, e-commerce), devices (smartphones, cards, web), and connectivity (online and offline). ### Digital Markets: Future financial services regulatory regime for cryptoassets (regulated activities)

This Policy Note from April 2025 provides commentary on the draft Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Regulated Activities and Miscellaneous Provisions) (Cryptoassets) Order 2025 (the "SI"). It explains the UK Treasury's intended policy outcomes for the new regulatory regime for cryptoassets and how the draft legislation aims to achieve them. A key change is that the government will not amend the Payments Services Regulations 2017 to bring UK-issued stablecoins into regulated payments *at this time*. However, they acknowledge the potential of stablecoins in payments.

- **Scope of the Draft SI:** This specific draft focuses on creating new regulated activities under the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Regulated Activities) Order 2001 (the "RAO") and related amendments to other legislation, including anti-money laundering and financial promotions. Provisions for market abuse and admissions/disclosures will be published later.

- **New Definitions:** The SI defines "qualifying cryptoassets" (fungible and transferable cryptoassets, including "qualifying stablecoin" but excluding "specified investment cryptoassets" and instruments like tokenised e-money/deposits) and "qualifying stablecoin" (stablecoins referencing fiat currencies and aiming to maintain stable value through backing assets). "Specified investment cryptoassets" are cryptoassets that also meet the definition of a specified investment (e.g., tokenised equity).

- **New Regulated Activities:** The draft SI introduces several new regulated activities requiring FCA authorisation:

  - **Issuing a qualifying stablecoin in the UK:** Encompasses offering, redemption, and maintaining the stablecoin's value from a UK establishment.

  - **Safeguarding (custody) of qualifying cryptoassets and relevant specified investment cryptoassets:** Extends the existing safeguarding regime for traditional securities to their tokenised forms. Temporary holding for trade settlement is excluded.

  - **Operating a qualifying cryptoasset trading platform:** Covers platforms where qualifying cryptoassets are exchanged for other qualifying cryptoassets or money (including e-money).

  - **Dealing in qualifying cryptoassets as principal:** Intended to capture cryptoasset lending and borrowing services.

  - **Dealing in qualifying cryptoassets as an agent.**

  - **Arranging deals in qualifying cryptoassets:** Intended to capture the operation of cryptoasset lending platforms.

  - **Qualifying cryptoasset staking:** Includes liquid staking, while the issuance of liquid staking tokens falls under the dealing activity.

- **Decentralised Finance (DeFi):** The draft SI does not include specific provisions for DeFi. Authorisation will only be required if an identifiable person or entity is undertaking a regulated activity by way of business, with the FCA determining this on a case-by-case basis.

- **Geographic Perimeter:** The regulatory perimeter ensures that firms dealing directly or indirectly with UK retail consumers in activities like operating trading platforms, dealing, and arranging deals in qualifying cryptoassets need UK authorisation, regardless of location. An exception exists for firms dealing with UK consumers through an authorised intermediary. Firms serving only UK institutional customers do not need authorisation. Safeguarding and stakeholder activities require UK authorisation if carried out in the UK or for UK consumers, except for firms safeguarding under the direction of an authorised entity. Stablecoin issuers need authorisation if operating from a UK establishment.

New controlled activities under the FPO include safeguarding, operating trading platforms, and qualifying cryptoasset staking. ### CBDC: Boe/ BIS - Project Pyxtrial

Project Pyxtrial is a joint initiative by the BIS Innovation Hub and the Bank of England to explore technology solutions for monitoring the balance sheets of asset-backed stablecoins. Motivated by the growing stablecoin market (\$162 billion as of June 2024), developing regulations, and lacking appropriate supervisory technology (Supertech), the project developed a prototype data analytics pipeline.

The core purpose is to provide supervisors with insight into whether a stablecoin issuer's backing assets consistently exceed its liabilities (coins in circulation). An asset-liability mismatch could undermine confidence, potentially triggering a "run" and posing financial stability risks if the stablecoin is systemically important.

Pyxtrial's architecture involves a data model, a database, and APIS. It collects on-chain data (public, primarily liabilities/circulating supply) and off-chain data (non-public, mainly assets). It uses APIS to pull on-chain data hourly from various blockchains (tested on five stablecoins across five blockchains, representing 13 tokens). Off-chain asset data relies on simulated data based on infrequent (monthly/quarterly) issuer reports, using algorithms to match the frequency of liabilities data. It employs the Digital Token Identifier (DTI) methodology to accurately track liabilities across different blockchains, overcoming limitations of traditional identifiers like ISINS.

The collected data is stored in a database and made available to supervisors via a dashboard. This enables near real-time monitoring of stablecoin balance sheets and flags potential inconsistencies. The design is modular and flexible, allowing components (APIS, database, dashboard) to be adapted or replaced.

Key challenges encountered included the lack of finalised stablecoin regulations (requiring a flexible data model based on drafts or proxies like MMF rules) and the difficulty in obtaining frequent, standardised, and granular data on backing assets (which are often self-declared by issuers).

The project demonstrated the feasibility of monitoring stablecoin backing through technology. It identified that while Pyxtrial is a functional proof of concept (PoC) providing a template for future tools, it is not a ready-made product. It highlights the value of integrating on-chain and off-chain data for supervision and the potential benefits for risk monitoring and cross-border cooperation.

**What it means for firms:**

The Project Pyxtrial document carries significant implications for regulators, supervisors, stablecoin issuers, and the future regulatory landscape:

1. **Need for Regulatory Data Requirements:** The project highlights a critical dependency: for a tool like Pyxtrial to be fully effective, regulators *must* mandate that stablecoin issuers provide frequent, standardised, and granular data on their backing assets. The reliance on infrequent, self-declared reports is insufficient for near-real-time monitoring. Pyxtrial functions best assuming that such regulatory reporting requirements will be in place.

2. **Requires Specific Supervisory Skills:** Implementing and operating a suptech tool like Pyxtrial necessitates multidisciplinary teams within regulatory bodies. Supervisors will need expertise in traditional financial regulation, cryptoassets, data engineering, and information technology to source, process, and interpret the data.

3. **Prudential Focus is Possible:** Pyxtrial demonstrates that a microprudential approach focused on the balance sheet health of individual stablecoin issuers is technically feasible, addressing a current gap in available solutions.

4. **Data Quality and Valuation are Crucial Dependencies:** While Pyxtrial can monitor asset-liability mismatches based on reported values, it does not assess the *quality* of assets or the *rigour* of their valuation. The tool's effectiveness depends heavily on the accuracy and quality of the data that issuers or third parties provide. Regulators may need to set rules or guidance on data quality and valuation methods.

5. **Facilitates Timelier Risk Monitoring:** By providing access to more frequent data in a standardised format, Pyxtrial enables supervisors to identify potential issues with stablecoin backing earlier than relying solely on historical reports.

6. **Potential for Cross-Border Cooperation:** The tool's ability to standardise data could be valuable for cross-border supervision, allowing host regulators to assess the backing of stablecoins issued in other jurisdictions, provided data sharing arrangements are in place.

7. **Template for Future Suptech:** Pyxtrial is a template for how future regulatory reporting and monitoring tools for digital assets could be built, demonstrating that suptech can be developed in parallel with evolving regulatory frameworks.

8. **Applicability Beyond Stablecoins:** The approach used in Pyxtrial (monitoring on-chain liabilities against off-chain backing assets) potentially applies to supervising other tokenised assets backed by real-world assets, such as tokenised securities or funds.

9. **Implementation Requires Effort:** Deploying Pyxtrial as a production system requires further testing, refinement, and careful consideration of implementation challenges, including integrating existing systems, managing data sharing (especially for confidential data), and ensuring appropriate change management for staff.

10. **Highlights On-Chain/Off-Chain Challenges:** The project underscores the complexity of integrating data from inherently different sources (public, real-time on-chain vs. private, infrequent off-chain) and the need for supervisory processes that account for these differences. The fact that some liabilities data is also off-chain suggests a purely on-chain supervisory process is unlikely.

### CBDC: BoE - Digital pound experiment report: Offline payments

- **Technology Choices:** Several technology options exist for offline payments, but their suitability depends on policy decisions related to risk appetite, product design, and liability.

  - **Synchronous:** Requires real-time interaction from both payer and payee.

  - Secure elements (microprocessor chips) were used to mitigate double-spending and counterfeiting.

- **User Authentication:** Standard mechanisms like Consumer Device Customer Verification Method (CDCVM) and PIN were implemented.

- **Communication Technology:** NFC (Near-Field Communication) and a combination of QR codes and BLE (Bluetooth Low Energy) were used for offline communication between devices, each with advantages and limitations.

### Tokenisation: IMF - Tokenisation and Financial Market Inefficiencies

This IMF Fintech Note examines the potential impact of tokenisation on financial markets, focusing on how it might alleviate or exacerbate existing market inefficiencies. The authors define tokenisation as creating asset representations on a shared, programmable, and trusted digital ledger. They analyse inefficiencies in terms of market frictions (information asymmetries, search problems, transaction costs, counterparty risks) across the asset lifecycle (issuance, trading, servicing, redemption) and broader issues like externalities, internalities, and market power. The note posits that tokenisation's benefits in reducing frictions stem primarily from increased **sharedness**(more participants on a unified ledger) and **programmability** (smart contracts automating processes). Trust in the ledger's integrity is a prerequisite for materialising these benefits.

**What it means for firms:**

- **Reduced Transaction Costs and Frictions:** Tokenisation can lower costs and frictions across the asset lifecycle. Native token issuance could bypass registrars and custodians. Programmable ledgers can automate auctions and embed asset information, improving transparency. Simultaneous settlement via smart contracts can mitigate counterparty risk in immediate trades, and shared ledgers can enable faster settlement, reducing opportunity costs. Increased sharedness can also alleviate search frictions in less liquid OTC markets. Automated servicing of tokenised assets can also reduce costs.

- **Increased Systemic Risk:** While reducing individual transaction risks, tokenisation could amplify systemic risk. Lower issuance costs might incentivise higher leverage. Programmability could facilitate rehypothecation. Reduced reliance on stable retail deposits, favouring other tokenised investments, could increase dependence on less stable wholesale funding. Greater interconnectedness through easier access to diverse and complex tokenised assets can accelerate shock transmission. Furthermore, faster trading speeds on shared and programmable ledgers could intensify market volatility and the impact of runs.

- **Heightened Operational Risk:** Greater sharedness can concentrate cyber risk, creating a single point of failure. Increased programmability introduces the risk of smart contract failures due to errors or malicious actors, with potentially broader impacts due to interconnectedness.

- **Ambiguous Impact on Network Externalities:** Increased sharedness and fractionalisation of assets via tokenisation could enhance market liquidity and attract more participants, creating positive network effects. However, a proliferation of non-interoperable tokenised ledgers could fragment markets, reduce liquidity, and diminish these benefits.

- **Potential for Increased Retail Investor Internalities:** Direct trading on tokenised ledgers could bypass financial advisors, potentially leading to ill-informed investment decisions and insufficient diversification. The ease of creating complex tokenised products through programmability could exacerbate these risks if retail investors don't fully understand them. The decentralised nature of permissionless ledgers could also make enforcing consumer protection regulations more challenging.

- **Altered Market Power Dynamics:** Shared ledgers could reduce investors' switching costs, fostering competition among brokers and potentially lowering fees. Direct access to issuers on tokenised ledgers could further challenge traditional brokerage models. However, the owner(s) of a dominant private tokenised ledger could accrue significant market power, potentially extracting monopoly rents through data access or fees, especially if network effects and high setup costs create barriers to entry for competitors.

- **Importance of Regulation:** The note emphasises that while tokenisation offers potential benefits, its adoption could also introduce or amplify existing risks. Policymakers must carefully evaluate their approach to regulating, adopting, and encouraging tokenisation to harness its advantages while mitigating potential negative consequences. Regulatory frameworks may need adaptation to address the specific characteristics of tokenised markets.

The crypto derivatives market has experienced substantial growth, with trading volumes often exceeding the spot market. This growth is fueled by increasing institutional participation, the development of more sophisticated trading tools, and a clearer regulatory landscape in some jurisdictions. Crypto derivatives, including futures, options, and perpetual contracts, allow investors to speculate on price movements, hedge risk, and gain leveraged exposure to cryptocurrencies without direct ownership. Both centralised and decentralised platforms offer these instruments, with innovation leading to new product types like UpDown options and staking yield swaps.

**What it means for firms:**

- **Increased Institutional Adoption and Market Maturation:** The rise of crypto derivatives signals increasing institutional investors' acceptance of cryptocurrencies as a legitimate asset class. This participation brings greater liquidity, potentially reduces volatility over time, and fosters the development of more robust market infrastructure, mirroring traditional financial markets.

- **Enhanced Risk Management and Trading Strategies:** Derivatives provide essential tools for hedging against the significant price volatility inherent in cryptocurrencies. This allows for more sophisticated trading strategies, attracting a wider range of participants, including those seeking to manage risk rather than speculate.

- **Bridging Traditional Finance and Crypto:** Crypto derivatives bridge traditional financial markets and the digital asset space. They allow institutions familiar with traditional derivatives to gain exposure to crypto without the complexities of direct custody and trading of the underlying assets, potentially leading to further crypto integration into mainstream finance.

- **Potential for Systemic Risk:** As the crypto derivatives market grows and becomes more interconnected with traditional finance, it introduces new potential sources of systemic risk. High leverage, market volatility, and the potential for contagion across platforms and asset classes require careful monitoring and appropriate regulatory frameworks to prevent destabilising effects on the broader financial system.

- **Regulatory Scrutiny and Development:** The increasing prominence of crypto derivatives necessitates more transparent and comprehensive regulatory oversight. Authorities worldwide are grappling with how to regulate these instruments best to protect investors, ensure market integrity, and mitigate systemic risks. This evolving regulatory landscape will significantly shape the future growth and structure of the crypto derivatives market.

- **Innovation and Market Evolution:** The crypto derivatives space is characterised by rapid innovation, with new products and platforms constantly emerging, including in the decentralised finance (DeFi) sector. This evolution offers new opportunities for investors but also presents challenges in terms of understanding and managing the risks associated with these novel instruments.

- **Increased Accessibility and Retail Participation:** While currently dominated by sophisticated and institutional traders, developing user-friendly platforms and products could increase retail participation in crypto derivatives. This necessitates a focus on investor education and appropriate safeguards to protect less experienced traders from the inherent risks of leveraged trading.

In conclusion, the growth of crypto derivatives as a primary digital asset class signifies a maturing market with increasing institutional involvement and sophisticated trading opportunities. However, it also brings forth significant implications for financial stability, regulation, and the need for robust risk management practices across the entire economic ecosystem.
