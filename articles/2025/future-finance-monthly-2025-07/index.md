## Executive Summary

In the **European Union**, the focus was on the granular implementation of the Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) and the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA). For firms in the crypto-asset space, the key themes from **MiCA** guidance are the need for absolute transparency, rigorous internal controls, and the understanding that national regulators will act as strict gatekeepers. The prohibition of shared global order books represents a fundamental operational shift. The implementation of **DORA** dominated the landscape, conveying an unequivocal message: financial entities retain ultimate responsibility for their operational resilience, regardless of whether they outsource. A comprehensive framework for overseeing Critical Third-Party Providers (CTPPs) is now in place, demanding intense scrutiny of the entire ICT supply chain. Furthermore, the EBA highlighted escalating financial crime risks from the FinTech and crypto-asset sectors, demanding stronger AML/CFT controls to combat AI-driven fraud and ensure sanctions compliance.

In the **United Kingdom**, the government unveiled the "Leeds Reforms," a major deregulatory package aimed at making the UK the most competitive global financial hub. The strategy signals a significant reduction in the regulatory burden by streamlining the Senior Managers & Certification Regime (SM&CR), accelerating authorisation times, and re-evaluating the impact of the Consumer Duty on wholesale markets. This pro-growth agenda is coupled with a significant push to digitise markets, featuring concrete plans for T+1 settlement and the elimination of paper share certificates. While fostering growth, regulators are also reinforcing standards in key areas, with a new focus on tackling non-financial misconduct and a continued crackdown on financial crime, particularly in the cryptoasset sector.

In **Australia**, regulators are actively fostering innovation through collaborative research. The milestone reached by **Project Acacia** demonstrates a proactive, hands-on approach to understanding the real-world application of tokenised assets and digital money, positioning the country as a key player in shaping the future of digital finance.

In **Hong Kong**, authorities launched a comprehensive and proactive strategy to become a premier global hub for digital assets. The "LEAP" framework moves beyond regulating exchanges to creating a complete, end-to-end licensed ecosystem for digital asset dealers and custodians. This is coupled with a clear focus on promoting Real-World Asset (RWA) tokenisation and establishing a high-bar, institutional-grade regime for stablecoin issuers, signalling a clear ambition to lead in the next phase of digital finance.

In the **United States**, lawmakers and regulators advanced several landmark proposals. The GENIUS Act seeks to create a federal charter for payment stablecoin issuers, bringing them into the banking regulatory perimeter. The CLARITY Act aims to resolve the long-standing jurisdictional dispute between the SEC and CFTC. Concurrently, the CBDC Anti-Surveillance State Act would prohibit the Federal Reserve from issuing a retail or wholesale CBDC without Congressional approval, while regulators provided detailed guidance for crypto ETPs and bank safekeeping of crypto-assets, signaling a move towards comprehensive market oversight.

## Europe

### 1. Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) Regulation

The focus for MiCA in July was on providing clarity for Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs) to ensure robust investor protection and market integrity as the regulation beds in.

#### a. ESMA Statement: Segregating Regulated and Unregulated Services

- **Summary:** ESMA issued a strong warning to CASPs against creating a "halo effect" where their regulated status under MiCA misleads clients into believing all their products (including unregulated ones) are protected.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Absolute Transparency:** Firms must clearly and prominently distinguish between regulated and unregulated services at every client touchpoint, from marketing materials to user interfaces (e.g., using pop-up acknowledgements).

  - **Operational Segregation:** Consider separating regulated and unregulated activities on websites and in client documentation to avoid confusion.

  - **Marketing Restrictions:** Do not use your MiCA-regulated status as a promotional tool for unregulated services.

- [🔗 Link](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA35-1872330276-2329_-_MiCA_Statement_Access_to_Unregulated_Activities.pdf)

#### b. ESMA Final Report: Guidelines on Staff Knowledge and Competence

- **Summary:** ESMA finalised guidelines detailing the minimum knowledge and competence requirements for staff at CASPs who provide information or advice.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Formalised Training:** Firms must implement structured training programs. New staff giving *information* need ~80 hours of qualification and 6 months of experience, while those giving *advice* need significantly more (~160 hours of qualification and 1 year of experience).

  - **Mandatory CPD:** All relevant staff must undertake annual Continuing Professional Development (CPD), with suggested minimums of 10 hours (information) and 20 hours (advice).

  - **Increased Compliance & Record Keeping:** CASPs must establish robust internal policies to assess and document staff competence, maintain detailed records, and be prepared to provide them to regulators upon request. A higher standard is explicitly set for advisory roles.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA35-1872330276-2380_Final_Report_on_MiCA_Guidelines_on_knowledge_and_competence.pdf)

#### c. ESMA Peer Review: Strict Enforcement of CASP Authorisations

- **Summary:** A peer review of Malta's MFSA criticised the authorisation of a CASP while "material issues" were still unresolved. The report signals that ESMA expects all National Competent Authorities (NCAs) to be rigorous gatekeepers.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Authorisation is not a Formality:** Expect scrutiny during the Authorisation Process. Firms must be fully compliant *before* a license is granted.

  - **Key Scrutiny Areas:** Be prepared for in-depth reviews of your governance, management of conflicts of interest, reliance on group entities, ICT and DORA compliance (particularly for custody), and management of Web3/DeFi risks.

  - **No Regulatory Arbitrage:** The actions of a home-state regulator will be closely watched to ensure consistent investor protection across the entire EU.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA42-2004696504-8164_Fast-track_peer_review_on_a_CASP_authorisation_and_supervision_in_Malta.pdf)

#### d. ESMA Guidelines: Preventing and Detecting Market Abuse

- **Summary:** ESMA has published guidelines for a consistent EU-wide framework to combat market abuse in the crypto sector.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Expanded Surveillance Scope:** Market surveillance can no longer be limited to order books. Firms, especially trading platforms, are now expected to monitor for **on-chain manipulation** (e.g., abusive MEV) and, on a "best effort basis," **social media** for the spread of misleading information.

  - **Investment in Technology:** Firms will need to invest in specialised surveillance tools and staff expertise capable of analysing on-chain and off-chain data.

  - **Higher Internal Standards:** Regulators will rigorously audit the internal systems firms use to detect and report suspicious activity (STORs).

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA75-453128700-1039_Guidelines_on_supervisory_practices_to_prevent_and_detect_market_abuse__MiCA_.pdf)

#### e. ESMA Q&A: Prohibition of Shared Order Books

- **Summary:** In a clear and unambiguous clarification, ESMA stated that a MiCA-licensed trading platform **cannot** share its order book with a non-EU, unlicensed entity.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Strict Ring-Fencing of EU Liquidity:** Global exchanges must now operate a distinct, self-contained order book and liquidity pool specifically for their EU-licensed entity. Tapping into a single global order book is forbidden.

  - **Major Operational Overhaul:** This requires significant investment in separate technical, legal, and compliance infrastructure to create a standalone, MiCA-compliant EU trading environment.

  - **Potential for Thinner Markets:** EU traders may face wider spreads and lower liquidity, particularly for less-traded assets, compared to global markets. This prioritises regulatory control and investor protection over market efficiency.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.esma.europa.eu/publications-data/questions-answers/2579)

### 2. Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA)

DORA was the primary focus of regulatory activity in July, with multiple publications clarifying expectations for managing ICT and third-party risk.

#### a. ESAs' Guide: Oversight of Critical ICT Third-Party Providers (CTPPs)

- **Summary:** The European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs) published a guide detailing how they will conduct oversight of designated CTPPs.

- **Key Implications for Firms (CTPPs and Financial Entities):**

  - **For CTPPs:** Expect direct, continuous, and intrusive oversight from a designated Lead Overseer. You must be prepared for information requests, investigations, and on-site inspections. Non-compliance with recommendations can lead to public notices and financial penalties.

  - **For Financial Entities:** While this framework does not directly oversee you, the information gathered will be used by your competent authority to supervise your own ICT risk. If your CTPP has issues, you may be required by your regulator to suspend or terminate the contract as a last resort. This reinforces the need for robust exit strategies.

- [**🔗** Link](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/JC_2025_29__DORA_Guide_on_oversight_activities.pdf)

#### b. EBA Consultation: Broader Management of All Third-Party Risk

- **Summary:** The EBA is updating its guidelines to cover all non-ICT "third-party arrangements," not just traditional outsourcing.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Mandatory Register:** You must create and maintain a detailed register of **all** third-party arrangements (including non-ICT arrangements), assessing the criticality of each.

  - **Contract Renegotiation:** Existing contracts for critical functions will likely need to be renegotiated to include mandatory clauses on audit rights, supervisory access, and subcontracting conditions.

  - **Tested Exit Plans:** A documented and tested exit strategy is now mandatory for every arrangement involving a critical function.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.eba.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/33a0ee15-9601-4c2b-828e-1b09201a6e9f/CP%20on%20Draft%20Guidelines%20on%20sound%20management%20of%20third%20party%20risk.pdf)

#### c. Delegated Regulation: Sub-contracting Critical ICT Services

- **Summary:** This regulation sets technical standards for when an ICT provider subcontracts services that support a financial entity's critical functions.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Supply Chain Responsibility:** Firms are now responsible for ensuring resilience across their entire ICT supply chain. Firm due diligence must extend to your provider's key subcontractors.

  - **Enhanced Contractual Rights:** Contracts must provide you with the right to be notified of, and potentially object to or terminate visibility into, material changes in subcontracting arrangements.

  - **IncVisibility Required:** You must bolster risk management frameworks to gain visibility into and continuously monitor risks throughout the subcontracting chain.

- **🔗** [Link](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202500532)

#### d. ECB Guide: Outsourcing Cloud Services for Banks

- **Summary:** The ECB finalised its guide for banks on outsourcing to Cloud Service Providers (CSPs), clarifying expectations under DORA.

- **Key Implications for Banks:**

  - **Ultimate Responsibility:** Firms remain fully responsible. The guide emphasises that you cannot rely solely on CSP certifications; independent verification and monitoring are also required.

  - **Granular Exit Plans:** Comprehensive, tested, and granular exit strategies for critical cloud services are mandatory *before* going live.

  - **Rigorous Security & Audit:** Firms must ensure robust data encryption, control over data location, and have contractual rights for full access and audit for themselves and their regulators.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/ecb/pub/pdf/ssm.supervisory_guides202507.en.pdf)

#### e. ESMA Final Report: Cloud Outsourcing for Non-DORA Depositaries

- **Summary:** ESMA has narrowed the scope of its existing guidelines for cloud outsourcing. They no longer apply to entities covered by DORA.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **For Most Firms:** The primary compliance requirements for cloud outsourcing for firms are now DORA and its associated technical standards.

  - **For Specific AIF/UCITS Depositaries:** If DORA does not cover your entity, you must continue to adhere to the (now amended) ESMA guidelines on cloud outsourcing.

- [**🔗** Link](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA65-294529287-2639_Final_Report_revised_Guidelines_on_outsourcing_to_cloud_service_providers.pdf)

#### f. ESMA Report: Measuring and Simulating Cyber Risk

- **Summary:** An ESMA report highlights the systemic nature of cyber risk, using a stress simulation of the EU repo market to demonstrate how an incident at one node can lead to a system-wide liquidity shortage.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Conduct Stress Tests:** The report serves as a model for your stress tests. Firms, especially those in critical markets, must conduct their scenario-based stress tests to quantify the potential financial impact of cyber disruptions.

  - **Map Your Interdependencies:** Firms must meticulously map and understand their reliance on third-party providers and critical market infrastructures to identify contagion channels and single points of failure.

  - **DORA Reporting is Key:** The report underscores the importance of DORA's mandatory incident reporting, which will enable regulators to gather the necessary data for systemic risk monitoring. Ensure your reporting systems are ready.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.esma.europa.eu/document/trv-article-operational-and-cyber-risks-eu-financial-markets-measurement-and-stress)

### 3. Financial Crime & Sanctions - EU

#### a. EBA Opinion on Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing Risks

- **Summary:** The European Banking Authority (EBA) published its fifth Opinion on ML/TF risks, highlighting a dynamic and increasingly complex risk landscape. The report identifies escalating risks from the FinTech and crypto-asset sectors, the use of AI to fuel sophisticated fraud, and significant challenges in complying with complex EU sanctions. While noting some improvements in traditional sectors, the EBA found that shortcomings in Customer Due Diligence (CDD) remain a critical and widespread vulnerability.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **FinTech & RegTech Risks:** The rapid growth of FinTech has outpaced robust AML/CFT controls. Firms must ensure that their governance and compliance frameworks align with their innovation strategies. Over-reliance on poorly implemented RegTech solutions is a key failure point.

  - **Crypto-Asset Risks:** The abuse of crypto-assets remains a significant concern. The practical application of the new MiCA and Transfer of Funds Regulation (TFR) frameworks is crucial. CASPs must implement robust AML/CFT controls, particularly regarding customer identification and the "Travel Rule."

  - **Escalating Fraud and Cybercrime:** Firms must invest in advanced technologies and specialised expertise to combat increasingly sophisticated, AI-driven fraud schemes, including deep-fakes used to evade CDD measures.

  - **Sanctions Compliance:** Firms must urgently review and enhance their sanctions screening systems to handle complex sectoral sanctions, in line with new EBA guidelines applicable from the end of 2025.

  - **CDD as a Core Weakness:** Despite new threats, 61% of AML/CFT breaches are still caused by CDD shortcomings. Firms must reinforce these fundamental processes to ensure they are robust and effectively applied.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.eba.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/13ae2f94-dc04-4a50-9f24-af2808e78944/Opinion%20and%20Report%20on%20ML%20TF%20risks.pdf)

### 4. Digital Markets & Innovation

Regulators are also looking ahead to emerging technologies and their impact on market structure and integrity.

#### a. ESMA Report: Implications of Maximal Extractable Value (MEV)

- **Summary:** ESMA published a detailed report on MEV, acknowledging its potential to harm users, undermine market integrity, and threaten blockchain security.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **MEV is on the Regulatory Radar:** Although MiCA does not directly address MEV, this report indicates that predatory forms (e.g., front-running, sandwich attacks) are considered a serious concern for market abuse.

  - **Potential for Future Regulation:** Firms operating in the DeFi space, particularly those involved in searching and block building, should anticipate increased scrutiny. The line between acceptable arbitrage and illicit manipulation is being drawn.

  - **Reporting Obligations:** Certain MEV activities could already fall under MiCA's requirements for reporting suspicious transactions.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-07/ESMA50-481369926-29744_Maximal_Extractable_Value_Implications_for_crypto_markets.pdf)

#### b. ECB: Dual-Track Strategy for DLT Settlement

- **Summary:** The ECB has committed to a plan for settling DLT-based transactions in central bank money, outlining a short-term pilot track ("Pontes") and a long-term vision ("Appia").

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Engage Early:** The ECB is forming market contact groups. Firms that engage now can help shape the future of DLT settlement in Europe.

  - **Prepare for Integration:** The "Pontes" track aims to link DLT platforms with existing TARGET Services. This highlights the critical importance of building interoperable solutions.

  - **New Business Opportunities:** The direct integration of DLT with central bank money will create significant opportunities for new financial products and services, streamlining processes and reducing costs.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2025/html/ecb.pr250701~f4a98dd9dc.en.html)

## United Kingdom

In July 2025, the UK government and regulators launched a significant, coordinated effort to enhance the competitiveness of the UK financial services sector through a series of deregulatory and pro-growth initiatives, collectively known as the "Leeds Reforms."

### a. The "Leeds Reforms": A Strategy for Growth & Competitiveness

- **Summary:** The government unveiled a comprehensive ten-year strategy to make the UK the leading global financial hub by 2035. Articulated in the Chancellor's Mansion House speech and detailed across several policy papers, the reforms focus on boosting retail investment, reducing bureaucracy, freeing up capital, and attracting international business. Key proposals include significantly streamlining the SM&CR authorisations statutory deadlines for authorisations, raising the MREL threshold for challenger banks, and creating a "concierge service" for international firms.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Reduced Regulatory Burden:** Firms can anticipate a lighter regulatory touch, reduced compliance costs, and faster time-to-market. The goal is to double the real growth rate of net financial services exports over the next decade.

  - **New Revenue Opportunities:** A major push to create a "retail investment culture," including allowing banks to provide "Targeted Support" to customers with cash in low-interest accounts, opens up significant growth potential in wealth management.

  - **Enhanced Competitiveness:** Changes to capital requirements (MREL threshold raised to £25-40 billion, competitive application of Basel 3.1) are designed to help challenger banks compete, while the overall strategy aims to make London more attractive than rival financial centres.

  - **Shift to Disclosure-Based Regime:** The reforms signal a move away from pre-emptive rules towards a regime based on transparency and disclosure, giving firms more freedom but also increasing the importance of accurate reporting.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/leeds-reforms-to-rewire-financial-system-boost-investment-and-create-skilled-jobs-across-uk)

### b. Conduct & Accountability Reforms

- **Summary:** The government is consulting on a radical overhaul of the Senior Managers & Certification Regime (SM&CR), aiming to reduce its burden by 50%. The headline proposal is to remove the Certification Regime from primary legislation, allowing for a more flexible, regulator-led approach, and to significantly reduce the number of senior manager roles requiring finalisation. Simultaneously, the FCA is finalising rules to extend its framework for tackling serious Non-Financial Misconduct (NFM), such as bullying and harassment, to all regulated firms, clarifying its relevance to an individual's fitness and propriety.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **SM&CR Relief:** Firms can expect a substantial reduction in the administrative costs and operational friction associated with the SM&CR, particularly the annual certification process for ~262,000 functions and delays in senior appointments.

  - **Heightened Focus on NFM:** Despite deregulation elsewhere, there appears to be a crackdown on poor workplace culture. The new rules, effective September 2026, require firms to review and strengthen their internal policies, training, and disciplinary procedures to prevent and address NFM, which now carries significant regulatory and reputational risk.

  - **Balancing Act:** Firms must navigate a dual reality: a more streamlined accountability regime for business operations, but a stricter, more explicit set of expectations for personal conduct and workplace culture.

- **🔗** [Link](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687635c0352c290d20dcae75/Reforming_the_Senior_Managers__Certification_Regime_Consultation_2025.pdf)

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/consultation/cp25-18.pdf)

### c. Digital Transformation of Markets

- UK is taking concrete steps to digitise its market infrastructure. The Digitisation Taskforce has recommended the mandatory elimination of paper share certificates by the end of 2027. This is part of a broader strategy that includes a commitment to move to T+1 securities settlement by October 2027 and the pilot issuance of a Digital Gilt Instrument (DIGIT) using DLT. The Bank of England is actively supporting this shift with an upgraded RTGS system and a detokenized framework for stablecoins and tokenised deposits.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Mandatory Digitalisation, moving** away from paper (dematerialisation) and towards faster settlements, is not optional. Firms must make significant investments in their digital infrastructure, back-office operations, and data management systems to comply with these regulations.

  - **Embrace DLT and Tokenisation:** The DIGIT pilot and Digital Securities Sandbox are strong signals for firms to tokenise and invest actively in DLT and asset tokenisation. This presents opportunities for new products, streamlined post-trade processes (like intraday repos), and significant cost reductions.

  - **Intermediation Models Must Evolve:** The digitisation plan includes a "baseline service" that intermediaries must provide to beneficial owners, extending shareholder rights and requiring transparent fees. This will necessitate changes to service offerings and pricing models.

  - **Interoperability is Key:** As the Bank of England has stressed, a "mixed ecosystem" of old and new systems will persist. Firms must develop solutions that can bridge traditional and DLT-based infrastructures to prevent the creation of fragmented liquidity pools.

### d. Financial Crime

- **Summary:** The 2025 National Risk Assessment reaffirms that the UK's Money Laundering (ML) and Terrorist Financing (TF) risk remains high, with fraud (over 4.1 million incidents) and sanctions evasion being top concerns. The ML risk for the cryptoasset sector has been upgraded to "high." A specific OFSI threat assessment for cryptoassets highlights significant under-reporting of suspected sanctions breaches. It warns of exposure to designated Russian entities (like Garantex) and sophisticated North Korean cyber threats.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Heightened Scrutiny for Crypto Firms:** Cryptoasset firms must urgently enhance their compliance frameworks to meet the increased scrutiny. OFSI expects robust due diligence, including on-chain transaction monitoring (scanning a minimum of **3-5 hops**), and prompt, detailed reporting of any suspected breaches.

  - **Vigilance Against Evasion Tactics:** All firms must be vigilant for red flags associated with sanctions evasion, such as the use of anonymity-enhancing technologies (mixers, privacy coins), rapid asset movement through new wallets, and counterparties operating in sanctioned jurisdictions.

  - **Proactive Reporting:** The message from authorities is clear: retrospective discovery of a breach is not an excuse for non-reporting. Firms must report any suspected breaches, even historical ones, to OFSI, the FCA, and the NCA. Freezing assets linked to alleged sanctions evasion is a mandatory obligation.

## Australia

In July 2025, Australian regulators and industry bodies demonstrated a strong, collaborative push towards understanding and enabling the future of digital financial markets.

### a. Project Acacia: Exploring Tokenised Asset Markets

- **Summary:** Project Acacia, a joint initiative by the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) and the Digital Finance Cooperative Research Centre (DFCRC), has selected 24 industry participants to pilot use cases for tokenised assets. The project, supported by ASIC and the Treasury, will test transactions across various asset classes (fixed income, carbon credits, etc.) using different forms of digital money, including stablecoins, bank deposit tokens, and a pilot wholesale Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC). To facilitate this, ASIC is providing regulatory relief for the responsible testing of these new technologies.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Opportunity for Innovation:** For the selected financial institutions and fintechs, this project offers a unique opportunity to gain hands-on experience with tokenised assets and influence the development of market infrastructure and regulatory frameworks.

  - **Regulatory Collaboration:** The project showcases a proactive and collaborative approach from Australian regulators (RBA, ASIC), who are using controlled, real-world pilots to inform future policy rather than regulating in a vacuum.

  - **Future of Money and Payments:** The testing of multiple forms of digital settlement assets (CBDC, stablecoins, tokenised deposits) will provide crucial insights into the optimal models for a tokenised economy, potentially shaping the evolution of Australia's payment systems.

  - **Platform Diversity:** The use of various DLT platforms (including Hedera, R3 Corda, and EVM-compatible networks) highlights the importance of technological diversity and the need for future interoperability.

  - **Economic Potential:** The research aims to unlock significant financial gains, with estimates suggesting up to AU\$19 billion per year from improved efficiency in markets and cross-border payments, positioning Australia as a leader in digital finance innovation.

## Hong Kong

In July 2025, Hong Kong's authorities launched a coordinated and comprehensive strategy to cement the city's status as a premier global hub for digital assets, moving decisively from cautious regulation to proactive ecosystem development.

### a. "LEAP" Framework: A Proactive Strategy for Digital Asset Growth

- **Summary:** The government's "Policy Statement 2.0" introduced the "LEAP" framework (Legal streamlining, Expanding products, Advancing use cases, People and partnerships) as the core of its new strategy. This represents a significant pivot from regulating "Virtual Assets" to actively developing a broader "Digital Assets" ecosystem.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Tokenisation is the Next Frontier:** The government is placing a major strategic bet on the tokenisation of Real-World Assets (RWAs). The commitment to regularise the issuance of tokenised government bonds, clarify that the stamp duty waiver for ETFs also applies to tokenised ETFs, and expand tax concessions for funds to include digital assets sends a clear signal for firms to innovate in this space.

  - **Stablecoins as Core Financial Utility:** The explicit support for stablecoin payment use cases, including for potential government services, is a powerful endorsement. This positions Hong Kong as a future hub for issuing and utilising regulated, fiat-backed stablecoins, which could revolutionise areas like cross-border trade finance.

  - **Government-Backed Ecosystem:** The creation of funding schemes for blockchain projects via Cyberport and the encouragement of local Regtech solutions demonstrate a commitment to building a deep and sustainable local ecosystem.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.fstb.gov.hk/fsb/en/publication/consult/doc/VACUSTODY_consultation_paper_en.pdf)

### b. Regulating the Full Lifecycle: Custodians and Dealers

- **Summary:** The FSTB and SFC launched a consultation to create a mandatory licensing regime for all entities providing Virtual Asset (VA) dealing and custodian services. This closes a significant regulatory gap and aligns with the "same activity, same risks, same regulation" principle. The SFC will be the lead regulator, with the HKMA supervising banks' activities in this area.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Mandatory Licensing and Increased Costs:** All entities providing VA custody or dealing, including subsidiaries of licensed exchanges and fund managers offering self-custody, will need to be licensed. This will require significant investment in compliance, technology, and personnel to meet stringent requirements, including a minimum capital of HK\$10 million.

  - **No Grandfathering:** The proposal includes **no deeming arrangement** or grace period. All existing providers must apply for and secure a license before the regime commences to avoid business interruption, creating a sense of urgency for the entire industry.

  - **Market Consolidation and Professionalisation:** The high bar for licensing will likely drive market consolidation, forcing smaller players to exit or merge, thereby increasing the overall security and professionalism of the sector and boosting institutional confidence.

- **🔗** [Link](https://gia.info.gov.hk/general/202506/26/P2025062600269_500089_1_1750909574183.pdf)

### c. Stablecoin Issuers

- **Summary:** Following the passage of the Stablecoins Ordinance, the HKMA clarified its highly selective approach to licensing issuers, with the regime set to commence on August 1, 2025. The HKMA firmly defines stablecoins as payment tools, not speculative investments, and will grant "only a handful of licences initially" to applicants who meet an exceptionally high bar.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Quality Over Quantity:** Hong Kong is not seeking a large number of issuers but a small group of highly credible, well-capitalised, and compliant players. This favours large financial institutions and globally recognised fintechs.

  - **Business Viability is Crucial:** Applicants must demonstrate not only robust risk management and security but also a sustainable business plan that addresses real economic "pain points." A compelling use case is as important as regulatory compliance.

  - **Major Hurdle for Cross-Border Operations:** The requirement for issuers to ensure compliance in all jurisdictions where their stablecoin is used presents a significant legal and operational challenge, potentially limiting the initial geographic reach of new issuers.

- **🔗** [Link](https://www.hkma.gov.hk/eng/news-and-media/insight/2025/06/20250623/)

### d. Banks and Cryptoassets: From Policy to Practice

- **Summary:** The HKMA began consulting with the banking sector on detailed regulatory reporting templates for cryptoasset exposures. This operationalises the Basel Committee's global standard and formalises the role of banks in the digital asset market.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Crypto is Now a Formal Banking Asset Class:** By embedding cryptoasset reporting into core prudential returns for capital adequacy, liquidity, and market risk, the HKMA is officially recognising crypto as a legitimate, albeit risky, asset class that banks can hold and must manage.

  - **System Upgrades are Mandatory:** Banks must now make tangible investments in their IT and data infrastructure to identify, classify, measure, and report their cryptoasset exposures by the new, detailed templates.

  - **Quantifiable Capital Impact:** The new reporting will make the "cost of capital" for holding different types of crypto explicit, directly influencing banks' business decisions and risk appetite regarding which digital assets to engage with.

## United States

In July 2025, the U.S. saw a significant push to establish a comprehensive federal framework for digital assets, with several landmark legislative proposals and regulatory guidance documents being released.

### a. The GENIUS Act: A Federal Framework for Stablecoins

- **Summary:** The "Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins of 2025" (GENIUS Act) proposes a strict federal regulatory framework for payment stablecoins. It limits issuance to subsidiaries of insured banks, federally chartered non-bank issuers, and state-licensed issuers (under a \$10 billion cap). The Act mandates 1:1 reserves in high-quality liquid assets (cash, short-term Treasuries), prohibits rehypothecation, and imposes personal criminal liability on executives for the accuracy of their attestations.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Creates a "Regulated In-Group":** Provides a narrow but clear path to legitimacy for compliant issuers but poses an existential threat to algorithmic or un-backed stablecoins.

  - **Shifts Business Model:** Strict reserve rules eliminate yield generation on reserves, forcing issuers to become ultra-conservative financial utilities focused on transaction fees or other services, not asset management.

  - **Resolves Legal Ambiguity:** Crucially, the bill provides a "safe harbour" by explicitly excluding permitted payment stablecoins from the definition of a "security," removing a significant legal risk and barrier to institutional adoption.

  - **Enhances Consumer Trust, Raises Cost of Capital:** While giving stablecoin holders priority in an insolvency boosts user confidence, it subordinates other creditors, likely increasing the issuer's cost of debt financing.

- 🔗 [Link](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/s394/BILLS-119s394is.pdf)

#### b. The CLARITY Act: Delineating SEC and CFTC Jurisdiction

- **Summary:** The "Digital Asset Market Clarity Act of 2025" aims to resolve the jurisdictional ambiguity between the SEC and CFTC. It introduces the legal concept of a "digital commodity" and assigns the CFTC as its primary regulator. It also establishes a process for a blockchain to be certified as "mature" and decentralised, at which point its related assets would be treated as commodities.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **A Path to Commodity Status:** The Act establishes a formal process for digital assets that become sufficiently decentralised, allowing them to transition from being regulated as securities (under the SEC) to commodities (under the CFTC).

  - **Regulatory Clarity for Intermediaries:** The bill establishes a registration regime with the CFTC for "digital commodity exchanges," "brokers," and "dealers," providing a more straightforward path to compliance for these market participants.

  - **Reduced Risk for Secondary Trading:** By clarifying that secondary market transactions of digital commodities are generally not securities transactions, the Act would facilitate more liquid and efficient trading on regulated platforms.

- 🔗 [Link](https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr3633/BILLS-119hr3633rh.pdf)

#### c. The CBDC Anti-Surveillance State Act: Prohibiting a Digital Dollar

- **Summary:** This bill is designed to prevent the Federal Reserve from issuing a central bank digital currency (CBDC) without explicit prior authorisation from Congress. It prohibits the Fed from developing a direct-to-consumer retail CBDC or an indirect wholesale CBDC. It also explicitly protects private, "open, permissionless" digital currencies.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Stimulus for Private Innovation:** By taking a government-led CBDC off the table, the bill creates a significant market opportunity for private sector stablecoin issuers and payment innovators to fill the void.

  - **Major Victory for Commercial Banks:** The prohibition of a direct-to-consumer CBDC removes what many in the banking sector saw as an existential threat of deposit disintermediation.

  - **Regulatory De-Risking for Crypto:** The explicit legal protection for existing private cryptocurrencies provides a powerful safe harbour and distinguishes them from a potential government-controlled digital currency.

  - **Divergence from Global Trend:** The U.S. would be formally ceding ground in the global CBDC race, which could have long-term implications for the U.S. dollar's international role if other nations develop more efficient cross-border payment systems.

- 🔗 [Link](https://www.congress.gov/118/bills/hr5403/BILLS-118hr5403rfs.pdf)

#### d. SEC Guidance on Crypto Asset ETPs

- **Summary:** The SEC's Division of Corporation Finance issued detailed guidance on disclosure requirements for crypto asset Exchange-Traded Products (ETPs). The guidance emphasises clear and concise disclosures, covering a wide range of topics.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Increased Scrutiny and Standardisation:** ETP issuers face a higher bar for disclosure. They must provide granular detail on the underlying crypto asset and its network (e.g., consensus mechanism, total supply, forks), the trust's service providers, and custody arrangements (e.g., use of cold/hot storage, insurance coverage).

  - **Detailed Risk Factors:** Issuers must detail material risks specific to crypto, including price volatility, hacking, fraud, market manipulation, and concentration of ownership, rather than using generic risk warnings.

  - **Focus on Governance and Conflicts:** The guidance requires clear disclosure of the sponsor's governance structure, the roles of key individuals, and any potential conflicts of interest, such as whether the sponsor or insiders hold the underlying crypto asset.

- 🔗 [Link](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/cf-crypto-asset-exchange-traded-products-070125)

#### e. Joint Agency Statement on Crypto-Asset Safekeeping

- **Summary:** The Federal Reserve, FDIC, and OCC issued a joint statement clarifying that existing banking regulations and risk-management principles apply to crypto-asset safekeeping services. It does not create new rules, but instead reinforces existing expectations.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Robust Risk Management is Paramount:** Banks must conduct comprehensive due diligence on any crypto-asset they intend to safeguard and have a strong framework for managing cryptographic keys to prevent loss or theft.

  - **Detailed Customer Agreements:** Agreements must be well-written to address crypto-specific issues, such as forks, airdrops, and on-chain governance, ensuring customers are fully informed of the associated risks.

  - **Third-Party Accountability:** If a bank uses a sub-custodian, it remains ultimately responsible for the safekeeping of the assets. This necessitates rigorous due diligence on any third-party providers.

  - **Compliance Integration:** BSA/AML, CFT, and OFAC compliance functions must be deeply integrated into crypto-safekeeping operations to address the unique challenges of DLT.

- 🔗 [Link](https://www.fdic.gov/interagency-statement-crypto-asset-safekeeping.pdf)

## International

In July 2025, international bodies continued to address the global implications of financial innovation and crime, focusing on establishing common principles and highlighting emerging risks.

### a. FATF: Comprehensive Update on Terrorist Financing Risks

- **Summary:** The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) released a comprehensive update on terrorist financing (TF), highlighting that despite global efforts, significant deficiencies remain, with 69% of assessed jurisdictions lacking in effective investigation and prosecution. The report details how factors like territorial control, conflict zones, and weak governance exacerbate TF risks. It also outlines the diverse funding methods used by various terrorist actors, from global networks like ISIL to lone actors.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Heightened Awareness of Funding Channels:** Firms must be aware of the diverse and blended methods used to finance terrorism, including the abuse of cash, Money Value Transfer Services (MVTS), e-money, non-profit organisations (NPOs), and the increasing use of Virtual Assets (VAs). ISIL-K, for example, systematically uses VAs for transfers and donations.

  - **Strengthened Due Diligence:** The report underscores the need for robust implementation of FATF standards, particularly for VASPs (including the Travel Rule) and MVTS. Financial institutions must enhance their transaction monitoring to detect patterns associated with TF, such as small, repeated transfers or funds flowing to/from conflict zones.

  - **Collaboration is Key:** The FATF calls for expanded outreach to uncovered sectors (e.g., social media, gaming platforms) and stronger public-private partnerships. Financial firms should actively participate in these initiatives to share intelligence and develop more effective countermeasures.

- 🔗 [Link](https://www.fatf-gafi.org/en/publications/Methodsandtrends/comprehensive-update-terrorist-financing-risks-2025.html)

### b. BIS & FSB: Financial Stability in the Age of AI and Digital Assets

- **Summary:** The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) and the Financial Stability Board (FSB) released several reports focusing on the systemic implications of new technologies. The FSB highlighted the financial stability risks associated with Artificial Intelligence, specifically citing third-party dependencies, the potential for market correlations from similar models, and new cyber vulnerabilities. The BIS examined the growth of stablecoins (now a market of approximately \$255 billion), noting risks to monetary sovereignty and financial stability from potential fire sales of reserve assets. Another BIS bulletin explored the potential of tokenised government bonds to improve market liquidity and efficiency.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Proactive AI Risk Management:** Firms adopting AI must move beyond simple implementation to developing sophisticated governance frameworks. This includes managing model risk (especially for opaque "black box" models), ensuring data quality, mitigating third-party concentration risk (e.g., over-reliance on a few AI providers), and enhancing cybersecurity to defend against AI-powered attacks.

  - **Understanding the Systemic Impact of Stablecoins:** Firms involved with stablecoins must be aware of their potential impact on short-term funding markets. Regulators will likely require stronger liquidity risk management for issuers, and the growth of tokenised MMFs will intensify competition.

  - **Prepare for Tokenised Sovereign Debt:** The finding that tokenised government bonds can offer greater liquidity and accessibility signals a potential paradigm shift. Financial institutions should prepare for the operational and technological changes required to issue, trade, and use these instruments as collateral in a tokenised financial system.

### c. UNCITRAL & The Wolfsberg Group: Legal Frameworks and Risk-Based Approaches

- **Summary:** The United Nations Commission on International Trade Law (UNCITRAL) guided on the legal issues of using DLT in trade, emphasising challenges related to data privacy (e.g., the "right to be forgotten" on an immutable ledger), smart contract liability, and cross-border data flows. Separately, the Wolfsberg Group reiterated the importance of a sophisticated Risk-Based Approach (RBA) for financial crime risk management, stressing proportionality, prioritisation, and effectiveness over a rigid, "tick-box" compliance model.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Legal Due Diligence for DLT is Crucial:** Firms adopting DLT must conduct thorough legal due diligence on providers and systems. They must adapt internal policies to address the unique challenges of DLT, such as data immutability, and carefully structure contracts to manage liability for brilliant contract execution and data errors.

  - **Adopt an "Intelligent" RBA:** The Wolfsberg Group's statement encourages firms to move beyond one-size-fits-all compliance. This means tailoring financial crime controls to the firm's specific risk profile, focusing resources on higher-risk areas, and demonstrating the *effectiveness* of those controls, not just their existence.

  - **Navigating Regulatory Fragmentation:** The International Journal of Blockchain Law highlighted the ongoing challenge of regulatory fragmentation, such as the overlap between MiCA and PSD2 in the EU. Firms operating globally must invest in agile legal and compliance functions that can navigate these complex and sometimes conflicting jurisdictional rules.
