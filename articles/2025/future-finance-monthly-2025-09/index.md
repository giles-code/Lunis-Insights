## Executive Summary

September’s global regulatory activities in digital finance signalled a definitive transition from abstract policy formulation to the codification of detailed implementation and supervisory mechanics. The prevailing trend was the operationalisation of foundational legislative frameworks, exemplified by the European Union’s finalisation of key Regulatory and Implementing Technical Standards (RTS/ITS) for its Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) regulation.

This progression toward practical application, however, concurrently revealed nascent supervisory fragmentation, as a bloc of influential EU member states formally contested MiCA’s decentralised oversight model, presaging future challenges to regulatory convergence. Concurrently, the United Kingdom and Australia advanced their respective strategies of “regulatory assimilation,” focusing on integrating crypto-asset activities within the ambit of existing financial services law rather than establishing a sui generis regime.

In the United States, a coordinated pronouncement from the SEC and CFTC provided a procedural pathway for spot crypto-asset products on regulated exchanges. However, this move was met with internal dissent regarding the pace of market integration.

Across all jurisdictions, international standard-setting bodies, including the FATF and the Wolfsberg Group, have issued critical guidance on financial crime risk mitigation. Meanwhile, the BIS and IMF have published analyses on emerging challenges in artificial intelligence, tokenisation, and systemic risk. This report provides a technical analysis of these developments and their strategic implications for market participants.

## Europe

The European Union’s Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) regulation advanced from legislative principle to operational reality in September with the adoption of critical Level 2 measures for Asset-Referenced Token (ART) issuers. These technical standards provide the granular detail necessary for market authorisation. However, this progress was immediately juxtaposed with a significant political challenge from the financial authorities of France, Austria, and Italy, who questioned the framework’s decentralised supervisory architecture, indicating a potentially fragmented implementation landscape. In parallel, the European Central Bank continued to develop the strategic rationale for a digital euro, positioning it as critical public infrastructure for systemic resilience and innovation.

The European Commission advanced the operationalisation of MiCA by adopting two pivotal Level 2 measures. These regulations establish the technical bridge between the high-level principles articulated in the MiCA text and the specific, granular information firms must submit to their National Competent Authorities (NCAs) for authorisation, thereby providing the final components of the compliance framework for prospective ART issuers.

### MiCA: Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/1125 of 5 June 2025 supplements MiCA with RTS detailing information needed in applications to offer ARTs publicly or seek trading admission

This regulation specifies the substantive information required for a complete application. A set of Regulatory Technical Standards (RTS) that is part of this Delegated Regulation specifies the needed substantive information within an application for authorisation to offer ARTs to the public or to seek their admission to trading. Published in the Official Journal of the European Union, the text codifies supervisory expectations for a comprehensive and transparent submission, enabling NCAs to perform a thorough assessment of an applicant’s operational viability, governance structures, and risk management capabilities. The core components of the authorisation dossier now formally include:

- **Programme of Operations:** A detailed programme of operations is required to provide regulators with a holistic view of the firm’s business model, strategic objectives, and planned activities, extending beyond a standard business plan to cover the ART’s entire operational lifecycle.

- **Reserve Asset Policies:** The stability of an ART is contingent upon its reserve. The regulation therefore mandates the submission of detailed policies governing the composition, constitution, segregation, custody, and investment management of these reserve assets. This is crucial for supervisors to validate the robustness of the stabilisation mechanism and ensure the perpetual enforceability of token holders’ redemption rights, particularly in stress scenarios.

- **Governance and Suitability of Management:** The regulation requires sufficient information to permit NCAs to assess the reputation and suitability of the members of the management body and any direct or indirect qualifying shareholders. This includes verification that these individuals have no convictions for offences related to money laundering, terrorist financing, or other crimes that would compromise their good repute. Any prior assessments by different competent authorities must be disclosed to ensure supervisory coherence.

- **Financial Crime Risk Assessment:** Applicants must submit a comprehensive risk assessment detailing the business model’s exposure and sensitivity to money laundering and terrorist financing risks. This assessment must encompass the mechanisms for issuance, redemption, and distribution, including the roles of any third-party Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs).

- **Internal Control Framework:** The application must delineate the firm’s internal control framework, including risk management protocols, Information and Communication Technology (ICT) systems, and security architecture, all of which are deemed essential for the sound and prudent management of the issuer’s operations.

### MiCA: Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/1126 of 5 June 2025 sets technical standards for MiCA, including standard forms, templates, and procedures for applications to offer ARTs publicly and seek trading admission (C/2025/3219)

This Implementing Regulation, a set of Implementing Technical Standards (ITS), complements the substantive requirements by establishing a standardised procedural framework for application submissions across all 27 member states. The objective is to create a uniform and efficient application process, thereby mitigating the risk of procedural divergence among NCAs. Key procedural elements include:

- **Standard Forms and Templates:** Applicant issuers are required to use the standard form in Annexe I and the template in Annexe II for their submissions. NCAs must make these documents available on their websites to ensure a single, harmonised application format.

- **Submission and Communication Protocols:** NCAs are mandated to designate and publish a specific contact point for receiving applications. They must also clearly specify the modalities for submission, which may include an online portal, other electronic means, or, where required by national law, original paper form for specific documents.

- **Assessment of Completeness:** The regulation defines a straightforward process for NCAs. Upon receipt, an acknowledgement must be sent to the applicant. The NCA will then assess the application for completeness. If information is deficient, the NCA must notify the applicant, specifying the missing elements and setting a submission deadline. An application is only deemed complete once all requisite information is received in the prescribed format.

Both the Delegated and Implementing Regulations will enter into force on the 20th day following their publication in the Official Journal and will apply from October 5, 2025. This establishes a firm deadline for industry compliance. The level of detail mandated by these technical standards underscores the significant operational, governance, and compliance readiness that market entrants are expected to demonstrate.

### MiCA: European Crypto-Asset Markets’ Framework: Proposals from the French, The Austrian and the Italian Financial Markets Authorities

Contemporaneous with the finalisation of MiCA’s technical framework, a significant political challenge to its core supervisory principles emerged. The financial market authorities of France (Autorité des Marchés Financiers - AMF), Austria (Finanzmarktaufsichtsbehörde - FMA), and Italy (Commissione Nazionale per le Società e la Borsa - CONSOB) issued a joint proposal advocating for targeted amendments to the MiCA framework. The proposal, informed by initial experiences with MiCA’s application, contends that the current architecture is inadequate for ensuring consistent supervision and investor protection across the Union.

The primary concern is the potential for supervisory arbitrage and a “race to the bottom”. The authorities posit that CASPs may engage in “regulatory shopping,” seeking authorisation in member states with perceived lenient or under-resourced supervisory regimes. Once licensed, these firms can utilise the EU “passport” to operate across the single market, potentially exposing investors in more stringently regulated jurisdictions to risks originating from less stringent supervisory environments. This concern is amplified by a recent European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) peer review, which identified deficiencies in the crypto authorisation process of the Malta Financial Services Authority.

To mitigate these perceived weaknesses, the authorities proposed four amendments:

1. **Direct ESMA Supervision for Major CASPs:** The proposal’s central tenet is to grant ESMA direct supervisory authority over the largest, systemically essential CASPs. This would mirror the European Banking Authority’s (EBA) direct oversight of significant stablecoins under MiCA, intended to ensure uniform rule application and prevent opportunistic jurisdictional selection.

2. **Enhanced Cybersecurity Requirements:** Citing the elevated cyber risk profile of the sector, the proposal calls for mandatory, independent cybersecurity audits to be conducted *prior* to a CASP receiving MiCA authorisation, with periodic renewals. These audits would cover critical domains, such as asset protection (including custody), resilience to cyberattacks, and incident management protocols.

3. **Closure of the Non-EU Platform Loophole:** The authorities have identified a regulatory gap that allows European clients to access non-MiCA-compliant platforms through regulated CASP intermediaries. They propose a rule mandating that any intermediary executing crypto-asset orders must do so on a platform that is fully compliant with MiCA or a demonstrably equivalent regulatory regime.

4. **Centralised White Paper Scrutiny:** To enhance legal certainty and create efficiencies, the proposal suggests clarifying the scrutiny process for crypto-asset white papers and establishing a single, EU-wide access point for filing and managing token offerings (excluding stablecoins), reflecting the pan-European nature of most offerings.

The strategic implications are profound, representing the first significant challenge to MiCA’s decentralised supervisory model. The AMF’s president has indicated that France would not preclude challenging or refusing the passport of a firm licensed by another member state if it harbours serious concerns about the quality of that supervision. Such an action would directly threaten the integrity of the single market for crypto-assets.

### CBDC: ECB - The digital euro: ensuring resilience and inclusion in digital payments

As MiCA entered its implementation phase, the European Central Bank (ECB) continued its work on a potential central bank digital currency (CBDC), known as the digital euro. September saw the release of communications that refined the project’s strategic narrative and demonstrated its innovative potential, positioning the digital euro as public infrastructure designed to enhance the Eurozone’s resilience, inclusion, and innovation capacity.

In a speech to the European Parliament, ECB Executive Board member Piero Cipollone framed the digital euro as a “public good” essential for Europe’s future. The arguments centred on strategic imperatives:

- **Resilience and Strategic Autonomy:** The speech underscored the Eurozone’s dependence on non-EU providers for its digital payment infrastructure. Amid heightened geopolitical and operational risks, this dependence creates a systemic vulnerability. A digital euro, as a public payment rail, would provide critical redundancy. Resilience-enhancing features would include a distributed technical infrastructure, a dedicated ECB-run fallback application, and an **offline functionality** for peer-to-peer payments without connectivity.

- **Inclusion:** As physical cash usage declines, a digital euro is designed to guarantee universal access to a free, universally accepted digital means of payment. The ECB is collaborating with associations for vulnerable groups to incorporate accessibility features, such as voice commands and large-font displays.

Complementing this vision, the ECB published the outcome report from its Digital Euro Innovation Platform, which involved collaboration with nearly 70 market participants in “visionaries” (conceptual) and “pioneers” (technical) workstreams. The report demonstrated the digital euro’s potential to serve as a foundational layer for private-sector innovation. Several use cases were successfully tested:

- **Conditional Payments:** Identified as a key innovation driver, this functionality would allow payment service providers (PSPs) to build services where payment is automatically triggered upon fulfilment of predefined conditions, enabled by a core “reservation of funds functionality”.

- **Integrated Electronic Receipts (e-receipts):** The ecosystem could support strongly encrypted e-receipts, providing consumers with a structured digital record of purchases while reducing paper waste.

- **Financial Inclusion and Literacy:** The platform explored the development of tailored digital euro wallets for specific demographics, such as children and students, to promote financial literacy and streamline access to benefits.

Both workstreams concluded that the success of these innovations depends on the digital euro providing a shared, pan-European infrastructure with harmonised standards, which would reduce market fragmentation and unlock new business opportunities. A second round of experimentation is planned for 2026.

### CBDC: ECB - Digital euro innovation platform Outcome report: pioneers and visionaries workstreams

The ECB has strategically shifted its communication on the digital euro, emphasising its geopolitical and societal importance to accelerate the legislative process.

**A. A Strategic Initiative for Resilience and Autonomy**

In a formal address to the European Parliament, ECB Executive Board member Piero Cipollone articulated the case for the digital euro based on two core principles:

- **Resilience and Strategic Autonomy:** The EU’s digital payments ecosystem exhibits a critical dependency on non-EU commercial infrastructures, creating a strategic vulnerability. The digital euro is positioned as a public payment infrastructure—a resilient fallback mechanism—designed to guarantee payment continuity during systemic outages, cyber events, or geopolitical stress, supported by features like **offline functionality** and a dedicated ECB-provided application.

- **Financial Inclusion:** In an increasingly digitalised economy, the digital euro is designed to function as a public good, ensuring universal access to free, basic digital payments for all citizens, including vulnerable populations with limited digital literacy or disabilities.

**Key Implications for the Financial Sector:**

- **Mandated Interoperability Protocols:** The ECB is advocating for a legislative mandate requiring all private Payment Service Providers (PSPs) to ensure full technical interoperability with the public digital euro infrastructure, including the ECB’s fallback app.

- **Establishment of a Public Utility Baseline:** The provision of a free, secure, and universally accessible public payment rail will establish a new market baseline, exerting competitive pressure on the fee structures and value propositions of private-sector payment solutions.

- **Enhancing Economic Sovereignty:** The project is an explicit policy initiative aimed at bolstering the EEU’s economic sovereignty by developing a pan-European payment system under public control, thereby reducing its reliance on external networks.

**B. Timeline Contingent on Legislative Action**

Significantly, the ECB has made the project’s formal timeline contingent upon the finalisation of the legal framework by the European co-legislators (Parliament and Council). This strategic positioning places the onus on political actors to provide the necessary legal mandate for the ECB to proceed with implementation. Inaction is implicitly framed as a risk to the Union’s long-term economic security and autonomy.

## Switzerland

### Digital Assets: The Deposit Token Proof of Concept

Switzerland demonstrated its pragmatic and legally grounded approach to digital assets with the successful completion of a Proof of Concept (PoC) for a "Deposit Token." Conducted under the Swiss Bankers Association (SBA) and involving institutions like UBS and Sygnum, the PoC achieved the first legally binding interbank payment using tokenized bank deposits on a public blockchain (Ethereum).

The innovation was primarily legal, not technological. The participating banks structured the Deposit Token not as a new crypto-asset but as a "digital representation of a payment instruction" under Switzerland's established Code of Obligations. This legal "wrapper" had significant implications:

- **The Blockchain as a Messaging Layer:** The blockchain functioned as a secure and immutable messaging layer, where an on-chain token transfer represented an irrevocable instruction to trigger a conventional fiat money transfer via the existing Swiss Interbank Clearing system.

- **Programmability with Legal Certainty:** The use of smart contracts on Ethereum enabled advanced functionalities, such as the atomic settlement of Deposit Tokens against a tokenized asset, eliminating counterparty risk.

- **Immediate Enforceability:** By grounding the process in existing commercial law, all transactions were legally binding from day one without requiring new legislation.

This approach represents a "third way" in digital asset regulation, avoiding the complexity of a bespoke framework like MiCA or the friction of assimilation models. By prioritizing legal certainty and creatively applying established law, the Swiss model provides a blueprint for integrating traditional finance with blockchain technology for high-value institutional use cases.

## United Kingdom

While the EU advanced its bespoke crypto-asset framework, other major jurisdictions refined their distinct regulatory approaches in September. The United Kingdom and Australia pursued a strategy of "regulatory assimilation," integrating digital assets into existing financial services legal structures. In contrast, Switzerland demonstrated a pragmatic model, leveraging established commercial law to facilitate on-chain innovation without new financial regulation. These divergent strategies reflect different philosophical and practical considerations in bringing the digital asset economy within the regulatory perimeter.

The United Kingdom's regulatory philosophy for digital assets crystallized in September around the principle of assimilation: applying existing, well-established financial rules to crypto-assets that perform analogous functions. This strategy was evident in initiatives from the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), HM Treasury, and the Bank of England (BoE), all aimed at constructing an innovative "multi-moneyverse" upon the foundations of traditional financial law.

### Digital Markets: FCA - Consultation Paper CP25/25\\\Application of FCA Handbook for Regulated Cryptoasset Activities

The FCA's Consultation Paper 25/25 (CP25/25) detailed how the existing FCA Handbook will apply to cryptoasset activities once they are brought within the regulatory perimeter. Guided by the principle of "same risk, same regulatory outcome," the paper proposes extending core elements of the UK's financial regulatory regime to crypto firms:

- **Governance and Accountability:** The Senior Managers & Certification Regime (SM&CR) will be fully applied, imposing clear lines of personal responsibility on senior individuals for key functions within cryptoasset firms.

- **Operational Resilience:** All authorized crypto firms must comply with the FCA's operational resilience framework (SYSC 15A), requiring them to identify important business services, set impact tolerances, and conduct rigorous scenario testing.

- **Consumer Protection:** The Consumer Duty, requiring firms to deliver good outcomes for retail customers, will apply to regulated cryptoasset activities. The FCA is also considering providing consumers access to the Financial Ombudsman Service (FOS) for unresolved complaints.

HM Treasury published a draft Statutory Instrument (SI) proposing amendments to the Money Laundering, Terrorist Financing and Transfer of Funds Regulations 2017 (MLRs). The changes aim to enhance the risk-based nature of the UK's anti-money laundering (AML) regime:

- **Risk-Based Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD):** The mandatory trigger for applying EDD will be narrowed to only countries on the Financial Action Task Force's (FATF) "call for action” list, allowing for a more proportionate approach to countries on the “increased monitoring” list.

- **Alignment with FSMA:** The registration and change-of-control requirements for cryptoasset firms under the MLRs will be aligned with the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (FSMA) regime, shifting the “fit and proper” test from “beneficial owners” to FSMA-defined “controllers”.

### Digital Markets: Building trust and supporting innovation in the multi-moneyverse − speech by Sarah Breeden

Bank of England Deputy Governor Sarah Breeden outlined a strategic vision for a “multi-moneyverse,” a mixed ecosystem where traditional bank deposits, tokenised deposits, regulated stablecoins, and a potential digital pound CBDC are fully interoperable. Breeden articulated three pillars to achieve this vision: modern infrastructure, robust regulation, and a coordinated national strategy. Notably, she signalled a pragmatic shift in the BoE’s stance on stablecoins, indicating that revised proposals will allow systemic stablecoins to hold a portion of their backing assets in high-quality, liquid assets, such as short-dated government securities, rather than just central bank reserves.

### FCA highlights good practice for firms applying to become authorised or registered

The FCA outlines good practices and areas for improvement for firms applying for Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) authorisation or registration. The key takeaways are categorised into three main areas: personnel, policies and procedures, and financial resources.

**1. Personnel:**

- **Promising Practices:** Firms should conduct their own due diligence on key personnel, have a transparent ownership structure, proactively address staffing gaps, and design incentive programs that prioritise good customer outcomes.

- **Areas for Improvement:** Firms must demonstrate their own understanding of regulations and not overly rely on consultants. They need to ensure individuals with multiple roles have a clear time allocation plan and that the business has a genuine UK presence with eligible staff.

**2. Policies and Procedures:**

- **Promising Practices:** Firms should use the FCA’s templates, clearly document decision-making processes (especially for overseas operations), justify requested permissions, and integrate the Consumer Duty throughout their policies.

- **Areas for Improvement:** Policies must be tailored to the specific business, rather than being generic. Systems and controls should account for all customer scenarios, particularly for vulnerable customers. Policies should focus on *how* the firm will comply with regulations, rather than merely restating the rules. Risk assessments should prioritise customer risks, and IT plans must be clear and realistic.

**3. Financial Resources:**

- **Promising Practices:** Utilise FCA templates for financial forecasts, provide detailed supporting notes, and show evidence of funding arrangements and contingency plans.

- **Areas for Improvement:** Firms must submit historical financial accounts with their initial application, ensure all information is accurate, and provide all information required to verify they meet prudential requirements.

**Implications for Firms:**

Ultimately, the FCA is looking for firms that can demonstrate:

- **A deep understanding of their regulatory responsibilities.**

- **A customer-centric approach with the Consumer Duty at the core of their operations.**

- **Robust governance, clear structures, and tailored policies.**

- **Financial soundness and transparency.**

In short, the FCA wants to see a genuine culture of compliance and a commitment to delivering good outcomes for UK consumers, not just a “paper-based" compliance exercise.

## Australia

Australia’s regulatory strategy, clarified in September, mirrors the UK’s "assimilation” model by extending the existing Australian financial services framework to cover key digital asset activities. This was advanced through a policy statement from the Treasury and a facilitative action from the securities regulator, ASIC.

### Digital Assets: Developing an innovative Australian digital asset industry

The Australian Treasury’s statement, “Developing an innovative Australian digital asset industry,” outlined its reform work plan. The strategy leverages the existing Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL) regime:

- **DAPs to be Licensed:** Digital Asset Platforms, such as exchanges and custodians, will be required to obtain an AFSL, subjecting them to established obligations like providing services honestly and fairly and meeting minimum capital requirements.

- **Payment Stablecoins as Stored-Value Facilities (SVFs):** Payment stablecoins will be regulated as a type of Stored-Value Facility (SVF) under the government’s Payments Licensing Reforms, subjecting them to substantially the exact requirements as other SVFs.

🔗 [Link](https://storage.googleapis.com/files-au-treasury/treasury/p/prj37f059c66284c24051948/page/c2025_701519_ed_bill.pdf)

### Digital Assets: ASIC supports innovation through exemptions for distributors of Australian stablecoin

ASIC granted temporary “class relief” for intermediaries involved in the secondary distribution of stablecoins issued by an AFS-licensed entity. This exemption, formalised in *ASIC Corporations (Stablecoin Distribution Exemption) Instrument 2025/631*, is a key piece of regulatory facilitation:

- **Scope of the Exemption:** It exempts distributors like exchanges and wallet providers from needing their own separate AFSL for services related to an eligible, AFS-licensed stablecoin. The first to benefit is AUDM, issued by Catena Digital Pty Ltd.

- **Strategic Purpose:** The temporary measure, which sunsets on June 1, 2028, is designed to reduce the compliance burden for intermediaries and foster a secondary market for regulated Australian stablecoins. ASIC has already proposed extending the exemption to a second stablecoin.

The dominant regulatory strategy in the Anglosphere is “assimilation,” integrating crypto-asset activities into existing financial services legal frameworks. This approach leverages legal precedent and supervisory experience, signalling that digital assets are an evolution of finance. In contrast, Switzerland’s "legal wrapper” approach offers a third way, using existing commercial law to enable innovation with immediate legal enforceability, particularly for wholesale use cases.

## United States

September marked a significant shift in the U.S. digital asset regulatory landscape, moving from enforcement-led ambiguity toward coordinated, incremental clarification. A landmark joint statement from the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) signalled a jurisdictional detente, followed by a pivotal SEC decision to fast-track the listing of crypto-based exchange-traded products (ETPs). These developments, combined with forward-looking initiatives from the CFTC and congressional oversight on AI, indicate a move towards establishing clearer pathways for digital assets within the established financial system.

### Digital Markets: SEC-CFTC Joint Staff Statement (Project Crypto-Crypto Sprint)

The staff of the SEC’s Division of Trading and Markets and the CFTC’s Division of Market Oversight and Division of Clearing and Risk issued a landmark Joint Staff Statement on September 2, 2025. This statement announced a cross-agency initiative, combining the SEC’s “Project Crypto” and the CFTC’s “Crypto Sprint,” to coordinate efforts on enabling the trading of certain spot crypto asset products on regulated exchanges.

The statement’s most significant element was the declaration that “current law does not prohibit SEC- or CFTC-registered exchanges from facilitating trading of these spot crypto asset products”. This applies to SEC-registered national securities exchanges (NSEs) and CFTC-registered designated contract markets (DCMs).

This coordinated declaration signals a pivot towards a more collaborative approach, responding to recommendations from the President’s Working Group on Digital Asset Markets to provide “regulatory clarity that best keeps blockchain-based innovation within the United States”. By affirming a legal pathway for spot crypto trading on regulated U.S. venues, the agencies aim to promote onshore innovation and provide market participants with a choice of regulated platforms

🔗 [Link](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/sec-cftc-project-crypto-090225)

### Digital Assets: SEC - Passing the Buck on Reviewing Proposals to List and Trade Digital Asset ETPs,

A concrete procedural change from the SEC, followed by he joint statement. On September 17, the Commission approved rule changes from Nasdaq, Cboe BZX, and NYSE Arca to adopt generic listing standards for commodity-based ETPs, a category explicitly including those holding digital assets.

This decision alters the go-to-market process for crypto ETPs. Previously, each new crypto ETP required a bespoke Rule 19b-4 filing, a process that could take up to 240 days to complete. The new framework enables exchanges to list any ETP meeting pre-approved generic criteria through a streamlined self-certification process, allowing products to launch within five business days.

To qualify, the ETP’s underlying crypto asset must have had a futures market on a regulated U.S. exchange (such as a CFTC-regulated DCM) with a surveillance-sharing agreement for at least six months. This codifies the logic used for spot Bitcoin ETPs, creating a rules-based pathway for ETPs based on assets like Bitcoin and Ether.

However, the decision was not unanimous. Commissioner Caroline Crenshaw issued a dissenting statement, arguing the SEC was “passing the buck” on its investor protection mission by fast-tracking “new and arguably unproven products to market”. Her dissent was based on the novelty and unique risks of digital asset ETPs, the erosion of the distinction between 33 Act ETPs and more protected 40 Act ETFs, and the contradiction of the SEC’s long history of disapproving spot bitcoin ETPs.

🔗 [Link](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/crenshaw-statement-commodity-based-etps-091725)

### Digital Assets: CFTC - Acting Chairman Pham Launches Tokenised Collateral and Stablecoins Initiative

Acting CFTC Chairman Caroline Pham announced a new initiative focused on the use of tokenised collateral, including stablecoins, in derivatives markets. This initiative, part of the “Crypto Sprint,” aims to modernise collateral management and enhance capital efficiency by establishing a framework for using tokenised assets as regulatory margin. The move, following the passage of the GENIUS Act, is seen as a crucial step toward integrating stablecoins into regulated financial markets.

Meanwhile, the House Financial Services Subcommittee on Digital Assets, Financial Technology, and Artificial Intelligence held a hearing on the use of AI by financial firms and regulators. The discussion highlighted AI’s potential to boost efficiency and enhance fraud detection, but also raised concerns about algorithmic bias and the spread of misinformation.

The combination of these developments suggests a U.S. strategy of creating “regulated on-ramps” for digital assets, incorporating them into familiar structures (such as ETPs) on trusted venues. This pragmatic, incremental approach enables institutionalisation within existing legal frameworks. However, the sharp dissent from Commissioner Crenshaw reveals a deep ideological divide within the SEC, suggesting that the regulatory path forward will remain contentious.

## International

While national regulators implemented jurisdiction-specific rules, international standard-setting bodies provided foundational principles and technical guidance to harmonise the global approach to digital finance. The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) has released a toolkit to enhance the effectiveness of anti-money laundering measures, with a focus on virtual assets. The Wolfsberg Group provided a framework for global banks engaging with stablecoin issuers. Concurrently, the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) and the International Monetary Fund (IMF) published research on emerging regulatory challenges, including the explainability of AI and market fragmentation resulting from tokenisation.

### FinCrime: FATAF-Money Laundering National Risk Assessment Toolkit – Annexes A-C

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF) launched a new Money Laundering National Risk Assessment (NRA) toolkit to help member countries develop a more effective, risk-based approach to combating financial crime.

The toolkit provides practical advice, with Annexe A offering “quick guides” for challenging areas, including **Virtual Assets (VAs) and Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs)**. This initiative signals a shift in FATF’s supervisory expectations from “technical compliance”—transposing the 40 FATF Recommendations into national law—to “demonstrable effectiveness.” The measure of a country’s AML/CFT regime is now its ability to achieve the **11 Immediate Outcomes**, which are the tangible results of an effective system. For VASPs, this means supervisory scrutiny will intensify, with regulators demanding more sophisticated, data-driven risk assessments and evidence of effective mitigation of illicit finance risks.

🔗 [Link](https://www.fatf-gafi.org/en/publications/Methodsandtrends/Money-Laundering-National-Risk-Assessment-Toolkit-Annexes.html)

### FinCrime: Financial Action Task Force Immediate Outcomes Explained

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF), an international standard-setting body, has established a comprehensive framework to combat money laundering, terrorist financing, and the financing of the proliferation of weapons of mass destruction. A provided guide outlines the two main components of this framework: the 11 Immediate Outcomes, which assess the effectiveness of a country’s measures, and the FATF Recommendations, which detail the necessary actions countries should have in place.

The 11 Immediate Outcomes serve as the FATF’s tool to gauge how well a country is implementing its anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing (AML/CFT) regime. These outcomes focus on a risk-based approach, international cooperation, and adequate supervision of both financial and non-financial sectors. Key areas of assessment include the transparency of beneficial ownership of legal entities, the use of economic intelligence, and the successful investigation and prosecution of money laundering and terrorist financing cases. The framework also emphasises asset recovery and the implementation of targeted financial sanctions. Effectiveness is rated on a four-point scale: High, Substantial, Moderate, and Low.

Complementing the Immediate Outcomes are the FATF Recommendations, which provide a detailed blueprint for countries to follow. These recommendations encompass a broad range of topics, including the criminalisation of money laundering and terrorist financing, customer due diligence, record-keeping, and the reporting of suspicious transactions. They also address specific sectors and vulnerabilities, such as politically exposed persons, correspondent banking, new technologies like virtual assets, and the cross-border movement of cash. Compliance with these recommendations is also rated, with categories including Compliant, Largely Compliant, Partially Compliant, and Non-compliant.

**What it means for firms:**

The FATF’s standards have significant implications for financial institutions and designated non-financial businesses and professions (DNFBPs), which include lawyers, real estate agents, and casinos. These entities are on the front line of detecting and preventing illicit financial flows and must implement robust compliance programs to ensure effective prevention.

**Core obligations for firms include:**

- **Risk-Based Approach:** Firms must identify, assess, and understand their money laundering and terrorist financing risks and apply a corresponding level of scrutiny and control. This involves enhanced due diligence for higher-risk clients and transactions.

- **Customer Due Diligence (CDD):** A fundamental requirement is to verify the identity of customers and understand the nature of their business. This extends to identifying the ultimate beneficial owners of legal persons and arrangements to prevent the misuse of corporate structures.

- **Suspicious Transaction Reporting:** Firms are mandated to report suspicious transactions to the national Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU). This financial intelligence is crucial for law enforcement investigations.

- **Record Keeping:** Detailed records of transactions must be maintained to assist in future investigations.

- **Sanctions Compliance:** Firms must implement procedures to ensure they comply with targeted financial sanctions related to terrorism and the proliferation of weapons of mass destruction without delay. This includes freezing the funds of designated individuals and entities.

- **Internal Controls:** Financial groups are required to implement group-wide AML/CFT programs, ensuring consistency across their operations.

Failure by a country to effectively implement the FATF standards can lead to increased scrutiny from the international community. It may result in the country being placed on a list of jurisdictions with strategic AML/CFT deficiencies. This can have significant economic consequences, impacting a country’s access to the global financial system and deterring foreign investment. For firms operating in such jurisdictions, this can mean higher compliance costs, increased transactional friction, and reputational damage. Therefore, adherence to the FATF’s comprehensive framework is not only a regulatory requirement but also a critical component of sound risk management and corporate governance.

### FinCrime: [Wolfsberg Group guidance on providing banking services to fiat-backed stablecoin issuers](https://87cqfwtd.r.us-east-1.awstrack.me/L0/https:%2F%2Fuk.practicallaw.thomsonreuters.com%2FDocument%2FI348590528cb611f08d94a97a2fd4b363%2FView%2FFullText.html%3ForiginationContext=document%26transitionType=DocumentItem%26vr=3.0%26rs=PLUK1.0%26contextData=(sc.PLCurrentAwareness)%26listSource=Alert%26list=PLCurrentAwarenessAlert%26rank=1%26navigationPath=Alert%252Fv1%252FlistNavigation%252FPLCurrentAwarenessAlert%252Fi0a77eba7000001992c08876024b8e01c%253FalertGuid%253Di0a9f804900000178e48a91580627ab84%2526rank%253D1%26alertGuid=i0a9f804900000178e48a91580627ab84/1/010001992c089ca8-fa1cb98d-6333-4fb5-bf49-2e634c768349-000000/UKXGJCL7PiRt74RoMNuLppeI47E=442)

The Wolfsberg Group, an association of twelve central global banks, published its “Guidance on the Provision of Banking Services to Fiat-backed Stablecoin Issuers”. This document addresses the challenges stablecoin issuers face in establishing banking relationships by providing a risk-based framework for financial institutions (FIs) to manage these relationships. Key elements include:

- **Differentiating Account Risks:** The guidance advises FIs to distinguish between low-risk **operating accounts**, higher-risk **reserve accounts**, and highest-risk **settlement accounts**, tailoring monitoring to each type.

- **Assessing the Issuer’s Risk Appetite:** The FI’s primary role is to determine whether the issuer is operating within its stated risk appetite and whether the FI is comfortable with that appetite, placing the onus on the issuer to have a robust control framework.

- **A Proportionate Approach to On-Chain Monitoring:** The guidance rejects “hop-by-hop” monitoring of every stablecoin transaction, advocating instead for a proportionate level of on-chain oversight based on the issuer’s risk profile and client base.

### Digital Assets: Blockchain Consensus Mechanisms A Primer for Supervisors (2025 Update)

The paper provides a 2025 update for financial supervisors on the evolving landscape of blockchain consensus mechanisms and scalability solutions. It revisits Proof-of-Work (PoW) and Proof-of-Stake (PoS), and introduces newer models, such as Solana’s Proof-of-History (PoH). A key focus is the rapid growth of **Layer 2 (L2) scalability solutions** (e.g., Rollups, Sidechains) designed to make blockchains more commercially viable.

The report concludes that while some previously identified risks, such as the centralisation of Bitcoin mining, have not fully materialised, **new and more complex risks have emerged**. These primarily stem from:

1. **Liquid Staking:** This creates risks of hidden leverage, rehypothecation, and contagion, especially when staked assets are tokenised and reused within DeFi. Concentration in a few staking protocols is a significant vulnerability.

2. **Layer 2 Solutions:** Add layers of complexity and introduce new central points of failure (e.g., rollup operators, cross-chain bridges), which are prime targets for cyber-attacks and can amplify market integrity risks like front-running.

3. **Settlement Finality:** The fundamental challenge of probabilistic and varied settlement times across different blockchains remains a key obstacle to integration with traditional financial markets.

The overarching message is that supervisors must move beyond a basic understanding and actively monitor these technological shifts to protect consumers, ensure market integrity, and mitigate potential risks to financial stability.

🔗 [Link](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2025/09/19/Blockchain-Consensus-Mechanisms-A-Primer-for-Supervisors-2025-Update-570531)

### AI: Occasional Paper No 24 Managing explanations: how regulators can address AI explainability

The paper addresses the critical challenge regulators face as financial institutions adopt powerful but opaque Artificial Intelligence (AI) models, such as deep learning and Large Language Models (LLMs). The core problem is the **“explainability gap”**: these complex “black box” models often deliver superior performance, but their decision-making processes cannot be easily understood by humans.

This lack of explainability creates significant risks, undermining model risk management (MRM), regulatory compliance, consumer protection (e.g., hidden biases), and potentially financial stability. The paper finds that existing MRM guidelines are high-level and were not designed for advanced AI, only *implicitly* touching upon explainability. Furthermore, while techniques exist to explain AI models (e.g., SHAP, LIME, counterfactuals), they have severe limitations and can be inaccurate, unstable, or even misleading.

The central argument is that regulators must move beyond existing frameworks and adopt a more nuanced approach to regulation. Instead of an outright ban on complex models, the paper advocates for recognising the **trade-off between model performance and explainability**. It proposes a risk-based system that permits the use of less-explainable but high-performing AI in critical areas, provided that robust safeguards are implemented and the institution provides a clear justification. Ultimately, regulators themselves must urgently **upskill** to supervise this new technological frontier effectively.

🔗 [Link](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsipapers24.htm)

### Risk: BIS Working Papers No 1287 Banks’ regulatory risk tolerance

The paper introduces a new metric, **Regulatory Risk Tolerance (RRT)**, to measure how banks manage their capital. RRT synthesises three key elements of a bank’s strategy:

1. Its target for “management buffers” (capital held above regulatory minimums).

2. The speed at which it restores this buffer after a shock.

3. The volatility of shocks to its buffer.

In essence, a **higher RRT signifies a greater willingness to risk breaching regulatory requirements** to avoid the high costs of maintaining and quickly rebuilding large capital buffers.

The study’s primary finding is that banks across the US and the euro area systematically **increased their RRT** following the Great Financial Crisis (GFC). In response to the more challenging and more expensive Basel III capital framework, banks didn’t just hold more capital; they strategically **lowered their internal management buffer targets**, effectively absorbing part of the regulatory tightening.

Crucially, this strategic choice has significant real-economy consequences. The paper demonstrates that a bank’s RRT level predicts its behaviour under stress:

- **High-RRT banks** respond to a depletion of their capital buffer by **cutting lending**.

- **Low-RRT banks** respond by **de-risking their balance sheets** in other ways (e.g., shifting asset composition) without contracting their loan books.

🔗 [Link](https://www.bis.org/publ/work1287.htm)

### Digital Assets: Optimal Policy for Financial Market Tokenisation

The paper provides the first formal framework for analysing how financial markets might evolve with "tokenisation"—the creation of assets on new, programmable platforms. While these platforms promise significant efficiency gains, such as faster and cheaper settlement, the paper argues that a hands-off approach will likely lead to a suboptimal, fragmented market.

The core problem identified is **trade diversion**. When brokers form coalitions to create a new tokenised market, their primary incentive is not only to improve efficiency, but also to gain a competitive advantage by excluding their rivals. By creating an exclusive, faster settlement network, coalition members can divert trading activity away from competitors, allowing them to capture more business and charge higher fees.

This leads to two key market failures:

1. **Excessive Investment:** Brokers may build a costly tokenised platform solely to gain a competitive edge, even when the social cost of the platform outweighs its efficiency benefits.

2. **Insufficient Tokenisation:** Even when a universal tokenised platform would be highly beneficial for society, brokers will still prefer to form a smaller, exclusionary coalition to maximise their private gains from trade diversion, leaving the market fragmented and inefficient.

The paper concludes that neither of the two main policy tools being considered—**interoperability mandates** or **public cost-sharing (subsidies)**—is sufficient on its own. However, a **combination of both policies** can successfully align private incentives with the public good and achieve an optimal market structure.

🔗 [Link](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WP/Issues/2025/09/19/Optimal-Policy-for-Financial-Market-Tokenization-570540)

### Digital Markets: Chainanalysis - The Road to Crypto Regulation

This report outlines the global state of stablecoin regulation as of August 2025, highlighting a pivotal shift from an industry-driven market to one shaped by formal rules. Regulators, spurred by the potential of stablecoins to improve financial infrastructure and the risks demonstrated by collapses like Terra/Luna, are actively creating frameworks to govern them.

The primary focus of current regulations is on **consumer protection and value stability**. Most emerging frameworks, including the EU’s **MiCA** and the new US **GENIUS Act**, mandate that high-quality, liquid reserves fully back stablecoins. They also enforce clear redemption rights for holders and typically prohibit issuers from paying interest to prevent destabilising bank deposits.

However, this global push has resulted in a **fragmented regulatory landscape**, creating significant challenges for issuers who operate across borders. Different jurisdictions have varying rules on reserve composition, redemption timelines, and the treatment of foreign-issued stablecoins. This patchwork increases operational complexity and compliance costs.

Looking ahead, the next major regulatory frontier is **financial integrity**. Policymakers are moving beyond traditional anti-money laundering (AML) rules for intermediaries. They are now leveraging the transparency of blockchains to expect that **issuers themselves monitor their token’s entire ecosystem** for illicit activity, using tools like blockchain analytics to freeze funds and blocklist addresses proactively.
