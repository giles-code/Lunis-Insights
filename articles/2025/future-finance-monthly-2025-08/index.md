## Executive Summary

August 2025 marked a pivotal month in the global regulation of digital finance, characterised by a decisive and widespread shift from ambiguous, enforcement-led policies to the establishment of comprehensive, forward-looking legal frameworks. The United States signalled a fundamental pro-innovation pivot, with executive and legislative branches moving in concert to provide regulatory clarity, grant the CFTC authority over spot markets, create a federal pathway for stablecoins, and formally welcome banks into the digital asset ecosystem. Concurrently, the European Union advanced the operationalisation of its landmark MiCA regulation, finalising technical standards for market abuse that set a new global benchmark for on-chain and off-chain surveillance.

In Asia, Hong Kong and the UAE cemented their ambitions to become premier regulated digital asset hubs. Hong Kong took forceful steps to codify a robust regime, issuing mandatory standards for virtual asset custody, finalising a stringent prudential framework for stablecoin issuers, and clamping down on market speculation. The UAE moved to eliminate regulatory fragmentation by unifying its federal and emirate-level virtual asset frameworks while advancing the launch of its Digital Dirham CBDC. Elsewhere, the United Kingdom took a significant step toward mainstream adoption by lifting its ban on retail access to crypto ETPs. At the same time, international bodies like the BIS and IMF provided critical macro-level insights, proposing a novel, technology-native app.

## Europe

### EBA Consultation on Revisions to Internal Governance Guidelines

The EBA has initiated a consultation on significant amendments to its guidelines on internal governance, primarily driven by the implementation of Directive (EU) 2024/1619 (CRD VI) and lessons from recent supervisory cycles. The proposed revisions aim to enhance the robustness and transparency of institutional frameworks.

**Key Technical Revisions:**

- **Third-Country Branches (TCBs):** The proposals introduce enhanced prudential requirements for TCBs, mandating the implementation of a comprehensive governance framework analogous to that of EU-domiciled institutions. This includes the appointment of at least two resident managers and the maintenance of "sufficient local substance" to mitigate risks associated with "empty shell" structures.

- **Formalisation of Responsibilities:** A new requirement mandates the creation of two core documents: (i) **Individual Statements of Roles and Duties** for all members of the management body, senior management, and key function holders, and (ii) a **Comprehensive Responsibility Map** detailing reporting lines and accountability across the entire organisation.

- **Diversity and Inclusion:** The guidelines now prescribe the implementation of gender-neutral policies for remuneration, recruitment, and career progression, supported by quantitative monitoring of the gender pay gap and other diversity metrics.

- **Expanded Risk Management Scope:** The risk management perimeter is formally extended to include **ESG risks**, requiring institutions to develop strategic plans with quantifiable targets over short, medium, and long-term horizons (a minimum of 10 years). The framework is also updated to align with the **Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA)** and the emerging **AI Act**, explicitly incorporating ICT and AI-related risks.

- **Conflict of Interest Protocols:** The framework for managing conflicts of interest has been reinforced, with specific protocols for transactions involving members of the management body. Such transactions must be executed on an arm's-length basis and be subject to rigorous, documented, and objective decision-making processes.

  - **Implications:** The proposed revisions necessitate a fundamental review of corporate governance structures. Firms, particularly TCBs, face increased operational and compliance costs to meet heightened prudential standards. The mandate for formal documentation will require a granular mapping of internal responsibilities, impacting board charters, committee terms of reference, and individual job descriptions. The integration of non-financial risks into the core risk management apparatus demands significant investment in data, modelling capabilities, and strategic planning.

### MiCA: Regulatory Technical Standards for Market Abuse Finalised

The EU has published the Regulatory Technical Standards (RTS) that operationalise the market abuse framework under Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 (MiCA). These standards impose a technically rigorous compliance regime on all entities that professionally arrange or execute transactions in crypto assets.

**Core Technical Mandates:**

- **Multi-Faceted Surveillance:** Firms must deploy and maintain systems for the "effective and ongoing monitoring" of all orders and transactions. This surveillance must capture and analyse heterogeneous data sets, including **on-chain transactions, off-chain order book data, and mempool information**, to effectively detect market manipulation and insider dealing.

- **Suspicious Transaction and Order Reporting (STOR):** The obligation to submit a STOR to the relevant competent authority is triggered upon the formation of a "reasonable suspicion" of market abuse. The submission must be executed "without delay" via a harmonised electronic template.

- **Protocol-Level Monitoring:** In a novel extension of traditional surveillance, firms are required to monitor the functioning of the underlying DLT, including its consensus mechanism and validator behaviour, to identify potentially manipulative activities at the protocol level.

- **Human-in-the-Loop Analysis:** The RTS explicitly precludes fully automated surveillance, mandating an "appropriate level of human analysis" to investigate and contextualise alerts generated by surveillance systems.

  - **Implications:** Achieving compliance requires a material investment in advanced surveillance infrastructure. Existing market abuse systems (e.g., for Market Abuse Regulation, or MAR) must be re-engineered to ingest and process on-chain data, posing a significant challenge to data architecture. Firms must develop novel surveillance typologies and analytical models to address protocol-level risks. The substantial compliance overhead is expected to act as a significant barrier to entry, likely accelerating market consolidation and fostering a specialised RegTech vendor ecosystem.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202500885)

### DORA: Clarifications on Reporting Scope and Systemic Cyber Coordination

Two developments have provided greater clarity on the implementation of the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA), focusing on the scope of reporting and the EU's macroprudential coordination capabilities.

### Q&A on the Register of Information:

A clarification from the Joint European Supervisory Authorities confirmed that a financial entity's DORA Register of Information must include non-financial entities within its consolidation group if they are integral to the provision of ICT services. This scope encompasses both non-financial entities that act as formal intra-group ICT service providers and parties to contractual arrangements for ICT services on behalf of a financial entity.

- **Implication:** This interpretation expands the scope of DORA's mapping exercise, requiring firms to conduct a thorough assessment of their entire group structure to identify and document all ICT dependencies. This necessitates a robust data governance framework for the firm's Third-Party Risk Management (TPRM) and intra-group oversight programs.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.eba.europa.eu/single-rule-book-qa/qna/view/publicId/2025_7297)

### DORA: ESRB Report on Pan-European Cyber Coordination:

The European Systemic Risk Board (ESRB) assessed the development of the Pan-European Systemic Cyber Incident Coordination Framework (EU-SCICF). The report concluded that while the conceptual design is advanced, significant concerns persist regarding its operational readiness ahead of the January 2025 DORA implementation deadline.

- **Implication:** The high-level focus on a functional EU-SCICF signals that supervisory authorities will place a premium on a firm's demonstrated ability to withstand and recover from severe but plausible cyber incidents. Firms should anticipate an increase in mandatory, cross-jurisdictional resilience testing and heightened supervisory scrutiny of their incident response plans and crisis communication protocols.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.esrb.europa.eu/pub/pdf/recommendations/esrb.compliancereportsummary_202507.en.pdf)

### FinCrime: EBA Report on AML/CFT Supervisory Technology (SupTech)

The EBA's report on the adoption of SupTech for AML/CFT supervision indicates a clear trajectory toward more data-driven and technologically sophisticated oversight, despite adoption being in its early stages.

**Key Findings and Strategic Implications for Firms:**

- **Data Integrity as a Supervisory Priority:** As supervisors increasingly leverage data analytics, the quality, completeness, and governance of a firm's regulatory data will become a primary focus of examinations. Data deficiencies will be more readily identified, leading to more targeted supervisory interventions.

- **Convergence Towards a Common Supervisory Methodology:** The combination of AMLA's establishment and the proliferation of SupTech will drive a harmonisation of supervisory approaches across the EU. This will demand greater consistency in firms' internal controls and risk management frameworks across jurisdictions.

- **Elevated Expectations for Internal Systems:** Supervisory adoption of AI and machine learning will create an implicit expectation for firms to modernise their own AML/CFT compliance infrastructure. Reliance on legacy systems and manual processes will likely be viewed as a governance weakness.

- **Demand for Explainable AI (XAI):** As supervisors scrutinise the "explainability" of their own models, they will impose the same standards on firms. Firms deploying AI/ML for functions such as transaction monitoring or risk scoring must be able to validate and clearly articulate the logic and decision-making processes of their models to regulators.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.eba.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-08/2ed3b67d-880b-428d-8282-15689f65b12f/Report%20on%20the%20use%20of%20AMLCFT%20SupTech%20tools.pdf)

### Digital Assets: European Parliament - A Focus on Regulatory Sufficiency:

A report from the Parliament posits that foreign-denominated stablecoins do not constitute a material threat to the EU's monetary sovereignty. It argues that the institutional credibility of the euro, combined with the robust regulatory perimeter established by MiCA, effectively mitigates this risk. The report redirects criticism toward the proposed design of the digital euro, labelling the planned low holding limits (e.g., €3,000) as a "major mistake" that would inhibit its utility and competitiveness against both private digital assets and commercial bank deposits.

- **Implication:** This perspective suggests MiCA is functioning as intended, providing policymakers with the latitude to design a more ambitious and functionally practical digital euro without immediate existential pressure from foreign stablecoins.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/cmsdata/296480/MD_SFL%20June%202025_FINAL.pdf)

### Digital Assets: ECB - A Geopolitical and Financial Stability Perspective:

Conversely, an ECB publication frames the market dominance of USD-denominated stablecoins as a direct threat to Europe's financial stability and monetary sovereignty. The ECB views the trend as a form of "digital dollarisation" that could lead to deposit disintermediation from the EU banking sector and weaken the transmission of its monetary policy. The bank advocates for a proactive strategy to foster a native ecosystem for euro-denominated stablecoins and to accelerate the development of the digital euro.

- **Implication:** The ECB's stance is a direct call to action for the European financial industry. It signals a clear policy preference for the development of MiCA-compliant, euro-denominated stablecoins, creating a significant, regulator-endorsed market opportunity for first-movers. For incumbent banks, it is a warning to innovate or risk ceding the digital currency landscape to foreign competitors.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/blog/html/index.en.html)

## United Kingdom

### Digital Assets: FCA Lifts Ban on Retail Access to Crypto ETNs

The FCA has reversed its 2021 ban on retail access to cryptoasset-backed Exchange Traded Notes (cETNs), effective October 8, 2025. This landmark decision, however, is subject to strict guardrails: cETNs must be listed on a UK Recognised Investment Exchange (RIE), and firms offering them must adhere to financial promotion rules and the Consumer Duty. Notably, these investments will not be covered by the Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS).

- **Implications:** This move legitimises cryptoassets within the mainstream UK investment landscape, creating significant product development opportunities for asset managers and exchanges. However, the high compliance burden of the Consumer Duty places a heavy responsibility on wealth managers and brokers to ensure suitability and clear risk communication, creating a substantial risk of mis-selling litigation.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-opens-retail-access-crypto-etns)

### Payments: FCA Strengthens Safeguarding Regime for Payments & E-money Firms

The FCA published new rules (PS 25/12) enhancing the safeguarding regime for payments and e-money firms, effective May 7, 2026. Key changes include mandatory safeguarding reconciliations on each "reconciliation day," the maintenance of a "resolution pack" that can be retrieved within 48 hours to expedite the return of customer funds in the event of insolvency, and a requirement for most firms to conduct an annual safeguarding audit.

- **Implications:** The new regime imposes a significant increase in the compliance and operational burden, particularly for smaller firms. Firms must upgrade internal systems for more frequent reconciliations, formalise third-party due diligence and diversification strategies, and engage qualified auditors to meet the new annual reporting requirements.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/policy/ps25-12.pdf)

### Digital Assets: FMLC Highlights Legal Uncertainties in Stablecoin & Custody Rules

The Financial Markets Law Committee (FMLC) responded to the FCA's consultation on stablecoin and cryptoasset custody, welcoming the proposals but raising concerns about legal uncertainty. Key issues include the overly broad definition of a stablecoin "issuer," which could inadvertently capture ancillary service providers, and the lack of a clear transition path for a stablecoin designated as systemic. The FMLC also argued against a "one-size-fits-all" statutory trust for backing assets, advocating for a more flexible, outcomes-focused approach.

- **Implications:** The legal ambiguities identified by the FMLC could create disproportionate regulatory obligations and operational barriers for firms. A lack of clarity on the systemic transition process, in particular, poses a significant market risk, as a sudden requirement to shift backing assets could cause instability for the issuer.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/money-laundering-reporting-obligations/guidance-on-money-laundering-reporting-obligations-in-relation-to-the-daml-exemption-provisions-introduced-by-the-economic-crime-and-corporate-transpa)

### FinCrime: Updated Guidance on POCA Money Laundering Exemptions

Updated guidance was published on exemptions from principal money laundering offences under the Proceeds of Crime Act 2002 (POCA). The key changes, driven by the ECCT Act 2023, include increasing the threshold to £3,000 for both the "operating an account" exemption (for deposit-takers and payment firms) and the new "paying away" exemption, which allows firms to return funds when terminating a client relationship. A "mixed property" exemption was also introduced.

- **Implications:** The higher thresholds reduce the administrative burden on firms by lowering the number of Defence Against Money Laundering (DAML) SARs required for low-value transactions and account closures. This enhances operational efficiency but reinforces the critical importance of having robust Customer Due Diligence (CDD) processes, as compliance with CDD is a precondition for using the "paying away" exemption.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/904/introduction/made)

### OpRes: HM Treasury Publishes G7 Framework for Cyber Incident Reconnection

HM Treasury released a G7 Cyber Expert Group paper outlining a best-practice framework for determining when and how to reconnect with an organisation safely after a cyber incident. The guidance proposes a five-phase model: **(i) Assess, (ii) Remediate, (iii) Assure, (iv) Reconnect, and (v) Recover**. It emphasises cooperation, transparent communication, and the use of independent third-party assurance and formal attestations.

- **Implications:** This framework sets a new benchmark for incident response. Firms are now expected to develop proactive, internal reconnection frameworks aligned with this model. This requires establishing transparent governance, structured communication protocols, and predefined criteria for the formal attestations that will be needed to restore connectivity with partners and clients.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687f8f21f2ecaeb756d0e1f1/G7_CEG_Reconnection_Framework_Best_Practice.pdf)

### OpRes: FCA Cyber Coordination Group Shares Industry Insights

The FCA shared key insights from its Cyber Coordination Group, highlighting industry best practices and the challenges they pose. The report praised the effectiveness of threat-led penetration testing (e.g., CBEST) and the use of cross-industry forums for managing third-party incidents. Key risks identified include the cumulative effect of multiple non-critical vulnerabilities, the challenge of securing legacy systems, and emerging threats from the insecure use of AI.

- **Implications:** The insights signal a clear supervisory expectation for firms to move beyond passive defence. Firms must treat third-party risk as their own, invest in proactive threat-hunting capabilities, and establish formal governance bodies to manage the risks and opportunities presented by AI before widespread deployment.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://fmlc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/FMLC_Reponse-to-FCA-CP25-14.pdf)

### Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 Commenced (Smart Data)

Key provisions of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 came into force on 20 August 2025. This includes Part 1 of the Act, which establishes new frameworks for accessing and sharing customer and business data (i.e., "Smart Data"), alongside numerous amendments to the Data Protection Act 2018 and PEC Regulations.

- **Implications:** This legislation represents a significant overhaul of the UK's data landscape. Firms must adapt their data infrastructure and governance policies to comply with new data portability and sharing obligations. The changes necessitate a thorough review of all data processing activities to ensure alignment with the strengthened data protection provisions and the Information Commissioner's expanded powers.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/good-and-poor-practice/fca-cyber-coordination-group-insights-2024)

### AI: Law Commission Explores Foundational Legal Challenges of AI

The Law Commission published a discussion paper on AI, highlighting how its core characteristics—autonomy, adaptiveness, and opacity—strain foundational legal principles. The paper examines the risk of "liability gaps" where neither humans nor corporate entities can be held responsible for harm caused by an AI. It also examines how AI's "black box" nature poses a threat to transparency and accountability in public law decision-making.

- **Implications:** This paper signals that existing legal frameworks are ill-equipped to handle advanced AI, and minor legal tweaks may be insufficient. It puts firms on notice that a new standard of "digital diligence" is emerging, where they will be held accountable for how they choose, test, monitor, and rely on AI systems. The analysis suggests that proactive, forward-thinking legislative reform will be necessary to address these fundamental challenges.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://lawcom.gov.uk/news/artificial-intelligence-and-the-law-a-discussion-paper/)

## New Zealand

### Digital Assets: FMA Spotlight on Cryptocurrencies

The Financial Markets Authority (FMA) has reiterated its stance on the cryptocurrency sector, emphasising that while the assets themselves are not explicitly regulated, firms providing financial services related to them fall squarely within the existing regulatory perimeter.

**Regulatory Position:**

- **Application of Existing Laws:** Crypto exchanges, brokers, wallet providers, and ICO issuers are considered financial service providers and must comply with the Financial Markets Conduct Act 2013 (FMCA), the Anti-Money Laundering and Countering Financing of Terrorism Act 2013 (AML/CFT Act), and the Financial Service Providers Act 2008 (FSPA).

- **Mandatory Registration:** Firms must register on the Financial Service Providers Register (FSPR) and, in most cases, belong to an independent Dispute Resolution Scheme (DRS), providing consumers with a clear path for redress.

- **Focus on Scams:** The FMA highlighted that a significant portion of its warnings relate to crypto-related scams, often involving fake investment platforms promising unrealistic returns.

**Strategic Implications for Firms:**

- **Compliance is Non-Negotiable:** The primary implication is that regulatory compliance is mandatory. Firms cannot operate in a grey area; they must adhere to all applicable financial services laws, including robust AML/CFT obligations and fair dealing provisions under the FMCA.

- **Consumer Protection as a Core Function:** Given the high prevalence of scams, firms have a heightened responsibility to protect consumers. This includes providing clear warnings about the speculative nature of crypto, the risks of fraud, and the irreversibility of transactions.

- **Operational and Reputational Risk:** Firms must implement rigorous operational security measures to protect client assets and conduct thorough due diligence on partners to avoid reputational damage resulting from association with illicit activities. The FMA's focus on this area suggests that a firm's approach to scam prevention will be a key area of supervisory scrutiny.

## Hong Kong

### Digital Assets: Establishing a Comprehensive Regulatory Framework

Hong Kong regulators have taken decisive steps to establish a robust framework for digital assets, focusing on prudential standards for stablecoin issuance, operational requirements for virtual asset custody, and market integrity.

### Digital Assets: SFC Codifies Custody Standards for Virtual Asset Trading Platforms (VATPs)

The Securities and Futures Commission (SFC) issued a circular establishing immediate, mandatory minimum standards for the custody of client virtual assets. The framework mandates the formal designation of a Responsible Officer (RO) or Manager-in-Charge (MIC) with explicit oversight of the custody function. It also requires that private keys be generated and stored offline in certified Hardware Security Modules (HSMs) and that operational workflows be re-engineered to eliminate "blind signing" by using dedicated, secure hardware for transaction verification.

- **Strategic Implications:** This circular establishes an immediate and auditable compliance checklist, shifting from principles-based guidance to a prescriptive control framework. Firms must perform a formal gap analysis of their custody architecture, conduct rigorous technical due diligence on all third-party providers, and invest in technology to ensure verifiable transaction integrity. Accountability is now explicitly assigned to senior management, heightening personal liability.

<sup>🔗</sup> [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://apps.sfc.hk/edistributionWeb/gateway/EN/circular/intermediaries/supervision/doc?refNo=25EC44#:~:text=This%20circular%20elaborates%20on%20the,their%20compliance%20with%20these%20requirements.)

### Digital Assets: Joint Regulatory Intervention on Stablecoin-Related Market Speculation

The Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) and the SFC issued a joint statement to address abrupt price volatility in equities linked to corporate announcements regarding stablecoin licensing ambitions. The regulators clarified that the prudential bar for licensing is "reasonably high," anticipating only a "handful of licences" will be granted initially. The SFC initiated intensified market surveillance and warned of its readiness to take "forceful and decisive actions" against manipulative practices.

- **Strategic Implications:** This represents a direct regulatory intervention aimed at curbing speculative behaviour. It creates significant legal and reputational risks for listed companies that make premature or ambiguous public statements. The statement signals a highly selective and controlled licensing process, effectively warning the market that using regulatory engagement for stock promotion is a high-risk strategy that will attract immediate scrutiny.

<sup>🔗</sup> [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.hkma.gov.hk/eng/news-and-media/press-releases/2025/08/20250814-8/)

### Digital Assets: HKMA Finalises Prudential Framework for Stablecoin Issuers

The HKMA's guidelines under the new Stablecoins Ordinance establish a stringent supervisory regime. Key pillars of the framework include: a mandate for stablecoins to be fully backed by a segregated pool of high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) held in a legally distinct trust; a minimum paid-up share capital requirement of HK\$25 million; and a legally enforceable right for holders to redeem stablecoins at par on a T+1 basis. The framework also explicitly prohibits issuers from paying interest on stablecoin holdings.

- **Strategic Implications:** This framework establishes a high barrier to entry, favouring well-capitalised, institution-backed issuers and effectively precluding algorithmic or under-collateralised models. The conservative, safety-focused approach is designed to build institutional trust but may constrain certain forms of financial innovation. The stringent AML/CFT requirements will be a core component of the supervisory assessment, demanding significant investment in compliance infrastructure.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.hkma.gov.hk/eng/news-and-media/press-releases/2025/07/20250729-4/)

## United Arab Emirates

### Digital Assets & Payments: CBUAE Advances Digital Dirham Initiative for Q4 2025 Launch

The Central Bank of the UAE (CBUAE) is advancing the launch of its Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC), the Digital Dirham, with a full-scale rollout anticipated in Q4 2025. The initiative is a core component of the CBUAE's Financial Infrastructure Transformation (FIT) program. The CBDC will be non-interest-bearing and operate on a two-tier intermediated model, where the CBUAE issues the currency and licensed financial institutions (LFIs) and payment service providers manage distribution and customer-facing wallets.

- Implications: The intermediated model provides a clear business case for incumbent LFIs and FinTechs to remain central to the UAE's payment infrastructure. The programmability of the Digital Dirham will enable new business models, such as fractionalised asset ownership and automated disbursements. For firms engaged in international trade, the CBDC's integration with cross-border payment projects, such as mBridge, promises to reduce friction and costs in settlements.

🔗 [Link](https://www.centralbank.ae/en/news-and-publications/publications/cbuae-policy-papers/digital-dirham-a-primer-on-the-uae-s-central-bank-digital-currency/)

### Digital Markets: SCA and VARA Unify Virtual Asset Regulatory Framework

The UAE's Federal Securities and Commodities Authority (SCA) and Dubai's Virtual Assets Regulatory Authority (VARA) have signed a landmark agreement to establish a unified, nationwide regulatory framework for virtual assets. The deal establishes reciprocal recognition of Virtual Asset Service Provider (VASP) licenses, coordinated supervision, and joint enforcement actions, eliminating regulatory duplication between the federal and emirate levels.

- **Implications:** This agreement significantly reduces regulatory fragmentation and complexity for VASPs operating in the UAE. A single licensing and oversight system will streamline the application process, lower compliance costs, and provide greater legal certainty for market participants. The harmonisation of rules is a critical step in maturing the UAE's digital asset ecosystem and strengthening its position as a global hub.

🔗 [Link](https://www.sca.gov.ae/en/media-center/news/9/9/2024/sca-and-vara-set-regulatory-framework-for-the-uaes-virtual-assets-sector-in-boost-to-the-countrys-.aspx)

## United States

### Digital Markets: Executive & Inter-Agency Pro-Innovation Agenda

A fundamental shift in US policy occurred, driven by the President's Working Group on Digital Asset Markets, aiming to reverse the previous "regulation by enforcement" approach and establish the US as a global leader in digital finance.

### Digital Markets: President's Working Group Sets Pro-Growth Agenda

The multi-agency report provides a comprehensive roadmap to foster innovation and growth. Key recommendations include granting the CFTC apparent authority over spot markets for non-security digital assets, providing permissive guidance for banks to engage in crypto activities (custody, tokenisation), championing privately issued stablecoins under a federal framework (the "GENIUS Act"), and prohibiting a US CBDC.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/01/strengthening-american-leadership-in-digital-financial-technology/)

### Digital Assets: "Project Crypto" and "Crypto Sprint" Launched by the SEC and CFTC

In a coordinated effort, the SEC and CFTC launched initiatives to implement the recommendations of the Working Group. The CFTC's "sprint" includes a proposal to allow trading of spot crypto-asset contracts on CFTC-registered futures exchanges. The SEC's "Project Crypto" aims to establish bright-line rules for classifying crypto assets, create a tailored regulatory framework for digital asset securities, and modernise market infrastructure to support on-chain systems.

- **Implications:** This represents a seismic, top-down shift towards a supportive regulatory environment. The end of inter-agency jurisdictional disputes and the move towards a unified framework significantly reduces the risk for all participants in the US market. The policy pivot creates powerful tailwinds for onshore investment, product development, and institutional adoption. Not having a US digital asset strategy is now a significant competitive disadvantage.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9104-25)

### Digital Markets: Legislative Developments

Momentum is building in Congress to codify a durable legal framework for digital assets, moving to replace regulatory ambiguity with legislative clarity.

### Digital Markets: Senate Banking Committee Issues Comprehensive RFI

Republican members, led by Chairman Tim Scott, released a discussion draft and a broad Request for Information (RFI) for a digital asset market structure bill. The RFI seeks detailed feedback on definitively dividing jurisdiction between the SEC and CFTC, establishing investor protections in the event of bankruptcy, authorising banks to engage in crypto activities, and creating a federal preemption over state-level rules.

**House Advances Key Legislation:** House Financial Services Chairman French Hill highlighted significant bipartisan support for key bills, including the passage of the GENIUS Act (for payment stablecoins) and the advancement of the Digital Asset Market Clarity (CLARITY) Act, which aims to create a clear regulatory framework.

- **Implications:** The legislative branch is actively working to end "regulation by enforcement." The RFI and existing bills signal a near-certainty of a larger role for the CFTC and a formal invitation for banks to enter the market. The potential for a legislative safe harbour for past token sales and federal preemption over state laws could resolve billions in legal uncertainty and create a single, unified US market.

### Digital Markets: SEC Policy and Market Structure Enhancements

Under new leadership, the SEC has taken several steps to align its rules with the pro-innovation agenda, focusing on market access and providing targeted guidance to support this goal.

### Digital Assets: SEC Permits In-Kind Creations for Crypto ETPs

In a significant policy reversal, the SEC approved in-kind creations and redemptions for crypto asset Exchange-Traded Products (ETPs). This aligns crypto ETPs with the more efficient operational model used for other commodity ETPs. The SEC also approved a mixed Bitcoin/Ether ETP and options on spot Bitcoin ETPs and is soliciting comments for ETPs based on other large-cap crypto assets.

- **Implications:** The shift to an in-kind model dramatically reduces costs and tracking error for ETP issuers, making the products more efficient and attractive. It forces a deeper integration between traditional financial market makers and the spot crypto ecosystem. The approval of new product types and the clear path for ETPs beyond Bitcoin and Ether will unlock a new wave of institutional demand and product development.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom)

### Digital Assets: SEC Staff Issues Nuanced Statement on Liquid Staking

The SEC's Division of Corporation Finance has issued a statement outlining a specific, narrow model of liquid staking that it views as a "ministerial" service, which does not constitute the offer and sale of a security. The guidance hinges on the provider not exercising entrepreneurial or managerial efforts.

- **Implications:** Although not a formal rule, the statement provides a potential safe harbour and a highly sought-after blueprint for firms to structure liquid staking services in a compliant, non-security framework. However, the scope is extremely narrow, and any deviation from the described ministerial function would place an activity outside the guidance, meaning significant legal risk remains for more complex or innovative staking models.

## International

### Digital Assets: BIS Survey Indicates Strong Central Bank Engagement with Digital Currencies

The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) 2024 survey reveals sustained and deepening engagement by central banks with digital currencies. 91% of the 93 surveyed central banks are exploring CBDCs, with work on wholesale CBDCs being more advanced than retail, particularly in advanced economies. A key driver is the desire to preserve the role of central bank money as cash usage declines and asset tokenisation grows.

- **Implications:** The survey points towards a future financial landscape where public and private digital currencies coexist. The advanced state of wholesale CBDC projects signals a push by central banks to provide a secure settlement asset for an increasingly tokenised financial market. For private firms, this creates both a competitive dynamic and an opportunity to build services on top of new public infrastructure.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.bis.org/publ/bppdf/bispap159.htm)

### FinCrime: BIS Proposes Novel AML Compliance Framework for Cryptoassets

A BIS bulletin has proposed a new approach to Anti-Money Laundering (AML) compliance for cryptoassets that leverages the inherent traceability of public blockchains. The proposal centres on creating an "AML compliance score" for cryptoassets based on their transaction history. This score would be referenced at fiat "off-ramps" (e.g., exchanges) to assess the likelihood of a connection to illicit activity.

- **Implications:** This represents a potential paradigm shift in AML regulation, moving from a purely intermediary-based model to one that utilises the technology's native characteristics. If adopted, it would create a new market for compliance service providers that can analyse on-chain data to generate these scores. It also introduces the concept of a "duty of care" for users, incentivising a self-policing ecosystem where transacting with "clean" assets becomes the norm.

**<sup>🔗</sup>** [**<sup>Link</sup>**](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull111.pdf)

### Digital Markets: IMF Paper Decrypts International Stablecoin Flows

An IMF Working Paper presented a novel methodology using AI and machine learning to estimate the geographic distribution of international stablecoin flows. The 2024 analysis found that while North America and the Asia-Pacific region lead in absolute volumes, flows are most significant relative to GDP in Latin America and the Caribbean (7.7%) and Africa and the Middle East (6.7%). North America was identified as a significant net exporter of stablecoins, meeting global demand for U.S. dollars.

- **Implications:** The data provides clear evidence of significant product-market fit for stablecoins in emerging markets, particularly for use cases like remittances and cross-border payments. This highlights considerable growth opportunities for firms targeting these regions. The paper also highlights the deep interdependence between the crypto ecosystem and the traditional U.S. banking system, as disruptions in the latter directly impact global stablecoin flows.
