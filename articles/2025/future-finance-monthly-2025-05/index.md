## Executive Summary

May witnessed a surge of global regulatory activity in the digital asset sphere, with a particular focus on establishing frameworks for stablecoins, clarifying market structure, and enhancing discussions surrounding Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs). Key jurisdictions, including the European Union, the United Kingdom, Hong Kong, and the United States, all made significant advancements, indicating a persistent effort towards comprehensive oversight of the expanding crypto economy.

In **Europe**, the focus was on refining existing frameworks. The European Union (EU) published a corrigendum to the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA) regarding risk management tools. Meanwhile, the European Central Bank (ECB) provided an update on the implementation of the digital euro, indicating ongoing progress in its central bank digital currency (CBDC) exploration. **Switzerland** also saw movement in the digital markets sphere with developments concerning stablecoins.

The **United Kingdom** demonstrated a multi-faceted approach to digital market regulation. The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) launched consultations on a prudential regime for cryptoasset firms, as well as specific rules for stablecoin issuance and cryptoasset custody. The FCA also released a discussion paper covering a wide range of crypto activities, including trading platforms, intermediaries, staking, lending, borrowing, and Decentralised Finance (DeFi). Further highlighting the legal complexities, the Financial Markets Law Committee (FMLC) submitted a letter to HM Treasury on legal uncertainties surrounding draft cryptoasset regulated activities. On the payments front, the Bank of England delivered a speech emphasising the value of harmonised international payment rails.

Across Asia, **Hong Kong** has made a decisive move by passing its Stablecoins Bill, with a new licensing regime anticipated to be operational in 2025 following further consultation and the finalisation of detailed requirements.

The **United States** continued to witness legislative and regulatory engagement. Notable developments included ongoing legislative progress in digital markets, analysis from the Congressional Research Service (CRS) on stablecoin legislation (including the GENIUS Act of 2025 and key issues for the 119th Congress), and a report on Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs). Regulatory bodies were also active, with a restatement of a joint SEC and FINRA position on broker-dealer custody of digital asset securities. The SEC also addressed certain protocol staking activities and the tokenisation of real-world assets.

Internationally, organisations like the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) contributed to the discourse with working papers on stablecoins and safe asset prices, the verifiability of Total Value Locked (TVL) in DeFi, and an empirical analysis of cross-border Bitcoin, Ether, and stablecoin flows. Additionally, the Government Accountability Office (GAO) released a report on the use and oversight of Artificial Intelligence in financial services, a topic increasingly intertwined with digital asset innovation.

Overall, May's regulatory developments underscore a global trend towards establishing more explicit rules of the road for the digital asset industry, with a strong focus on mitigating risks, enhancing market integrity, and preparing for the future of finance.

**Key Themes:**

- **Stablecoins in the Spotlight:** Hong Kong took a significant step by passing its Stablecoins Bill, paving the way for a licensing regime in 2025. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) also launched consultations on stablecoin issuance and custody rules. In the US, legislative progress on stablecoins, notably the GENIUS Act of 2025 (S. 1582), was a key focus, with the Congressional Research Service (CRS) providing analysis on this and broader stablecoin legislation issues. Internationally, a BIS Working Paper explored the impact of stablecoins on safe asset prices.

- **Comprehensive Crypto Frameworks Evolving:** The UK was particularly active, with the FCA releasing a discussion paper that covers a wide array of cryptoasset activities, including trading platforms, intermediaries, staking, lending, borrowing, and DeFi. The regulator also consulted on a prudential regime for cryptoasset firms. The Financial Markets Law Committee (FMLC) provided feedback to HM Treasury on legal uncertainties in draft cryptoasset regulations. In the US, there was legislative movement on digital asset market structure.

- **Focus on Risk Management and Investor Protection:** The European Union (EU) has published a corrigendum to the Delegated Regulation on risk management tools under the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA). The US SEC and FINRA withdrew a 2019 joint statement concerning broker-dealer custody of digital asset securities, potentially signalling a revised approach. The SEC also addressed certain protocol staking activities and the tokenisation of real-world assets.

- **Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) Advancing:** The European Central Bank (ECB) continued its work on the digital euro, with a letter from Claudia Buch discussing its implementation. The US CRS also published a report on Central Bank Digital Currencies.

- **International Cooperation and Harmonisation:** The Bank of England highlighted the value of harmonised international payment rails. The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) has contributed several working papers, including analyses of cross-border Bitcoin, Ether, and stablecoin flows, as well as the verifiability of Total Value Locked (TVL) in decentralised finance (DeFi).

- **Emerging Technologies - AI in Finance:** The US Government Accountability Office (GAO) released a report on the use and oversight of Artificial Intelligence in financial services, indicating growing attention on this interconnected field.

**Regional Highlights:**

- **Europe (EU & Switzerland):** Alongside the DORA update and the ECB's progress on the digital euro, Switzerland witnessed developments related to stablecoins.

- **United Kingdom:** Demonstrated a proactive and comprehensive approach with multiple consultations and discussion papers from the FCA, alongside input from the FMLC and a focus on international payment systems by the Bank of England.

- **Hong Kong:** Achieved a significant milestone with the passage of its Stablecoins Bill.

- **The United States** exhibited significant legislative activity regarding stablecoins and digital asset market structure, accompanied by guidance and statements from the SEC, as well as CRS reports on CBDCs and stablecoins.

Overall, May highlighted a global trend toward establishing more precise regulatory boundaries for the digital asset industry. While approaches vary, the common goals of fostering innovation, managing risk, protecting consumers, and ensuring financial stability are evident across jurisdictions. International discussions and research, particularly from bodies such as the BIS, continue to play a crucial role in informing these national and regional efforts.

## Europe

### DORA: Corrigendum to Commission Delegated Regulation on RTS on risk management tools under DORA published in OJ

- **Document Corrected:** Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1774, which supplements the Digital Operational Resilience Act (DORA - Regulation (EU) 2022/2554). Specifically, it details regulatory technical standards for ICT risk management tools, methods, processes, policies, and the simplified ICT risk management framework.

- **Specific Correction:** The corrigendum amends Article 22, point (d) of the first subparagraph. This article addresses the ICT-related incident management policy.

- **Nature of the Error:** The original text incorrectly referenced "Article 15 of Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1772" for guidance on retaining evidence related to ICT incidents.

- **Correction Made:** The reference has been changed to **"Article 8(2) of Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1772"**.

- **Core Requirement Unchanged (by this Corrigendum):** The fundamental requirement to retain evidence relating to ICT-related incidents for a period "no longer than necessary for the purposes for which the data are collected, commensurate with the criticality of the affected business functions, supporting processes, and ICT and information assets... and with any applicable retention requirement under Union law" remains the same. Only the specific cross-reference to the detailed rules within Regulation (EU) 2024/1772 has been corrected.

**Firm Implications**

1. **Update Internal Policies and Procedures:** Firms must ensure their internal policies, procedures, and documentation related to ICT-related incident management, specifically concerning the retention of evidence, now correctly refer to **Article 8(2) of Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1772**.

2. **Review Compliance with the Correct Article:** Firms must review the requirements outlined in Article 8(2) of Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1772 to ensure their evidence retention practices fully comply with this corrected reference. Suppose their previous understanding was based on an incorrect interpretation of Article 15 (which may have different or nonexistent requirements on this specific point). In that case, adjustments to their retention schedules or processes may be necessary.

3. **Legal Certainty:** This corrigendum provides legal clarity. Firms now have the correct legal basis for this specific aspect of ICT incident evidence retention.

4. **No Change in Overarching Principle:** This corrigendum does not alter the core principle of retaining incident-related evidence only as long as necessary and in proportion to its criticality, as well as adhering to other Union law retention requirements. The change is purely a correction of a reference to the specific supporting article in another Delegated Regulation.

### CBDC: ECB: Letter from Claudia Buch on implementation of digital euro

The European Central Bank (ECB) published a letter from Claudia Buch, Chair of the Supervisory Board, to MEP Auke Zijlstra, addressing concerns about the digital euro's implications for banks, including potential costs, financial impact, and risks. Ms. Buch outlined several design features of the digital euro intended to safeguard banks from adverse consequences:

- **Individual holding limits:** This would cap the amount of digital euro an individual can hold, preventing large-scale shifts of funds from commercial banks.

- **Non-interest-bearing holdings:** The digital euro would not earn interest, reducing its attractiveness as a savings vehicle compared to traditional bank deposits.

- **No holdings for businesses:** Businesses would not be able to hold digital euros, further limiting their potential to displace commercial bank deposits.

- **Waterfall functionality:** This feature would facilitate the automatic transfer of digital euro holdings exceeding a specific limit into commercial bank accounts, ensuring liquidity remains within the banking system

Regarding potential bank revenues, Ms. Buch noted that the digital euro's legislative proposal includes a compensation model designed to incentivise banks. While initial investment by payment service providers (PSPs) will be necessary for digital euro distribution, efforts are underway to mitigate these costs. The Eurosystem is collaborating with PSPs and other stakeholders to maximise cost efficiency by leveraging existing infrastructures and standards.

Finally, Ms. Buch emphasised that the digital euro is the Eurosystem's response to the rapid digitalisation of the financial system, and its impact should be viewed within this broader, evolving economic landscape.

**Firm Implications for Banks:**

The ECB's letter provides several key implications for banks regarding the digital euro:

- **Mitigated Disintermediation Risk:** The design features, particularly individual holding limits and non-interest-bearing holdings, are intended to prevent a significant outflow of deposits from commercial banks to the digital euro.<sup>6</sup> This suggests the ECB is actively trying to avoid direct competition that would negatively impact bank funding.

- **Operational Investments Required:** Banks, as Payment Service Providers (PSPs), will need to invest in infrastructure and systems to distribute and manage the digital euro.<sup>7</sup> This represents an upfront cost; however, the ECB is working to minimise it by leveraging existing systems.

- **Potential for New Revenue Streams (Compensation Model):** The mention of a compensation model for banks suggests that participating in digital euro distribution could offer new revenue opportunities. The specifics of this model will be crucial in determining the actual financial benefit.

- **Enhanced Role in Digital Payments:** Banks are envisioned as key distributors of the digital euro, which could solidify their role in the evolving digital payments ecosystem, even as a central bank digital currency (CBDC) is introduced.

- **Adaptation to Digitalisation:** The digital euro is presented as a response to broader financial system digitalisation.<sup>9</sup> Banks will need to continue adapting their services and business models to this digital shift, with the digital euro being a significant component.

- **Collaboration with Eurosystem:** The ongoing collaboration between the Eurosystem and PSPs (including banks) in defining the digital euro's creation and distribution implies a continuous dialogue and opportunity for banks to shape its implementation. This suggests a more partnership-oriented approach rather than a top-down mandate.

## Switzerland

### Digital Markets: Stablecoins in Switzerland

This expert report from the Swiss Bankers Association (SBA) provides an overview of the opportunities and risks associated with stablecoins, particularly those issued by Swiss banks, and advocates for framework conditions that maximise their potential benefits for Switzerland.

**Key Points:**

- **Potential of Stablecoins:** Stablecoins are seen as having the potential to strengthen Switzerland's digital economy by offering efficient, potentially low-cost, and secure means of payment. They can streamline payment processes, reduce transaction times and costs, and are suitable for various applications, including payment transactions, trading in digital assets, and future decentralised financial (DeFi) services.

- **Swiss Franc Stablecoin:** The SBA suggests that Switzerland, as a global innovation leader with an intense financial centre, could benefit from a Swiss franc stablecoin. A stablecoin pegged to the Swiss franc is considered crucial for achieving widespread acceptance and realising its innovative potential within the country.

- **Banks as Issuers:** Banks, being regulated and supervised, are well-placed to issue stablecoins. They have a fundamental interest in the strategic options and new use cases emerging in the blockchain-based economy.

- **Desirable Stablecoin Characteristics:** The report emphasises that for broad adoption, stablecoins must be stable against the Swiss franc. This could be achieved through full backing by central bank money (ensuring exchange at par) or partial backing by high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) or commercial bank money. However, the latter would introduce some market risk. Confidence also relies on precise legal/regulatory requirements and customer protection in case of issuer bankruptcy. The "No-questions-asked" principle is vital for general acceptance.

- **Opportunities for Banks:**

  - **Income:** Potential for income from interest on underlying assets (if higher than any interest paid to stablecoin users) and commission fees for managing the stablecoin.

  - **Use Cases:** Supporting digitalisation through P2P payments, efficient trading and settlement of digital assets, enabling DeFi services, facilitating internal company money transfers and liquidity management, and serving as a store of value.

  - **Strategic Relevance:** A widely used Swiss franc stablecoin could be a competitive advantage for Swiss export-oriented companies, reduce dependence on the USD market, strengthen Switzerland's position in cross-border asset management, and ensure low interest rates.

- **Risks:**

  - **Disintermediation:** A key concern for banks is that stablecoins held by users instead of bank deposits are not available for refinancing loans. This could lead to higher refinancing costs and impair the transmission of monetary policy. The risk is highest with stablecoins fully backed by central bank money, which could act as a haven during turbulent times.

  - **Issuer Instability:** Stablecoins are subject to solvency and liquidity risks if the value of reserve assets is questioned or if assets cannot be liquidated quickly during a run.

  - **Financial Market Stability:** Large stablecoin volumes could influence asset pricing, collateral valuation, the money market, and monetary policy, especially if there's a run or if foreign users adopt them extensively as a store of value.

  - **Market Structure Risks:** Network effects could lead to monopolies. Data monopolies could arise from user profiling.

  - **Money Laundering:** Increased reputational risks in areas like money laundering, terrorist financing, and sanctions circumvention. Compliance with due diligence obligations (verifying identity, beneficial owners, "travel rule") is essential.

- **Regulatory Landscape:** The report notes ongoing international and national discussions on regulatory frameworks. It highlights the SIF's (State Secretariat for International Financial Matters) work on a bill for innovative financial business models, which is expected to include a license for payment service providers applicable to stablecoin issuers. The SBA emphasises that such regulation should not disadvantage banks that are more stringently supervised.

- **Inaction Risk:** Given dynamic international developments, inaction in Switzerland regarding stablecoins could increase the strategic dependence of the financial sector and monetary policy on other countries.

The SBA concludes that while stablecoins offer significant opportunities, careful consideration and clear regulatory measures are needed to manage risks. The SBA continues to advocate for a framework that shapes different payment types to maximise prosperity.

**Firm Implications**

1. **For Swiss Banks:**

    - **Opportunity for New Business Models:** The report encourages banks to explore issuing CHF stablecoins, which could generate new revenue streams (interest arbitrage, fees) and enable participation in emerging digital asset ecosystems and DeFi.

    - **Strategic Positioning:** Issuing a CHF stablecoin could be a strategic move to retain customers, offer innovative services, and play a central role in the evolving digital financial landscape in Switzerland.

    - **Disintermediation Risk Management:** Banks considering or facing the rise of stablecoins must strategise how to manage the risk of deposit outflows. This might involve offering their stablecoins, enhancing the attractiveness of traditional deposits, or adapting lending models.

    - **Compliance and Operational Costs:** If banks issue stablecoins, they will face significant compliance burdens related to reserve management, AML/CFT, customer protection, and new regulatory reporting requirements. Operational adjustments for managing DLT-based assets will also be necessary.

    - **Collaboration and PoCs:** Banks may need to collaborate on standards and participate in initiatives, such as the SBA's proof of concept for "deposit tokens," to understand the implications of tokenised money.

2. **For Non-Bank Financial Institutions & Fintechs:**

    - **Potential Competition:** While banks are seen as well-placed issuers, the evolving regulatory framework (e.g., new payment service provider license) might also open avenues for non-bank entities, leading to a competitive landscape.

    - **Partnership Opportunities:** Fintechs specialising in blockchain technology, smart contracts, or digital wallets may find partnership opportunities with banks that are looking to issue stablecoins.

3. **For the Swiss Financial Centre:**

    - **Enhancing Competitiveness:** The development of a robust, well-regulated CHF stablecoin ecosystem could strengthen Switzerland's position as a leading financial and innovation hub, particularly in digital finance and asset management.

    - **Attracting Investment:** A trusted CHF stablecoin might attract international capital, further bolstering the Swiss franc's role as a reserve currency.

    - **Regulatory Clarity is Key:** The success of stablecoins in Switzerland will heavily depend on a clear, supportive, yet prudent regulatory framework that addresses risks without stifling innovation. The anticipated financial market legislation will be critical.

4. **For Businesses Operating in Switzerland:**

    - **Improved Payment Efficiency:** Access to CHF stablecoins could offer businesses more efficient and potentially cheaper payment solutions, especially for cross-border transactions and automated payments in DLT ecosystems (e.g., IoT, supply chain finance).

    - **New Financial Products:** The integration of stablecoins could lead to new financial products and services, particularly in trade finance and liquidity management.

5. **For Regulators (FINMA, SNB):**

    - **Balancing Innovation and Risk:** Regulators face the challenge of fostering innovation while mitigating systemic risks, ensuring financial stability, and maintaining the effectiveness of monetary policy.

    - **Framework Development:** The report underscores the need for ongoing development of the regulatory framework, including clarifying issues around bankruptcy protection for stablecoin holders and access to central bank facilities for issuers. FINMA's existing guidance and future legislation will shape this landscape.

    - **Monitoring Foreign Stablecoins:** Even if Swiss banks are cautious, regulators must monitor the impact of foreign-issued stablecoins on the Swiss economy and financial system.

In conclusion, the SBA report presents a picture of cautious optimism, highlighting that Swiss banks are well-positioned to leverage stablecoins for innovation and efficiency, but this must be done within a robust regulatory framework that addresses significant risks, such as disintermediation and financial stability. Inaction is presented as a strategic risk in its own right.

## United Kingdom

### Digital Markets: FCA consults on prudential regime for cryptoasset firms

This Consultation Paper (CP25/15) from the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), published in May 2025, outlines proposals for a new prudential regime for UK firms undertaking specific cryptoasset activities. It primarily focuses on firms issuing "qualifying stablecoins" (those aiming to maintain value against a fiat currency) and firms safeguarding qualifying cryptoassets.

The consultation period for feedback on these proposals closes on **31 July 2025**.

This paper is part of a broader FCA cryptoasset roadmap and should be read in conjunction with CP25/14 (Stablecoins and Custody CP). The FCA aims to create a predictable and proportionate regulatory environment that supports innovation and growth in the UK cryptoasset sector while ensuring consumer protection and market integrity.

**Key Proposals Summarised:**

1. **New Prudential Framework (COREPRU & CRYPTOPRU):**

    - The FCA is introducing an integrated prudential sourcebook, **COREPRU**, which will contain core prudential requirements and standards applicable to different types of FCA-regulated firms. Cryptoasset firms will be the first to be subject to COREPRU.

    - Sector-specific requirements for cryptoasset firms will be detailed in a new sourcebook called **CRYPTOPRU**. Firms subject to these rules are referred to as "CRYPTOPRU firms.”

    - This CP covers specific elements of COREPRU and CRYPTOPRU, with further aspects to be consulted on later (in "CP2").

2. **Own Funds – Definition and Composition (COREPRU 3):**

    - **Capital Tiers:** Own funds will comprise Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1), Additional Tier 1 (AT1), and Tier 2 (T2) capital, similar to MIFIDPRU firms.

    - **Deductions:** Standard deductions include intangible assets and deferred tax assets. Notably, cryptoassets issued by the firm itself or by a connected party, or where the firm controls the supply, must be deducted from the Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) capital. However, regulated stablecoins backed according to FCA requirements will not require deduction.

    - **Prior Permissions:** FCA prior permission is required to issue CET1 capital instruments (unless substantially the same as previously approved and FCA is notified) and for any reduction in own funds instruments.

    - **Composition Requirements:** Firms must meet their total own funds requirement with specific minimum proportions:

      - CET1≥56%

      - CET1+AT1≥75%

      - CET1+AT1+T2≥100%

3. **Own Funds Requirements (OFR) (COREPRU 4 & CRYPTOPRU 4):**

    - The OFR is the highest of:

      - **Permanent Minimum Requirement (PMR)** (CRYPTOPRU 4.2)

        - Issuing qualifying stablecoins: £350,000

        - Safeguarding qualifying cryptoassets: £150,000

        - If a firm undertakes multiple activities, the highest PMR applies.

      - **Fixed Overheads Requirement (FOR)** (COREPRU 4.3)

        - Calculated as one quarter of the firm's relevant expenditure in the previous year.

      - **K-Factor Requirement (KFR)** (CRYPTOPRU 4.3-4.5)

        - **K-SII (Qualifying Stablecoin in Issuance):** 2% of the average qualifying stablecoins in issuance (SII). Average SII is calculated based on daily values over the previous 9 months, excluding the most recent 3 months.

        - **K-QCS (Qualifying Cryptoassets Safeguarded):** 0.04% of the average qualifying cryptoassets safeguarded (QCS). Average QCS is calculated similarly to average SII. QCS includes assets where safeguarding is outsourced to a third party.

4. **Liquid Assets Requirement (COREPRU 6 & CRYPTOPRU 6):**

    - **Basic Liquid Assets Requirement (BLAR)** (COREPRU 6.2): Applies to all CRYPTOPRU firms.

      - Equal to one-third of the FOR, plus 1.6% of the total amount of any guarantees provided to clients.

      - Must be met with "core liquid assets" (e.g., cash, short-term UK bank deposits, UK government debt, units in short-term MMFs).

    - **Issuer Liquid Asset Requirement (ILAR)** (CRYPTOPRU 6.1): Applies only to qualifying stablecoin issuers, in addition to BLAR.

      - Designed to cover price volatility in the backing asset pool, enabling issuers to top up the pool promptly (T+1).

      - Must be met with on-demand deposits denominated in the reference currency of the stablecoin(s).

      - Calculated by applying specific charges (haircuts) to non-cash assets in the backing pool (e.g., government debt), based on asset type, residual maturity, and coupon (detailed in a table in CRYPTOPRU 6.1.11R). For PDCNAV MMFs, firms should look through to the underlying assets or apply a 5% charge.

5. **Concentration Risk (COREPRU 5 & CRYPTOPRU 5):**

    - All CRYPTOPRU firms must monitor and control concentration risk arising from various sources, including assets, client money, custody asset locations, own cash deposits, earnings sources, and, for issuers, the backing assets of qualifying stablecoins.

6. **Interaction with other regimes:**

    - For firms also subject to MIFIDPRU, the PMR will be the highest applicable, the FOR will be consistent, and the KFR will be the sum of K-factors across sourcebooks.

**Implications for Firms:**

- **Increased Regulatory Scrutiny and Compliance Costs:** Firms will need to understand and implement new requirements under COREPRU and CRYPTOPRU, establish new policies, systems, and controls, and prepare for more intensive FCA supervision. This will likely involve a significant investment in compliance resources and expertise.

- **Higher Capital Requirements:**

  - Firms will need to assess their capital adequacy against the new OFR (PMR, FOR, KFR). This may necessitate raising additional capital, particularly for firms with significant fixed overheads, large volumes of stablecoins in issuance, or substantial assets under custody.

  - The deduction for self-issued or controlled cryptoassets (unless regulated stablecoins) could significantly impact the capital position of some firms.

- **Enhanced Liquidity Management:**

  - All in-scope firms must meet the BLAR, requiring a buffer of highly liquid assets.

  - Stablecoin issuers face an additional ILAR, potentially requiring substantial holdings of on-demand cash, which could impact the yield generated from backing assets and overall business models. They must also manage potential foreign exchange risk if the ILAR is held in a currency different from their functional currency.

- **Operational Adjustments:**

  - Firms will need robust systems for calculating and monitoring their FOR, K-factors (SII and QCS, including 9-month averaging ), BLAR, and ILAR.

  - Daily valuation of QCS (using market value or best-efforts fair value if market value unavailable) will be required for K-QCS calculations.

  - Establishing and maintaining sound administrative and accounting procedures for monitoring concentration risk across various exposures will be essential.

- **Strategic Business Model Considerations:**

  - The choice of backing assets for stablecoins will directly influence the ILAR; riskier or longer-dated assets will attract higher charges.

  - The requirement to include cryptoassets safeguarded by third parties in the K-QCS calculation means firms cannot reduce their capital burden by outsourcing custody.

  - The overall financial commitments may influence market entry decisions and the types of services firms choose to offer in the UK.

- **Need for Advance Planning:** Firms will need to prepare for these changes well in advance of the regime's go-live date, expected in Q4 2025 or Q1 2026, with the gateway opening in Q3 2025. However, all timelines are subject to change. This includes understanding the detailed rules in the draft Handbook text (Appendices in the CP).

Firms are encouraged to provide detailed feedback on the proposals, including the draft rules and the Cost Benefit Analysis, by the **31 July 2025** deadline.

### Digital Markets: FCA consults on stablecoin issuance and cryptoasset custody rules

This Consultation Paper (CP25/14) from the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), published in May 2025, details proposed rules and guidance for the activities of issuing "qualifying stablecoins" and safeguarding "qualifying cryptoassets" in the UK. It follows Discussion Paper DP23/4 and is published alongside CP25/15, which covers prudential requirements for these firms. The consultation period for CP25/14 ends on **31 July 2025**.

The proposals aim to support innovation and UK competitiveness while providing consumer protection and market integrity. The rules focus on single fiat currency-referenced stablecoins.

**Key Proposals for Qualifying Stablecoin Issuers (Chapter 3):**

- **Creating a Qualifying Stablecoin:**

  - Issuers must understand and manage risks associated with the design and build of a qualifying stablecoin before it is issued. This includes analysing the risks associated with the underlying Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT).

- **Backing Assets (CASS 16):**

  - **1:1 Backing & Trust:** Qualifying stablecoins must be fully backed at all times by secure, liquid assets held in a statutory trust for the benefit of stablecoin holders, with the issuer as trustee. All minted qualifying stablecoins, including those held by the issuer, must be fully backed.

  - **Safeguarding of Backing Assets:** Backing assets must be held with an independent third-party custodian not connected with the issuer's group.

  - **Composition:**

    - **Core Backing Assets:** Default assets are on-demand deposits and short-term government debt instruments (maturing in one year or less).

    - **Expanded Backing Assets:** Issuers can include longer-term government debt, units in Public Debt Constant Net Asset Value Money Market Funds (PDCNAV MMFs), and assets from repurchase/reverse repurchase agreements, provided they notify the FCA and meet additional requirements for skills, risk management, and the Backing Asset Composition Ratio (BACR).

    - **On-Demand Deposit Requirement (ODDR):** A minimum of 5% of the backing pool must be held in on-demand bank deposits.

    - **Backing Asset Composition Ratio (BACR):** Issuers using expanded assets must calculate a BACR, representing the minimum proportion of core backing assets needed, based on peak estimated daily redemptions and historical accuracy of these estimations. Specific rules apply to issuers with less than 180 days of redemption data.

  - **Denomination:** Backing assets must be in the same denomination as the reference currency of the qualifying stablecoin.

  - **Remuneration:** Issuers can retain revenue/interest from backing assets, but cannot pass it to stablecoin holders.

  - **Reconciliations & Discrepancies (CASS 16.4):** At least daily reconciliations of backing assets are required. Shortfalls must be topped up (from issuer's resources or unallocated backing funds) or excess stablecoins burned; excesses in backing assets must be removed to the issuer's account (or more stablecoins minted) promptly (max one business day).

- **Redemption (CRYPTO 2.4):**

  - **Right to Redeem:** Issuers must offer redemption at par value to all holders, with no minimum redemption amount. Payment orders for redeemed funds must be placed by the end of the business day following a valid redemption request.

  - **Fees:** Redemption fees must be commensurate with operational costs, not exceed the value of stablecoins being redeemed, and not pass on losses from selling backing assets.

  - **Contractual Rights:** A contract must exist between the issuer and holder stating redemption conditions. Contractual obligations must transfer with the stablecoin to new holders.

  - **Suspension:** Redemption can only be suspended in exceptional circumstances threatening the stablecoin's integrity or holders' interests, after reasonable steps to avoid suspension are taken. The FCA must be notified immediately.

  - **AML/CTF:** Issuers must comply with financial crime legislation, including Customer Due Diligence (CDD) for redemptions. The redemption timeframe starts after CDD checks are completed.

- **Use of Third Parties (CRYPTO 2.3):**

  - The issuer remains responsible for compliance when using third parties.

  - Due diligence, comprehensive contracts with information-sharing provisions, and annual reviews are essential.

  - Funds received for issuing qualifying stablecoins must be paid directly to the issuer, not via a third party acting on their behalf.

  - The decision to suspend redemptions is reserved for the issuer.

- **Disclosures (CRYPTO 2.5):**

  - Information must be published online, be transparent, fair, and not misleading.

  - **Updated at least quarterly:** Total number of stablecoins minted and issued; value and percentage breakdown of backing assets; confirmation of 1:1 backing.

  - **Updated whenever inaccurate:** Description of technology used, redemption process, and potential fees; names of third parties involved in issuance or holding backing assets.

  - **Independent Review:** At least annually, an independent, qualified person must review the accuracy of published statements regarding the 1:1 backing. The outcome must be publicly disclosed.

**Key Proposals for Qualifying Cryptoasset Custodians (Chapter 4 & CASS 17):**

- **Regulatory Approach:** Based on the existing Client Assets Sourcebook (CASS) framework, adapted for the unique characteristics of cryptoassets. The focus is on safeguarding qualifying cryptoassets when a firm holds them on behalf of clients, rather than holding them in self-custody.

- **Segregation of Client Assets (CASS 17.3):**

  - Client-qualifying cryptoassets must be segregated from the firm's assets.

  - Permits the use of both individually segregated and omnibus wallets.

- **Trust Requirement (CASS 17.3):**

  - Clients' qualifying cryptoassets must be held in a non-statutory trust by the custodian, with the custodian acting as a bare trustee.

- **Record-Keeping (CASS 17.5):**

  - Firms must maintain accurate, client-specific records of qualifying cryptoassets, detailing the type, quantity, blockchain address of each asset, the nature of the client's claim, and the details of any other parties with control or access to the assets.

  - These records must be maintained independently from the DLT and not supplemented by third-party/on-chain records.

- **Reconciliations (CASS 17.5):**

  - A qualifying cryptoasset reconciliation must be performed at least once daily. This involves checking the firm's internal client-specific records against the content of wallet addresses controlled by the firm and records from any third-party sub-custodians.

  - Procedures for investigating and resolving discrepancies (shortfalls or excesses) are proposed. Firms must notify the FCA if a shortfall is unlikely to be resolved before the following reconciliation or if neither the firm nor a third party is responsible.

- **Organisational Arrangements (CASS 17.2):**

  - Firms must have adequate organisational arrangements, policies, and procedures to minimise the risk of loss or diminution of client qualifying cryptoassets due to misuse, fraud, poor administration, inadequate record-keeping, or negligence.

- **Private Key Management and Security (CASS 17.4):**

  - Adequate organisational controls are required to ensure private keys and means of access are generated, stored, and controlled securely throughout their lifecycle.

  - Accurate and verifiable 'key-mapping' records must be maintained.

  - Strategies to mitigate loss or compromise of access means, including secure back-ups, are required.

- **Liability for Loss (Future Consultation):**

  - No new specific statutory liability standard for loss is proposed in this CP. Contractual terms and existing law will generally determine liability.

  - Firms will be required to clearly outline clients' rights, safeguarding obligations, and liability terms in their contracts.

- **Use of Third Parties (Sub-Custodians) (CASS 17.6):**

  - A firm may appoint a third party to safeguard qualifying cryptoassets if it is in the client's best interest and necessary.

  - The appointing firm must exercise due skill, care, and diligence in selection and ongoing review. This includes considering the third party's expertise, market reputation, and relevant legal and regulatory requirements.

  - A written agreement is required, detailing the services, liability, segregation of assets, and acknowledgement that assets are held on trust by the primary custodian for its clients, as well as consent for any further sub-custody.

  - The firm's governing body must approve such appointments.

- **Client Disclosures, CASS Oversight Officer, Audits, Reporting:** Specific rules for these areas will be consulted on in future CPs, likely the 'Conduct and Firm Standards' CP and 'Trading platforms, intermediation, lending and staking' CP.

**Wider Context:**

- The Treasury does not intend to regulate stablecoins within UK payments at this time.

- The FCA will collaborate with the Bank of England on its regime for systemic payment systems utilising stablecoins. Dual regulation by the Bank and FCA is expected for issuers of systemic stablecoins used for payments.

- Firms currently registered under MLRs for cryptoasset activities will need to be authorised under FSMA for these new regulated activities.

**Implications for Firms:**

- **Significant Compliance Overhaul:** Both issuers and custodians will need to implement substantial changes to meet these detailed requirements, developing new policies, procedures, systems (especially for record-keeping, reconciliation, and DLT risk management), and governance structures.

- **For Stablecoin Issuers:**

  - **Backing Asset Management:** Strict rules on composition, 1:1 backing, statutory trust, ODDR, and potentially BACR will heavily influence business models and investment strategies for backing assets. The prohibition on passing interest to holders is a key constraint.

  - **Redemption:** Ensuring at-par redemption for all holders by the next business day is a significant operational and liquidity challenge. AML/CTF integration is crucial.

  - **Third-Party Reliance:** While permitted, the issuer retains full responsibility, requiring robust due diligence, contractual frameworks, and ongoing monitoring. Direct receipt of issuance funds is a key operational point.

  - **Transparency:** Extensive public disclosure requirements will necessitate systems for regular updates and independent verification.

- **For Cryptoasset Custodians:**

  - **CASS Alignment:** Firms will need to adapt or build systems aligned with CASS principles, specifically for cryptoassets. This includes establishing non-statutory trusts, detailed client-specific record-keeping independent of the blockchain, and daily reconciliations.

  - **Security:** Robust private key management and organisational controls are paramount.

  - **Liability:** Clear contractual terms regarding liability for loss will be critical.

  - **Sub-Custody:** Appointing third-party custodians involves a rigorous due diligence and contractual process.

- **Cost Implications:** Firms will face significant one-off and ongoing costs for familiarisation, legal advice, system development/upgrades, staffing, training, independent reviews/audits, and potentially holding more liquid, lower-yield assets.

- **Strategic Decisions:** Firms must decide whether to operate in the UK under this regime. Stablecoin issuers must carefully select their backing asset strategy. Custodians must decide on wallet structures and trust arrangements.

- **Future Consultations:** Firms need to be aware that further rules (e.g., SMCR, Consumer Duty application, financial crime, operational resilience, market abuse, and specific regulations for vertically integrated firms) are forthcoming and will add to the regulatory landscape.

### Digital Markets: FCA crypto asset discussion paper on trading platforms, intermediaries, staking, lending and borrowing, and DeFi

The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) has published Discussion Paper DP25/1, outlining its current thinking and seeking feedback on potential regulatory approaches for various cryptoasset activities in the UK. This paper follows HM Treasury's announcement of plans to legislate a comprehensive financial regulatory regime for cryptoassets, expanding the FCA's current remit beyond anti-money laundering (MLRS), financial promotions, and consumer protection legislation. The proposed regime, informed by international standards and extensive stakeholder engagement, aims to create a safe, competitive, and sustainable crypto sector in the UK, enabling innovation while protecting consumers and ensuring market integrity. The activities addressed explicitly in the paper, and expected to be brought within the FCA's regulatory perimeter under the new Regulated Activities Order (RAO) Statutory Instrument, include:

- Operating a cryptoasset trading platform (CATP)

- Cryptoasset intermediation (dealing as principal/agent, arranging)<sup>4</sup>

- Cryptoasset lending and borrowing<sup>5</sup>

- Restricting the use of credit to purchase cryptoassets<sup>6</sup>

- Staking

- Decentralised Finance (DeFi), where identifiable controlling entities exist

The FCA acknowledges that, even with regulation, the majority of cryptoassets will remain high-risk, speculative investments, and consumers should be prepared to lose all their money.

Key Proposals and Regulatory Approaches:

The DP sets out desired outcomes and explores potential policy proposals for each activity:

- **Cryptoasset Trading Platforms (CATPS):** Generally require UK authorisation for platforms operating in the UK or serving UK retail clients. Explores allowing overseas firms to operate via UK branches under certain conditions (e.g., affiliated UK subsidiary) to facilitate access to global liquidity while ensuring adequate supervision. Proposes additional rules for retail access, algorithmic trading, and market making. Favours non-discretionary trading systems. Expresses concern about CATP operators dealing as principals on their platform and potential conflicts from off-platform or affiliated principal trading, seeking ways to manage these risks (e.g., functional or legal separation).<sup>8</sup> Proposes pre-trade and post-trade transparency requirements and asks about harmonising on-chain/off-chain data recording <sup>9</sup>

- **Cryptoasset Intermediaries:** Proposes rules aligned with traditional finance where possible, applying to firms dealing as principal/agent or arranging deals.<sup>10</sup> Focuses on order handling and execution (aiming for best execution outcomes, potentially requiring checks across UK authorised CATPS for retail), managing conflicts of interest (requiring functional separation for own account trading, explicitly prohibiting Payment for Order Flow - PFOF), and considering pre- and post-trade transparency.<sup>11</sup> Also considers guidance or rules on client categorisation, particularly regarding retail opting for professional status. Proposes restricting retail access to cryptoassets only admitted to trading on a UK authorised CATP.<sup>12</sup>

- **Cryptoasset Lending and Borrowing:** Identifies significant risks, particularly for retail consumers (loss of ownership, liquidity/counterparty risk, lack of understanding, platform token conflicts, margin calls, lack of creditworthiness assessment). The current intention is to restrict retail access to these products in their existing form, while allowing institutional access. Explores potential mitigation measures if retail access were permitted, such as applying elements of the Consumer Credit sourcebook (CONC) regarding creditworthiness assessments, arrears, and forbearance, restricting automatic collateral top-ups, requiring enhanced consumer understanding measures (key features document, express consent, appropriateness tests), limiting the use of platform tokens, and potentially exempting specific uses of qualifying stablecoins.

- **Restricting the use of credit to purchase cryptoassets:** Notes the increasing use of credit and the risks due to volatility and potential over-indebtedness.<sup>13</sup> Explores restricting firms from accepting credit (like credit cards or e-money credit lines) for crypto purchases, potentially with an exemption for qualifying stablecoins.<sup>14</sup>

- **Staking:** Recognises increasing retail participation. Addresses risks specific to staking, including slashing, third-party dependencies, consumer understanding, and safeguarding against these risks. Proposes requiring firms to absorb retail consumer losses from preventable operational/technological failures, applying the operational resilience framework, obtaining explicit consumer consent, providing a key features document detailing risks and processes (including ownership transfer status, fees, delays), and implementing strict safeguarding requirements (segregation from firm and other clients' assets, accurate record-keeping, regular reconciliations).

- **Decentralised Finance (DeFi):** Affirms the "same risk, same regulatory outcome" principle. The FCA proposes applying the exact requirements for centralised services where DeFi activities involve identifiable controlling person(s) and fall within the RAO perimeter. Plans to develop guidance to help firms assess decentralisation and comply with obligations, engaging with stakeholders on practical constraints and emerging industry practices.<sup>15</sup>

The DP also addresses ESG considerations, noting limited direct relevance but acknowledging crypto's energy consumption and highlighting staking as a less energy-intensive mechanism than proof-of-work. ESG disclosures proposed in a previous DP (DP24/4) are referenced.

A recent YouGov survey commissioned by the FCA indicated that 93% of UK adults have heard of cryptoassets, and 12% (around 7 million adults) own them.<sup>16</sup> Among crypto owners, 27% engaged in staking and 9% in lending/borrowing in the 12 months before August 2024. The survey also found that 20% of owners *believed* they had financial protection, and a significant portion (52% of current users and 31% of non-users) indicated regulation or financial protection would make them more likely to invest. Cryptoasset owners were found to be more likely male, under 35, and to have a higher-than-average household income. The FCA does not foresee these proposals negatively impacting groups with protected characteristics under the Equality Act 2010 or financial inclusion.

Implications for Firms:

The proposals outlined in DP25/1, if implemented, will have significant implications for firms operating or planning to operate in the UK cryptoasset market:

1. **Authorisation Requirements:** Firms conducting or facilitating the listed activities in the UK or serving UK clients (especially retail) will likely need to be authorised by the FCA. Overseas firms may need to establish a UK legal entity or a branch structure in the UK.

2. **Operational and Systems Changes:** Firms must build or adapt systems for order handling (non-discretionary CATPS), best execution (intermediaries), conflict management, transparency, and potentially on-chain/off-chain data recording. Staking firms face specific requirements for segregation, record-keeping, and reconciliation.

3. **Enhanced Conduct Standards:** Compliance with the Consumer Duty will be critical for retail-facing firms. This includes ensuring transparent, fair, and non-misleading communications, providing sufficient information on risks and product features (e.g., key features documents for staking, disclosures for intermediaries), obtaining explicit consent where required (staking, potentially collateral top-ups), and conducting appropriateness assessments.<sup>17</sup>

4. **Stricter Risk Management:** Firms must significantly enhance their risk management frameworks, particularly for conflicts of interest (requiring operational or legal separation in some cases and prohibiting Payment for Order Flow (PFOF), liquidity, counterparty risk, and technological and operational resilience (especially for staking, including potential liability for third-party failures).

5. **Potential Restrictions on Business Models:**

    - Cryptoasset lending and borrowing in their current form may be prohibited for retail consumers. <sup>18</sup> Firms offering these products must assess whether they can meet stringent potential requirements (like CONC elements) or pivot to serving only institutional clients in the UK.

    - Firms may be restricted from accepting credit for crypto purchases (excluding qualifying stablecoins). .<sup>19</sup>

    - Intermediaries may only be able to offer regulated services for retail on cryptoassets admitted to trading on a UK authorised CATP.<sup>20</sup>

    - Firms using platform tokens in lending/borrowing models may face significant restrictions on their use.

6. **Increased Transparency and Reporting Burden:** CATPS and potentially intermediaries must implement pre- and post-trade transparency systems to enhance transparency and reduce reporting burdens. Firms across activities must maintain detailed transaction records for up to 5 years.

7. **DeFi Considerations:** Firms involved in DeFi must assess their activities against the "identifiable controlling person" criterion and understand that regulated activities conducted via automated or decentralised mechanisms will still be subject to the same rules as their centralised counterparts.

8. **Need for Engagement:** Firms are strongly encouraged to review the DP, consider the detailed questions posed, and provide feedback to the FCA by **13 June 2025**.<sup>21</sup>. This feedback will directly inform the development of future Consultation Papers and final rules. Engagement through the FCA's planned roundtables and meetings is also encouraged.

### Digital Markets: FMLC letter to HM Treasury highlighting legal uncertainties relating to draft cryptoassets regulated activities

The Financial Markets Law Committee (FMLC) acknowledges HM Treasury's Draft Statutory Instrument (SI) as a vital step towards regulating cryptoassets in the UK. However, the FMLC identifies several provisions that, in their current form, would create **significant legal uncertainty and potentially unintended negative consequences**, including criminal liability, for financial market participants. The letter addresses these concerns and proposes solutions or areas that require further guidance.

**Key Issues Highlighted by the FMLC:**

1. **Circular Definitions (Stablecoin vs. Electronic Money):** The definitions of "qualifying stablecoin" and "electronic money" are circular and create uncertainty. It's unclear whether many existing stablecoins would fall under one category or the other, which has significant commercial and regulatory consequences.

    - **Proposal:** Amend the definitions so that the "qualifying stablecoin" definition prevails if a cryptoasset meets its criteria.

2. **Overly Broad Safeguarding Activity:** The new safeguarding activity for cryptoassets is broader than the existing regime for traditional investments. It could inadvertently capture entities that do not hold title but have control (e.g., those receiving cryptocurrency as collateral or with security interests), disrupting current market practices.

    - **Proposal:** Either integrate cryptoassets into the existing RAO Article 40 safeguarding and administration activity, or narrow the new activity to "holding legal or beneficial title."

3. **Safeguarding Inadvertently Covering Traditional Securities:** The broad definition of "cryptoasset" (any cryptographically secured digital representation) could mean that traditional securities recorded on electronic registers (like CREST) might unintentionally fall under the new cryptoasset safeguarding rules.

    - **Proposal:** Amend FSMA to clarify that using cryptographically secured records for traditional securities doesn't make them cryptoassets.

4. **Issues with Cryptographically Secured Records (General):** The broad cryptoasset definition and unclear requirements for "transferability" and "fungibility" for qualifying cryptoassets could bring unintended records (e.g., a cryptographically secured record of ownership for physical assets) into scope.

    - **Proposal:** Remove the definition of "transferable" and provide detailed FCA guidance on the scope of qualifying cryptoassets, especially regarding records of extrinsic assets.

5. **Unclear Territorial Scope for Overseas Firms:** It's not clear that the policy intent (that overseas cryptoasset firms serving only UK institutional clients, not consumers, won't need UK authorisation) is achieved. Section 418 FSMA amendments alone are insufficient without explicit RAO exclusions.

    - **Proposal:** Extend RAO Article 72 (overseas persons exclusion) to relevant cryptoasset activities with UK institutional clients or create new exclusions.

**Other Significant Concerns:**

- **Meaning of "established in the UK":** Lack of a clear definition creates uncertainty for the activity of "issuing qualifying stablecoin."

- **Offering/Arranging Stablecoins:** Treating "offering" as a regulated activity is inconsistent with the usual RAO/FPO distinction (where offering is typically a financial promotion).

- **Redeeming Stablecoins:** Unclear scope of "redeeming" and its interaction with dealing activities for overseas issuers.

- **Maintaining Stablecoin Value:** The activity of "activities designed to maintain the value" is too broad and could capture banks, custodians, and investment advisors already authorised.

- **UK Subsidiaries of Non-UK Issuers:** Uncertainty about what activities a UK subsidiary can undertake before being deemed a "creator" and falling into the regulatory perimeter.

- **Reference to "Fiat Currency":** Unclear meaning of "referencing a fiat currency" for qualifying stablecoins.

- **Exclusion for "Creation/Minting":** Inconsistently applied (only for stablecoins, not all qualifying cryptoassets).

- **Inconsistent Safeguarding Exclusions:** Existing exclusions for traditional safeguarding (nominees, trustees) are not consistently mirrored for cryptoasset safeguarding.

- **"Relevant Specified Investment Cryptoassets":** Custodians of cryptoassets that are already securities/contractually based investments (under RAO Art 40) might unnecessarily fall under new, different rules.

- **Qualifying Cryptoasset Staking:** "Making arrangements" for staking is too broad and could capture layer one protocols and node operators.

- **Transferability & Fungibility Definitions:** The proposed definition of "transferable" is unworkable, and "fungible" lacks clarity.

- **Safeguarding/Staking Territoriality (Intermediaries):** New FSMA section 418(6C) could make overseas custodians/staking providers subject to UK regulation if UK consumers are involved, even indirectly through multiple intermediaries, disrupting global custody models.

**Firm Implications**

The FMLC's letter outlines critical flaws in the Draft SI that, if not addressed, will have firm and largely negative implications:

1. **Significant Legal and Operational Risk for Market Participants:** The pervasive legal uncertainties identified will make it extremely difficult for businesses (both UK-based and overseas) to determine if their activities are regulated, how they are regulated, and what compliance measures are necessary. This increases the risk of inadvertent non-compliance.

2. **Risk of Criminal Liability:** As breach of the FSMA general prohibition can be a criminal offence, legal uncertainty in the RAO directly translates to a risk of unintentional criminal liability for firms attempting to operate within the UK cryptoasset market.

3. **Stifling Innovation and UK Competitiveness:** Instead of creating a "sophisticated and competitive regulatory framework," the current draft could deter businesses from operating or innovating in the UK due to the high legal risks and compliance burdens stemming from ambiguity. This undermines the stated policy goal.

4. **Policy Intentions Not Met:** The FMLC indicates that several policy intentions described in the Policy Note (e.g., regarding territorial scope for institutional business) are not effectively translated into the legal drafting of the SI.

5. **Increased Litigation Risk:** Ambiguity in regulations often leads to disputes and litigation, for instance, regarding the enforceability of contracts if one party is later found to be operating in breach of the general prohibition.

6. **Operational Disruptions to Existing Practices:** The broad definitions and scope of new activities (especially safeguarding) threaten to disrupt established market practices, including collateral arrangements, global custody models for traditional assets, if they touch cryptographic systems, and the operations of ancillary service providers (banks, investment advisors) to stablecoin issuers.

7. **Regulatory Burden and Cost:** Firms will incur significant costs seeking legal advice to navigate the uncertainties. If the rules are applied too broadly, many firms performing activities do not core to the intended regulatory focus might be unnecessarily brought into scope.

8. **HM Treasury Must Undertake Significant Revisions:** The letter implies that minor tweaks will be insufficient. HM Treasury should seriously consider the FMLC's detailed proposals for revising key definitions and provisions, as well as providing extensive guidance from the FCA.

9. **Challenge for the FCA:** If the SI is implemented as is, the FCA will face enormous challenges in supervising and enforcing a framework riddled with uncertainties, potentially leading to inconsistent application and market confusion.

In essence, the FMLC assesses that while the goal of regulating cryptoassets is welcome, the current Draft SI is not fit for purpose and risks creating more problems than it solves without substantial amendments to enhance legal certainty and align with stated policy objectives.

## Hong Kong

### Stablecoins Bill passed by LegCo, with a new licensing regime expected to come into effect in 2025 following consultation on and finalisation of detailed regulatory requirements.

Hong Kong's Legislative Council (LegCo) passed the Stablecoins Bill on May 21, 2025, establishing a new licensing regime for fiat-referenced stablecoin (FRS) issuers. This framework is expected to take effect in 2025, following further consultations on detailed regulatory requirements by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA). Under the new Stablecoins Ordinance, any entity issuing an FRS in Hong Kong, or issuing an FRS pegged to the HKD (whether in or outside Hong Kong), will be required to obtain a license from the HKMA. Licensed issuers must adhere to stringent requirements, including:

- **Reserve Asset Management and Redemption:** Proper segregation of client assets, robust stabilisation mechanisms, and the ability to redeem stablecoins at par value under reasonable conditions. Reserve assets must be high-quality, highly liquid, and fully back the stablecoins at all times.

- **Compliance:** Adherence to anti-money laundering (AML) and counter-terrorist financing (CTF) regulations, comprehensive risk management, regular disclosure and auditing, and meeting "fitness and propriety" standards.

- **Offering Restrictions:** Only specified licensed institutions can offer FRS in Hong Kong, and only FRS issued by licensed issuers can be provided to retail investors. Unlicensed FRS advertising is prohibited to prevent fraud.

The HKMA will conduct further consultations on the detailed implementation, and a transitional arrangement will be in place to assist the industry in complying with the new requirements. Following the stablecoin regime, Hong Kong plans to consult on regulations for virtual asset over-the-counter (OTC) services and custodian services, and issue its second policy statement on the development of virtual assets.

**What it means for firms:**

The passage of the Stablecoins Bill has significant implications for firms operating or looking to operate in the stablecoin space in Hong Kong:

- **Mandatory Licensing for Issuers:** Any firm currently issuing FRS in Hong Kong or issuing HKD-pegged FRS globally will *need to apply for and obtain a license* from the HKMA. This represents a new and essential hurdle for market participation.

- **Increased Compliance Burden:** Firms will face a substantial increase in compliance requirements across various areas, including stringent rules on reserve management, asset segregation, redemption processes, AML/CTF, risk management, and disclosure. This necessitates significant investment in internal controls, systems, and personnel.

- **Operational Overhaul:** Existing stablecoin issuers will likely need to overhaul their operational frameworks to meet the new standards for reserve backing (1:1), asset quality, and redemption mechanisms. This includes ensuring proper segregation of client assets from company funds.

- **Enhanced Transparency and Reporting:** Regular public disclosure of reserve assets, their composition, and market value will be mandatory. Firms will also be subject to frequent reporting and auditing requirements by the HKMA.

- **Restricted Market Access for Unlicensed Entities:** Unlicensed firms will be unable to issue or offer FRS in Hong Kong, and crucially, retail investors can only access FRS from licensed issuers. This creates a regulated market where compliance is key to reaching a broader customer base.

- **Focus on Consumer Protection:** The emphasis on protecting retail investors through licensing, clear disclosure, and restrictions on advertising for unlicensed FRS means firms must prioritise robust consumer safeguards.

- **Opportunity for Licensed Firms:** For firms that successfully navigate the licensing process and comply with the regulations, there's an opportunity to gain market trust and operate in a clearly defined and stable regulatory environment, potentially attracting more mainstream adoption of their FRS products.

- **Ongoing Regulatory Evolution:** This bill is part of a broader regulatory push in Hong Kong. Firms in the virtual asset space should anticipate further regulations for OTC services and custodian services, requiring continuous adaptation and proactive engagement with upcoming consultations.

- **Global Alignment:** Hong Kong's approach aligns with international regulatory trends for stablecoins, emphasising full reserve backing, investor protection, and anti-money laundering/countering the financing of terrorism (AML/CFT), which could facilitate cross-border interoperability for compliant firms.

### Digital Markets: CRS - Stablecoin Legislation: An Overview of S. 1582, GENIUS Act of 2025

This CRS Insight provides an overview of S. 1582, the Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act of 2025 (GENIUS Act). On May 8, 2025, the Senate did not invoke cloture to proceed with the bill, meaning it did not advance at that time. The bill aims to establish a regulatory framework for stablecoins.

**Key Provisions of S. 1582:**

- **Definition of Payment Stablecoin:** A digital asset issued for payment/settlement, redeemable at a predetermined fixed amount (e.g., \$1).

- **Reserve Requirements:** Issuers must hold at least 1:1 reserves for outstanding stablecoins. Permitted reserves include coins, currency, insured deposits, short-dated Treasury bills, Treasury-backed repos/reverse repos, government money market funds, central bank reserves, and similar government-issued assets approved by regulators. Use of reserves is restricted.

- **Regulatory Standards:** Federal and state regulators would issue tailored capital, liquidity, and risk management rules for issuers. Stablecoin issuers are exempt from traditional bank regulatory capital standards.

- **Redemption and Reporting:** Issuers must establish and disclose redemption procedures. Periodic reports on outstanding stablecoins and reserve composition, certified by executives and "examined" by public accounting firms, are required. Issuers with over \$50 billion in stablecoins are required to have audited annual financial statements.

- **AML/Sanctions:** Issuers are subject to the Bank Secrecy Act, with FinCEN to write tailored AML rules and facilitate "novel methods ... to detect illicit activity involving digital assets". Issuers must certify AML and sanctions compliance programs. Individuals convicted of certain financial crimes are prohibited from being officers/directors.

- **Issuer Eligibility & Registration:** Banks, credit unions (via their subsidiaries), and non-banks (excluding financial firms) can issue stablecoins, but they must register with the relevant federal banking regulator. Applications are evaluated on meeting baseline requirements; deemed approved if not acted upon in 120 days.

- **Dual Regulatory Regime (Federal/State):**

  - **Federal Regime:** Issuers opting for federal regulation or with \>\$10 billion in issuance are supervised by their primary federal banking regulator (or OCC for nonbanks). Supervision includes financial condition, systemic risk, and risk management.

  - **State Regime:** Nonbank issuers with \<\$10 billion in stablecoins can opt for state regulation if the state regime is deemed "substantially similar" to the federal one by the Treasury, Fed, and FDIC. State regulators have primary oversight but can cede to the Fed; Fed/OCC can intervene in emergencies.

- **Foreign Issuers:** Offering stablecoins to U.S. persons is restricted to permitted issuers within three years of enactment. Treasury can establish reciprocal agreements with "comparable" foreign jurisdictions. Qualifying foreign stablecoins (with freeze capacity, OCC registration, and U.S. reserves for U.S. redemptions) could be traded and used in the United States. The Treasury can waive requirements.

- **Other Provisions:** Rules for custodians (must be regulated entities, no commingling with exceptions). Banks can custody stablecoins and reserves, utilise blockchains, and issue tokenised deposits. Stablecoin holders get priority in bankruptcy. Payment stablecoins are clarified as neither securities nor commodities and are not federally insured. Existing ethics laws would prohibit senior executive officials from issuing stablecoins.

**Firm Implications**

The Senate's failure to advance S. 1582 means the proposed comprehensive federal framework for stablecoins is not moving forward at this time. However, the bill's content outlines what a future regulatory regime might entail, and its consideration has implications:

1. **Continued Regulatory Uncertainty:** Without federal legislation like S. 1582, the regulatory landscape for stablecoin issuers in the U.S. remains fragmented and uncertain, relying on existing state-level regulations and interpretations by various federal agencies. This makes long-term planning difficult for firms.

2. **Guidance for Future Compliance (if similar legislation passes):**

    - **Potential Issuers (Banks, Nonbanks):** If similar legislation is enacted, firms would face significant compliance burdens, including strict 1:1 reserve requirements with specific asset limitations, tailored capital and liquidity rules, robust AML/sanctions programs, and detailed reporting and auditing obligations. The dual federal/state regime offers options but also complexity, particularly around the \$10 billion issuance threshold.

    - **Accounting and Auditing Firms:** The bill's requirements for examination of reserve reports and audits for large issuers would create new business lines and necessitate specialised expertise in digital assets.

    - **Custodians:** The bill establishes rules for stablecoin assets and reserve custodians, requiring them to be regulated entities and adhere to regulations like no commingling of funds (with exceptions). This would formalise the role and responsibilities of digital asset custodians.

    - **Traditional Banks:** The bill explicitly permits banks to custody stablecoins and reserves, use blockchains, and issue tokenised deposits, potentially opening new avenues for innovation and service offerings within a regulated framework.

3. **Impact on Foreign Issuers:** The provisions for foreign issuers, including the three-year timeline for compliance, reciprocal agreements, and OCC registration for U.S. operations, indicate a move towards bringing significant offshore activity under U.S. regulatory purview or restricting access to the U.S. market.

4. **Status Quo for AML/BSA:** While S. 1582 would have mandated FinCEN to write tailored AML rules, existing BSA obligations already apply to many digital asset activities. Firms must continue to navigate current AML requirements. The call for "novel methods" to detect illicit activity suggests an expectation of enhanced technological capabilities.

5. **Innovation and Market Structure:** The bill's attempt to clarify that payment stablecoins are not securities or commodities could reduce specific regulatory conflicts and encourage development. However, the overall regulatory burden may favour larger, well-capitalised entities. The 120-day deemed approval for applications could expedite entry for compliant firms if enacted in future bills.

6. **Political and Legislative Outlook:** The failure to invoke cloture suggests significant hurdles remain for comprehensive stablecoin legislation. Firms will need to monitor ongoing legislative efforts and adapt to any incremental regulatory changes from individual agencies.

### CBDC: CRS - Central Bank Digital Currencies

The Congressional Research Service (CRS) report examines the ongoing debate about whether the Federal Reserve (Fed) should create a Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC), also known as a "digital dollar." While the U.S. already has widespread digital payments, accelerated by the FedNow real-time settlement system (launched 2023), a CBDC is seen by proponents as a way to leverage crypto-pioneered technology for a more efficient, central-bank-backed digital payment system, potentially overcoming the volatility and transaction cost issues that have hindered cryptocurrencies and stablecoins from broad payment adoption.

**Key Developments and Stances:**

- **Federal Reserve:** In its January 2022 report, the Fed defined a CBDC as "a digital liability of a central bank that is widely available to the general public." It outlined desired characteristics: privacy-protected (within legal limits), intermediated (services via financial institutions), widely transferable, and identity-verified (not anonymous). The Fed stated it "does not intend to proceed with issuance of a CBDC without clear support from the executive branch and Congress, ideally in the form of a specific authorising law" and has taken no position on its ultimate desirability. The Fed has engaged in pilot programs (Projects Hamilton, Cedar, and Agora) for technical research.

- **Executive Branch:** President Biden's March 2022 Executive Order 14067 prioritised the assessment of a CBDC. However, President Trump's January 2025 Executive Order 14178 prohibited federal agencies from "undertaking any action to establish, issue, or promote a CBDC" and mandated the termination of any related plans.

- **International Context:** As of February 2025, 134 jurisdictions are exploring CBDCs.Some smaller nations (Bahamas, Jamaica, Nigeria) have launched them. China is making significant progress with its digital yuan (e-CNY) pilot. Six advanced economies, including the Eurozone, the UK, and Switzerland, are also in research or pilot phases. Motivations vary from payment efficiency and financial inclusion to increasing economic control, surveillance, and reducing reliance on the U.S. dollar and vulnerability to sanctions.

- **Design Considerations:** A U.S. CBDC's features are unresolved. Proposals range from a wholesale CBDC (banks-only, minor change) to retail CBDC accounts held directly at the Fed (fundamental change). The Fed’s report envisioned an intermediated model. A CBDC could potentially compete with cryptocurrencies, stablecoins, foreign central bank digital currencies (CBDCs), private payment platforms, or traditional banks.

**Issues for Congress:**

- **Necessity and Efficiency:** Unclear if a CBDC would outperform existing U.S. payment systems at a lower cost. Cross-border CBDC use offers potential gains but faces more challenges.

- **Displacement of Private Sector:** A retail CBDC could displace private payment systems and banks, potentially affecting credit availability and raising questions about government provision of private goods. It could also diminish the role of stablecoins.

- **Financial Stability:** Debated whether a CBDC would mitigate or exacerbate bank runs.

- **Privacy vs. Illicit Finance:** Anonymity is seen as incompatible with AML/CFT policies, necessitating user data collection.

- **Fed's Role and Risks:** Implementing a CBDC would expose the Fed to operational, reputational, and political risks, potentially impacting its independence.

- **Monetary Policy:** Proponents argue that a CBDC could improve monetary policy transmission, including potentially enabling negative interest rates if cash is displaced.

- **International Implications:** Foreign CBDC initiatives could challenge the U.S. dollar's global dominance and the effectiveness of U.S. sanctions

- **Legislative Action:** Congress can authorise, prohibit, or set parameters for a CBDC. The House passed H.R. 5403 in the 118th Congress to prohibit the Fed from issuing a CBDC or offering retail accounts.<sup>11</sup> A similar bill (substitute to H.R. 1919) was ordered reported by the House Financial Services Committee on April 2, 2025, in the 119th Congress, indicating a legislative leaning against a U.S. CBDC under current proposals.

**Firm Implications**

1. **Significant Policy Uncertainty:** The conflicting stances of the previous (Biden) and current (Trump) administrations, coupled with congressional efforts to prohibit or restrict a CBDC, create substantial uncertainty for any firm planning around a U.S. digital dollar. Near-term development of a U.S. CBDC appears unlikely.

2. **Financial Institutions (Banks & Credit Unions):**

    - **Reduced Disintermediation Threat (Short-Term):** The current political climate alleviates immediate concerns about a retail CBDC drawing deposits away from banks.

    - **Focus on Existing Rails:** Banks will likely continue to focus on enhancing existing payment systems, such as FedNow, Zelle, and the RTP Network, rather than preparing for CBDC integration.

    - **Intermediated Model Still a Factor:** If policy shifts back towards exploring a CBDC, the Fed's preferred intermediated model would still position banks as crucial intermediaries, requiring investment but also offering new service opportunities.

3. **Payment Companies (Fintech, Card Networks):**

    - **Breathing Room:** The slowdown/prohibition of a U.S. CBDC gives private payment providers more time to innovate and solidify their market positions without direct competition from a Fed-issued digital currency.

    - **Stablecoin Ecosystem Impact:** The debate and potential restrictions on CBDCs may indirectly influence the regulatory landscape for stablecoins, which could become the primary private-sector alternative if a U.S. CBDC is not pursued.

4. **Cryptocurrency and Stablecoin Providers:**

    - **Reduced Direct Competition from Government:** The current U.S. stance against a CBDC could be seen as positive for stablecoin issuers, as it removes a potential government-backed competitor in the digital payments space.

    - **Continued Regulatory Scrutiny:** However, the broader concerns about digital assets that fuel the CBDC debate (privacy, illicit finance, financial stability) will likely ensure continued regulatory scrutiny of stablecoins and other cryptocurrencies.

5. **U.S. International Competitiveness & Dollar Dominance:**

    - **Risk of Falling Behind:** If the U.S. definitively halts CBDC research and development while other major economies (e.g., China, EU) advance theirs, it could erode the U.S. dollar's technological edge and potentially its dominance in international payments and settlements over the long term.

    - **Sanctions Effectiveness:** Proliferation of foreign CBDCs, especially those designed for cross-border use, could make it easier for countries to transact outside the dollar system, potentially weakening the impact of U.S. financial sanctions.

6. **Technology and Infrastructure Providers:**

    - **Shift in Focus:** Companies involved in developing blockchain or digital ledger technology might see reduced domestic opportunities related to a U.S. CBDC, shifting focus to private sector applications or international CBDC projects.

7. **Consumers and Businesses:**

    - **Status Quo for Payments:** End-users will likely continue to rely on existing digital payment methods, with incremental improvements through services like FedNow, rather than a transformative shift to a CBDC shortly.

    - **Ongoing Privacy Debates:** The privacy concerns highlighted in the CBDC discussion will remain relevant for all digital payment forms and will continue to be a key factor in any future digital currency considerations.

### Digital Markets: CRS - Key Issues in Stablecoin Legislation in the 119th Congress

The Congressional Research Service (CRS) report outlines key debates surrounding stablecoin legislation in the 119th Congress, focusing on House Bill H.R. 2392 and Senate Bills S. 919 (with a revised version, S. 1582). Stablecoins, digital assets pegged to a reference asset such as the U.S. dollar, are a significant focus of regulatory attention.

1. **Who Could Issue Stablecoins?**

    - **Debate:** Should issuance be limited to banks, or also include nonbank financial firms and nonfinancial commercial firms?

    - **Current Bills (H.R. 2392 & S. 919):** Both would allow all three types of firms to issue stablecoins under a *new* regulatory regime, separate from existing bank regulations.

    - **Arguments for Bank Issuance:** Banks are central to payments and already under strict supervision.

    - **Concerns/Nuances:** The new regime is less comprehensive than the existing bank regulation. Stablecoin issuers (even bank subsidiaries) would be carved out of banks’ consolidated capital requirements. Critics fear exposing banks to the risks of the crypto market. Concerns also exist about "big tech" dominating payments if commercial firms issue stablecoins.

2. **Risk:**

    - **Primary Concern:** The risk of mass redemptions if holders lose confidence in the peg, similar to bank runs.

    - **Proposed Solution:** Both bills mandate 100% backing by reserves in relatively safe and liquid assets (e.g., deposits, government money market funds).

    - **Trade-off:** Safety vs. profitability for issuers. Fully cash-backed offers no profit (excl. fees); riskier assets increase profit but also risk.

    - **Residual Risk:** Even mandated reserves are not entirely riskless. Regulators could set additional capital/liquidity rules.

    - **No Federal Backstop:** Unlike banks (FDIC insurance, Fed discount window), stablecoin issuers would explicitly have no comparable federal support.

3. **Federal vs. State Regulation:**

    - **Current State:** Stablecoins are mainly state-regulated with limited requirements.

    - **Proposed Dual Regime:** Bills allow issuers to choose state or federal regulation.

      - **H.R. 2392:** Any non-national bank issuer can choose.

      - **S. 919:** Limits state regulation to issuers with \<\$10 billion in stablecoins outstanding; allows national banks to choose state regulation.

    - **Arguments for State:** Fosters innovation through diverse approaches.

    - **Arguments Against State:** Risk of a "race to the bottom" with lax standards. <sup>Five</sup> bills attempt to mitigate this by establishing mandatory minimum standards and requiring Treasury certification of state regulators.<sup>6</sup> State regulators may have fewer resources.

    - **Key Difference from Banking:** State-chartered stablecoin issuers could operate interstate without multiple state registrations. Bank stablecoin subsidiaries opting for state regulation could fall outside the federal bank regulator’s purview in many instances, which is unusual.

4. **U.S. vs. Foreign-Issued Stablecoins:**

    - **Market Context (May 2025):** U.S. dollar stablecoin market estimated at \$242 billion; Tether (foreign, El Salvador-licensed) and Circle (U.S.-based USDC) dominate ~90%.

    - **H.R. 2392:** Prohibits secondary offer/sale of stablecoins unless US-licensed (within 18 months) or Treasury deems the foreign regime "comparable." Reciprocity limits foreign coins to secondary markets.

    - **S. 919:** Allows Treasury reciprocity for "comparable" foreign regulations, permitting interoperability. Also allows foreign-issued stablecoins on secondary markets if technically compliant with lawful orders (e.g., freezing transactions), even without reciprocity.

    - **Gap:** Neither bill addresses U.S. customer access to foreign-issued stablecoins via foreign exchanges or public blockchains, potentially limiting the bills' scope and incentivising issuers to seek less rigorous jurisdictions.

5. **Money Laundering (BSA/AML):**

    - **Challenge:** The Pseudonymous nature of public, decentralised blockchains complicates Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) and anti-money laundering (AML) compliance.

    - **Bills' Approach:** Stablecoin issuers would be deemed "financial institutions" under BSA. Applications cannot be denied solely because they are issued on a public, decentralised network.

    - **Implementation Gap:** While manageable for issuers dealing with known customers (e.g., exchanges), it's unclear how BSA/AML responsibilities extend to pseudonymous transactions on public blockchains beyond issuer control. This poses reputational and other risks. Monitoring foreign issuers on secondary markets (as per Section 919) for BSA/AML compliance is also unclear.

6. **Potential Conflicts of Interest:**

    - **Emerging Issue:** Following an announcement by World Liberty Financial (with ties to former Trump Administration officials) to issue a stablecoin, there's debate on adding provisions to govern conflicts of interest for public officials

**Firm Implications**

- **Regulatory Uncertainty and Cost for Issuers:** Aspiring and existing stablecoin issuers face a complex, evolving regulatory landscape. Compliance with new federal or state regimes will entail significant operational and financial costs. The choice between federal and state oversight will be a critical strategic decision.

- **Shifting Market Dynamics:** The rules for foreign-issued stablecoins (especially market leaders like Tether) will significantly impact competition. If U.S. access to foreign stablecoins is restricted or subject to stringent "comparability" requirements, U.S.-domiciled and regulated stablecoins could gain market share. Still, it may also drive U.S. users to unregulated offshore platforms.

- **Banking Sector Evolution:** Banks entering the stablecoin space will operate under a new, potentially less stringent (in some aspects, like consolidated capital) framework than traditional banking. This could introduce new revenue streams but also novel risks, especially if stablecoin activities are not fully firewalled from core banking operations. The ability of bank subsidiaries to opt for state-level stablecoin regulation outside direct federal bank oversight is a notable departure.

- **Systemic Risk Mitigation (Partial):** While reserve requirements aim to reduce run risk, the absence of a federal backstop (like deposit insurance) means stablecoins will still carry inherent risks for holders. The effectiveness of these measures depends on the final details and the strictness of asset requirements.

- **Persistent AML/CFT Challenges:** Despite classifying issuers as financial institutions, the fundamental nature of public blockchains means significant challenges will remain in preventing illicit use once stablecoins enter the broader crypto ecosystem. This could lead to ongoing regulatory scrutiny and potential reputational damage for issuers.

- **Innovation vs. Regulation Balance:** The dual regulatory approach (state/federal) attempts to balance innovation with consumer protection. However, it could lead to regulatory arbitrage or a "race to the bottom" if the Treasury does not robustly enforce minimum standards.

- **Increased Role for Treasury:** The Department of the Treasury will gain significant authority in certifying state regulatory regimes and determining the "comparability" of foreign stablecoin regulations, making it a key arbiter in the global stablecoin market.

- **Limited Scope on User Access:** The current bills primarily target issuers. U.S. individuals accessing foreign stablecoins through non-U.S. exchanges or decentralised finance (DeFi) protocols may remain largely outside the direct scope of this legislation, potentially undermining its comprehensiveness.

### Digital Markets: Joint Staff Statement from the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's (SEC) Division of Trading and Markets and the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA), originally issued on July 8, 2019, concerning broker-dealer custody of digital asset securities

The purpose of this rule is to safeguard customer assets and ensure their return in the event of a broker-dealer's failure. The unique nature of digital asset securities—how they are issued, held, and transferred—creates risks such as theft, loss of private keys, and the inability to reverse fraudulent or mistaken transactions, which may not align with the rules’ requirements for possession or control.

- **Non-Custodial Models:** The staff indicated that business models for digital asset securities not involving broker-dealer custody generally raised fewer concerns, provided other relevant laws and rules were followed. Examples included facilitating bilateral settlements between buyers and issuers, or operating trading platforms where trades settle directly between parties without the broker-dealer controlling the assets.

- **Securities Investor Protection Act (SIPA):** Concerns were raised that specific digital asset securities might not meet the definition of a "security" under the SIPA. If so, customers holding such assets might not receive SIPA protection in the event of a broker-dealer failure, instead becoming unsecured general creditors. This outcome was deemed likely to be inconsistent with customer expectations.

- **Ongoing Dialogue:** The staff mentioned their engagement with industry participants regarding custody solutions and affirmed their commitment to continued dialogue as the market develops.

- **Nature of the Statement:** It was explicitly stated that the document represented staff views and was not a rule, regulation, or official statement of the SEC or FINRA, carrying no legal force.

**Firm Implications**

The withdrawal of this statement on May 15, 2025, has several significant implications for firms operating or looking to operate in the digital asset securities space:

1. **Increased Regulatory Uncertainty:** The withdrawal of this long-standing (though informal) guidance document, without immediate replacement, may heighten uncertainty for broker-dealers. Firms that might have been using the 2019 statement as an indicator of staff thinking now lack that specific benchmark for developing their custody solutions for digital asset securities.

2. **Potential for New Guidance:** The withdrawal could signal that the SEC and FINRA are in the process of developing a new, possibly more formal, regulatory framework or updated guidance for digital asset custody. This new approach is likely to reflect the evolution of the market, technological advancements, and enforcement actions since 2019.

3. **Continued Caution on Custodial Solutions:** The fundamental challenges identified in the 2019 statement regarding possession or control, books and records, and SIPA applicability for digital asset securities remain pertinent. Firms will likely need to exercise continued, if not increased, caution when developing or seeking approval (e.g., FINRA NMAs/CMAs ) for custodial services.

4. **Preference for Non-Custodial Models:** Given that the withdrawn statement already views non-custodial activities as less problematic, firms might further lean towards these models to minimise regulatory friction. At the same time, the landscape for custodial services remains undefined.

5. **Emphasis on Direct Engagement:** With the informal guidance removed, firms will need to engage directly and proactively with SEC and FINRA staff to understand current expectations and to navigate the approval process for any digital asset securities activities, especially those involving custody.

6. **Heightened Compliance Review:** Legal and compliance teams at financial institutions will need to re-evaluate their strategies for digital asset securities in light of this withdrawal, anticipating potential shifts in regulatory focus.

7. **Industry Awaits Clarification:** The withdrawal makes the industry particularly attentive to any forthcoming announcements, proposed rules, or new interpretive guidance from the SEC and FINRA that would clarify the regulatory path forward for broker-dealer custody of digital asset securities.

### Staking: SEC - Statement on Certain Protocol Staking Activities

The Division of Corporation Finance has issued a statement providing its view that "Protocol Staking Activities" generally do not involve the offer and sale of securities under the Securities Act of 1933 or the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. This view applies specifically to the staking of "Covered Crypto Assets," which are intrinsically linked to the programmatic functioning of public, permissionless Proof-of-Stake (PoS) networks and are used to participate in or earn rewards for maintaining the network's consensus mechanism, technological operations, and security.

The statement clarifies that various forms of Protocol Staking, including self (solo) staking, self-custodial staking directly with a third party, and custodial arrangements (where a custodian facilitates staking without making entrepreneurial decisions or guaranteeing returns), are typically not considered securities. The Division bases its analysis on the "Howey test," concluding that these activities do not involve an expectation of profits derived from the "entrepreneurial or managerial efforts of others." Instead, the rewards are viewed as payments for administrative or ministerial services provided to the network. The statement also covers certain "Ancillary Services" like slashing coverage, early unbonding, and aggregation of crypto assets, deeming them administrative and not indicative of a security offering.

**What it means for firms:**

- **Reduced Regulatory Burden for Protocol Staking:** For companies and individuals involved in "Protocol Staking Activities" as defined in the statement, this guidance suggests that they **do not need to register with the SEC** for these specific activities. This provides a degree of regulatory clarity and potentially reduces compliance costs for participants in these forms of staking.

- **Emphasis on "Efforts of Others" in Howey Test:** The statement reinforces the SEC's interpretation of the "efforts of others" prong of the Howey test. It distinguishes between "managerial or entrepreneurial efforts" (which would indicate a security) and "administrative or ministerial activities" (which do not). Firms engaged in crypto activities must carefully assess whether their offerings involve active management that generates profits for investors.

- **Exclusion of Certain Staking Models:** The statement explicitly *does not* cover all forms of staking, such as "liquid staking," "restaking," or "liquid restaking." This implies that firms offering these or other non-covered staking products should not assume the same regulatory treatment and may still be subject to securities laws.

- **Importance of Custodial Practices:** For custodial staking arrangements to fall outside the scope of securities, custodians must strictly adhere to specific practices: deposited crypto assets must *not* be used for operational purposes, lent, pledged, rehypothecated, or subject to third-party claims, and the custodian must *not* make discretionary decisions about staking. Firms offering custodial staking must ensure their practices align with these criteria.

- **Continued Nuance in Crypto Regulation:** The statement emphasises that a definitive determination of whether a specific Protocol Staking Activity involves a security requires a detailed analysis of the facts. This means that while the guidance provides clarity for *certain* activities, firms must still conduct their legal assessments, as variations in facts could lead to different conclusions.

### Digital Markets: BIS Working Papers No 1270 Stablecoins and safe asset prices

### DeFi: BIS Working Papers No 1268 Towards verifiability of total value locked (TVL) in decentralised finance

This BIS Working Paper critically examines Total Value Locked (TVL), the primary metric used to gauge the economic scale of Decentralised Finance (DeFi) protocols. The authors argue that, despite blockchain data being public, current TVL computation methods, which are primarily reliant on self-reports from community members to aggregators like DeFiLlama, lack standardisation and make independent verification difficult. This opacity can lead to discrepancies in reported TVL figures (e.g., Ethereum TVL estimates varied from \$80 billion to \$190 billion at the end of 2024) and potential manipulation.

**Key Findings from their systematic study of 939 DeFi projects on Ethereum:**

1. **Reliance on Off-Chain Data:** 10.5% of protocols depend partially or entirely on external servers for TVL computation, hindering full reproducibility from on-chain data.

2. **Heterogeneity in Methods:** While most (78.6%) use standard balance queries, 68 alternative, self-defined balance functions were identified, making interpretation difficult. However, the use of these alternative methods decreased over time (from 28.2% in Jan 2023 to 8.9% in Jan 2024).

3. **Potential Double Counting:** 240 instances of identical balance queries were found to be repeated across multiple protocols for the same contracts and tokens, which could inflate overall TVL figures. The value in such contracts peaked at nearly \$16 billion at the end of 2021.

4. **Code Stability:** The TVL computation code for individual protocols tends to be relatively stable over time, which may indicate consistency or a lack of updates to reflect smart contract changes.

Proposed Solution: "verifiable Total Value Locked" (vTVL)

The paper introduces vTVL, a metric designed to measure the TVL that can be verified using solely on-chain data and standard balance queries (balanceOf and eth_getBalance).

In a case study of 400 protocols:

- vTVL estimations were closely aligned (discrepancy ±0.05) with published figures for 23.5% of protocols.

- Estimations were broadly aligned (discrepancy ±0.5) for another 23%, totalling 46.5% reasonable alignment.

- Significant discrepancies were found for the remainder, often smaller protocols or those using external hosts or custom functions.

Recommendations for Standardisation:

The authors propose design guidelines to improve TVL verifiability, standardisation, and transparency:

1. Compute TVL exclusively from **on-chain sources**.

2. Protocols should **publish explicit lists of smart contracts and tokens** used in their TVL calculation.

3. **Favour standard balance methods**; if alternatives are used, they must be adequately documented.

4. Aggregators should **publish their token categorisations** and allow users to filter assets (especially derivative vs. non-derivative).

5. Establish **common standards for protocol selection criteria and version management** by aggregators.

The paper concludes that achieving common standards for verifiable TVL is critical for the DeFi ecosystem's maturity, informing user decisions, ensuring fair competition, and meeting expectations for financial transparency from users, investors, and regulators.

1. **Increased Scrutiny on DeFi Protocols:** Protocols may face greater pressure to justify their Total Value Locked (TVL) figures and the methodologies used to determine them. Those relying heavily on off-chain data or complex, undocumented custom functions for TVL calculation might be viewed with more scepticism.

2. **Push for Transparency from TVL Aggregators:** Platforms like DeFiLlama may need to implement stricter submission guidelines for protocol adapters, enforce greater transparency in how Total Value Locked (TVL) is calculated, and potentially differentiate between standard-reported TVL and vTVL. This could become a competitive factor among aggregators.

3. **Enhanced Due Diligence for Investors and Users:** The findings provide investors and users with a deeper understanding of the potential unreliability and inflation risks inherent in current Total Value Locked (TVL) metrics. They may increasingly demand or seek out vTVL-like metrics for more robust decision-making.

4. **Development of New Auditing Standards:** The concept of vTVL could pave the way for new auditing practices in DeFi, focusing not just on smart contract security but also on the veracity and verifiability of reported financial metrics.

5. **Regulatory Interest:** This paper, originating from the BIS, signals significant institutional interest in the transparency and reliability of DeFi metrics. It provides a framework that regulators may consider when assessing the size, risks, and interconnectedness of the decentralised finance (DeFi) market.

6. **Impact on Protocol Valuations and Competition:** If vTVL or similar verifiable metrics gain traction, protocols demonstrating higher verifiability might be perceived as more trustworthy, potentially impacting their perceived value and competitive standing. It could also lead to a more accurate understanding of market share.

7. **Operational Adjustments for Protocols:** To meet new transparency standards, protocols may need to invest in better documentation, standardised reporting mechanisms for their Total Value Locked (TVL), and ensure that their community-contributed adapters align with best practices for verifiability.

8. **Potential for DeFi Market Re-evaluation:** Wider adoption of verifiable metrics could lead to a more accurate (and potentially different) sizing of the DeFi market and individual protocol dominance, mainly if issues like double-counting are systematically addressed at the infrastructure level.

### AI: GAO - ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE Use and Oversight in Financial Services

This GAO report examines the use and oversight of artificial intelligence (AI) in the financial services industry, as part of GAO's annual review of financial services regulations.

**Key Findings:**

1. **AI Use, Benefits, and Risks:**

    - Financial institutions are increasingly utilising AI in areas such as automated trading, credit decisions, customer service (e.g., chatbots), investment advice (robo-advisors), fraud detection, and risk management.

    - **Benefits** include improved efficiency, reduced costs, enhanced customer experiences (e.g., personalised advice), and potentially greater financial inclusion for those with thin credit files. Some AI providers reported that their AI models led to increased credit approvals for women and people of colour.

    - **Risks** include biased lending decisions (fair lending risk), data quality issues, privacy concerns (e.g., leakage of sensitive data from AI models), new cybersecurity threats, operational disruptions, model risk (e.g., AI underperforming due to data issues or changing conditions), compliance risks (e.g., limited explainability hindering compliance with adverse action notice requirements), concentration risk (reliance on a few third-party AI providers), and herding risk (AI leading to similar decisions, potentially causing systemic issues). Generative AI specifically can produce "hallucinations" (credible yet false information).

2. **Federal Oversight of AI Use:**

    - Federal financial regulators primarily oversee AI using existing laws, regulations, guidance, and risk-based examinations, as these generally apply regardless of the technology used.

    - Some regulators have issued guidance specific to AI (e.g., on its use in lending) or conducted examinations focused on AI. For instance, the SEC conducted exams on AI disclosures by investment advisers, finding that some had misrepresented their use of AI. OCC reviewed the use of AI by large banks and found that some risk assessments didn't explicitly capture AI-specific risks.

    - Regulators are assessing AI risks and may refine guidance or update regulations. Most regulators believe existing statutory authorities are generally sufficient for now, though industry representatives see opportunities to clarify guidance.

    - **NCUA's Oversight Gaps:**

      - The National Credit Union Administration's (NCUA) model risk management guidance is limited in scope (addresses only interest rate risk modelling) and detail, not providing sufficient guidance for managing broader model risks, including AI models.

      - NCUA lacks the authority to examine technology service providers, despite credit unions' increasing reliance on them for AI-driven services. GAO reiterated its 2015 recommendation that Congress grant NCUA this authority.

3. **Regulators' Use of AI:**

    - Federal financial regulators are increasingly integrating AI into their own operations and supervisory activities, including identifying risks, supporting research, and detecting potential legal violations or reporting errors.

    - As of December 2024, the Federal Reserve and the SEC had the most activities using AI.

    - Most regulators use AI outputs to inform staff decisions, not as the sole basis for decisions. Regulators are developing AI-specific policies and strategies for their internal use.

**GAO Recommendations:**

- GAO reiterates its 2015 recommendation that Congress consider granting NCUA authority to examine technology service providers for credit unions.

- The GAO recommends that the NCUA update its model risk management guidance to encompass a broader range of models used by credit unions and provide more detailed information. NCUA generally agreed with this recommendation.

**Firm Implications**

1. **For All Financial Institutions (Banks, Credit Unions, Investment Firms, etc.):**

    - **Increased Scrutiny on AI Use:** Firms using AI can expect continued and potentially heightened scrutiny from regulators, focusing on risk management, data quality, model validation, bias, explainability, and compliance with existing laws (e.g., fair lending, consumer protection).

    - **Need for Robust AI Governance:** Institutions must develop and maintain strong AI governance frameworks, including model risk management that explicitly addresses AI-specific risks, data governance policies, and cybersecurity measures tailored to AI threats.

    - **Explainability Remains Key:** The difficulty in explaining outputs from complex AI models will continue to be a challenge, particularly for complying with adverse action notice requirements in lending and ensuring non-discrimination.

    - **Third-Party Risk Management:** Reliance on third-party AI vendors necessitates rigorous due diligence and ongoing monitoring, as regulators are focused on these relationships. This is especially critical for smaller institutions that lack in-house AI development capabilities.

    - **Documentation and Justification:** Firms should be prepared to thoroughly document their AI models, data sources, testing processes, and how they mitigate risks like bias and privacy violations.

    - **Generative AI Caution:** While generative AI offers productivity benefits (e.g., for internal policy questions, code writing assistance), its use in customer-facing applications or high-stakes decisions will require extreme caution due to risks like "hallucinations" and potential for misleading information.

2. **For Credit Unions:**

    - **Anticipate Updated NCUA Guidance:** Credit unions should expect the NCUA to review and potentially update its model risk management guidance to be more comprehensive, covering AI models and aligning more closely with the approaches of banking regulators. This may lead to increased expectations for compliance.

    - **Potential for Vendor Examinations:** If Congress grants NCUA authority to examine third-party technology providers, credit unions' vendors (including AI service providers) could face direct NCUA oversight. This might lead to improved vendor risk management, but could also affect service costs or availability.

3. **For Technology Service Providers (including AI Vendors):**

    - **Increased Due Diligence from Clients:** Financial institutions will likely demand greater transparency, better documentation, and more robust risk management features from their AI vendors.

    - **Potential Direct Oversight (NCUA Vendors):** Technology providers serving credit unions could face direct examination by NCUA if legislative changes occur, requiring them to meet specific regulatory standards.

    - **Focus on Trustworthy AI Principles:** Vendors developing AI solutions for financial services should align their products with principles of trustworthy AI, including fairness, transparency, explainability, and security.

4. **For Regulatory Technology (RegTech) Firms:**

    - **Opportunities:** The increasing complexity of AI and associated regulatory expectations create opportunities for RegTech firms to offer solutions that help financial institutions manage AI risks, ensure compliance, and improve AI governance.

5. **General Market Implications:**

    - **Clarification of Rules Forthcoming:** While regulators are currently leveraging existing frameworks, the report suggests ongoing assessment and potential refinement of guidance and regulations to address emerging AI vulnerabilities. Firms should monitor these developments closely.

    - **Cost of Adoption vs. Compliance:** The cost of developing or acquiring AI, alongside the necessary compliance and risk management infrastructure, may be a significant factor, potentially creating a divide between larger institutions with more resources and smaller ones.

### Digital Markets: BIS Working Papers No 1265 DeFiying gravity? An empirical analysis of cross-border Bitcoin, Ether and stablecoin flows

The BIS Working Paper No. 1265, authored by Raphael Auer, Ulf Lewrick, and Jan Paulick (May 2025), investigates the trends and drivers of cross-border flows for Bitcoin (BTC), Ether (ETH), Tether (USDT), and USD Coin (USDC) across 184 countries from 2017 to 2024.

Key findings include:

1. **Substantial Flows:** Cross-border flows of these four cryptoassets are significant, peaking at around USD 2.6 trillion in 2021, with stablecoins (USDT and USDC) accounting for nearly half. Volumes fell in 2023 but have since resurged.

2. **Geographical Nodes & Shifts:** The United States, the United Kingdom, and major emerging markets are key nodes. Significant geographical shifts occurred, notably from China (due to tighter regulations) to other emerging markets, such as India, Indonesia, and Türkiye.

3. **Network Characteristics:** Crypto network density is higher and concentration is lower than in traditional cross-border banking.

4. **Drivers of Native Cryptoassets (BTC, ETH):** Speculative motives and global funding conditions (e.g., US dollar appreciation, credit spreads, VIX) are primary drivers, indicating increasing interconnectedness with mainstream finance.

5. **Drivers of Stablecoins (USDT, USDC) & Low-Value BTC:** Transactional motives are more prominent. There is a strong association with higher costs of traditional remittances, suggesting that crypto is used as an alternative, particularly for low-value BTC transfers and stablecoins flowing from advanced economies to emerging markets. High inflation in sending and receiving countries also boosts these flows.

6. **Diminished Geographic Barriers:** Traditional "gravity" factors like physical distance play a much smaller role in crypto flows, especially for stablecoins, compared to traditional financial flows. Linguistic proximity has some effect, but it is less pronounced than in conventional banking.

7. **Ineffectiveness of Capital Flow Management (CFM) Measures:** CFMs targeting outflows or inflows appear to have little impact on crypto flows and may even correlate with an increase in some cryptoasset flows, hinting at circumvention.

The paper employs a gravity framework for its analysis. It contributes a comprehensive dataset on these four major cryptoassets, enabling a nuanced understanding of their varied use cases (speculative versus transactional) and their interactions with traditional financial systems and regulations.

**Firm Implications**

The findings of this paper have several important implications for firms, financial institutions, and policymakers:

1. **Challenge to Traditional Financial Intermediaries:** The strong link between high remittance costs and increased stablecoin/low-value BTC flows suggests that cryptoassets are actively competing with traditional remittance services. Firms in this sector need to innovate and reduce costs to remain competitive.

2. **Capital Control Evasion:** The apparent ineffectiveness of CFMs against crypto flows means that firms and individuals may increasingly use cryptoassets to bypass national capital controls. This poses challenges for countries attempting to manage capital flows and could impact currency stability.

3. **Regulatory Arbitrage and Scrutiny:** The "defying gravity" nature of crypto flows and their ability to circumvent existing measures will likely lead to increased regulatory scrutiny and calls for international coordination. Firms operating in the crypto space should anticipate evolving and potentially stricter regulatory landscapes.

4. **Integration with Mainstream Finance:** The influence of global funding conditions on native cryptoassets indicates a growing integration with the traditional financial system. This means that crypto markets are becoming increasingly susceptible to mainstream financial shocks, and vice versa, creating new risk transmission channels that financial firms must monitor.

5. **New Market Opportunities:** For firms in the crypto industry, the findings highlight growing transactional use cases, particularly in regions with high inflation or expensive traditional financial services. This suggests market opportunities that extend beyond pure speculation.

6. **Risk Management:** Financial institutions that are exposed to or deal in cryptoassets must understand these flow dynamics. The speculative nature of native cryptocurrencies, coupled with their global reach and the transactional utility of stablecoins, presents a complex risk profile that includes market, operational, and compliance risks (e.g., AML/CFT in cross-border transactions).

7. **Data and Monitoring Needs:** The paper underscores the difficulty in tracking and attributing crypto flows. Firms and regulators will need to invest in advanced analytics and data sources to better understand and supervise this evolving ecosystem.
