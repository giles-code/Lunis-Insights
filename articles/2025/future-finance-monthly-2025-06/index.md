## Executive Summary

June 2025 marked a significant global shift from high-level policy to detailed, operational rule-making for digital finance, compelling firms to address the practical realities of compliance. The primary focus was on establishing precise technical standards and licensing frameworks, representing a clear advancement in the industry's regulatory maturity.

In the European Union, the focus was firmly on implementing its landmark regulations. A comprehensive set of technical standards under MiCA was published, covering topics from conflicts of interest and record-keeping to the authorisation process for ART issuers and, importantly, the minimum requirements for liquidity management policies. At the same time, the first delegated act under DORA mandated a strict, TIBER-EU-based framework for threat-led penetration testing, imposing significant compliance and cost challenges for critical financial entities. The EBA also provided vital short-term clarity on the complex relationship between MiCA and PSD2, offering a temporary route for crypto firms that provide payment-like services.

The United States saw a surge in legislative activity, creating a dynamic and complex legislative landscape. The Senate’s bipartisan approval of the GENIUS Act indicated that federal stablecoin regulation is approaching. Meanwhile, the CLARITY Act, which aims to define broader market structures, continued its progress through House committees. However, this legislative momentum was met with resistance from the SEC’s perceived inconsistency regarding the security status of major cryptocurrencies and its public preference for a slow, formal rulemaking process, which frustrated industry demands for immediate clarity.

In the **United Kingdom**, regulators concentrated on strengthening key legal and data frameworks. The landmark Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 was published, updating the UK’s data protection system. Alongside this, the Law Commission put forward crucial proposals to tackle the complex private international law issues caused by digital assets, aiming to bring clarity to cross-border disputes involving crypto-tokens and electronic trade documents.

Major financial hubs in **Asia** took decisive steps to formalise their regulatory regimes. Hong Kong has gazetted its comprehensive Stablecoins Ordinance, immediately followed by a consultation on specific AML/CFT requirements, signalling a rapid move to create a fully licensed and supervised stablecoin ecosystem. Meanwhile, Singapore clarified and tightened its rules, effectively requiring digital token service providers that exclusively serve overseas customers to wind down, demonstrating a commitment to robust domestic supervision.

Internationally, the narrative was shaped by significant economic stakes and the crucial importance of risk management. The **Financial Action Task Force (FATF)** released its latest update, revealing frustratingly slow global implementation of AML/CFT standards for crypto, particularly the Travel Rule, and highlighting the increasing use of stablecoins in illicit finance. A report by the UK Cryptoasset Business Council urged the government to act decisively to capture a share of the projected US\$16.1 trillion tokenisation market. Underscoring this, a Moody’s report emphasised that institutional adoption is entirely contingent on managing the novel risks of digital assets through robust smart contract security, AI-powered monitoring, and secure interoperability.

## Europe

### DORA: Delegated Regulation on RTS on threat-led penetration testing under DORA published in OJ

This Commission Delegated Regulation supplements the primary DORA regulation (EU) 2022/2554 by establishing detailed rules for **Threat-Led Penetration Testing (TLPT)** for significant financial entities. The regulation formalises and mandates a testing framework primarily based on the existing TIBER-EU (Threat Intelligence-based Ethical Red Teaming) framework. The core objective is to create a harmonised, intelligence-driven approach to testing the cyber resilience of the EU's most critical financial institutions against sophisticated, real-world cyberattacks.

**Key Components:**

1. **Identification of Entities:**

    - TLPT is mandatory for systemically critical financial entities, including specific credit institutions (G-SIIs, O-SIIs), central counterparties, central securities depositories, and large payment institutions, trading venues, and insurance undertakings that meet high quantitative thresholds (e.g., transaction value, market share, assets).

    - National authorities (TLPT Authorities) have the discretion to exempt entities if testing isn't justified by their risk profile or to include other entities based on qualitative assessments of their systemic importance and ICT risk profile.

2. **Standardised Testing Process:** The regulation defines a structured, multi-phase process for every TLPT:

    - **Preparation Phase:** The financial entity is notified by the TLPT authority and must establish a **Control Team** to manage the test, define the scope (which critical functions to test), and select certified external providers for threat intelligence and testing (the **Red Team**). All key documents, including the scope and provider selection, require validation by the TLPT authority.

    - **Testing Phase:**

      - **Threat Intelligence:** An external provider produces a **Targeted Threat Intelligence Report**, identifying likely threat actors and creating realistic attack scenarios based on the entity's specific profile.

      - **Red Team Test:** Testers simulate the approved attack scenarios on the entity's live production systems for a minimum of 12 weeks. The goal is to test the entity's full range of defences (people, processes, and technology) covertly, without the knowledge of the entity's security defence team (the **Blue Team**).

    - **Closure Phase:** After the test, the Red Team and Blue Team produce detailed reports. A **purple teaming** exercise is conducted, where both teams collaborate to analyse the attack, share insights, and maximise the learning experience. The financial entity must then create a **Remediation Plan** to address all identified vulnerabilities.

3. **Roles and Responsibilities:**

    - **Financial Entity:** Appoints a **Control Team** to manage the test, a **Blue Team** (the defenders, initially unaware of the test), and contracts external **Threat Intelligence Providers** and **Testers** (Red Team).

    - **TLPT Authority:** A designated national authority establishes a **TLPT Cyber Team** (TCT) to oversee and validate every critical step of the process, ensuring compliance and issuing a final **Attestation** upon successful completion.

    - **Testers:** Must meet stringent requirements for experience, certifications, and professional indemnity insurance. Internal testers can be used, but external testers must be contracted at least once every three tests.

4. **Cooperation and Mutual Recognition:**

    - The regulation establishes clear rules for conducting tests involving entities that operate cross-border, use shared ICT service providers (joint TLPT), or use a common third-party provider (pooled TLPT).

    - A lead TLPT authority is designated to coordinate efforts, ensuring efficiency and preventing redundant testing. Results are to be mutually recognised across Member States.

**Implications for Financial Entities and Regulators**

The regulation moves advanced security testing from a discretionary best practice to a mandatory, highly structured, and supervised compliance requirement.

- **For Financial Entities:**

  - **Significant Compliance Overhead and Cost:** TLPT is a complex and resource-intensive undertaking. Entities must budget for highly specialised external providers, dedicate considerable time from senior internal staff to the control team, and manage a lengthy project cycle (often spanning 6-9 months).

  - **Increased Senior Management Accountability:** The requirement for the management body to approve key documents (like the scope specification) and remediation plans places direct, auditable responsibility on senior leadership for overseeing digital operational resilience.

  - **Mandatory Uplift in Security Maturity:** The process forces a shift from standard vulnerability scanning or basic penetration testing to a more sophisticated, intelligence-led approach. It tests not only technology, but also detection and response procedures, as well as staff awareness, providing a holistic and realistic assessment of resilience.

  - **Inherent Operational Risk:** Testing is conducted on live production systems, creating a risk of service disruption or data corruption. Robust risk management, validated by the regulator, is a critical and non-trivial component of the process.

  - **End of "Testing in Isolation":** For entities within a group or using major third-party providers, the framework pushes for joint or pooled testing. This requires unprecedented coordination with other financial firms and service providers, breaking down silos.

- **For the Broader Financial Sector:**

  - **Strengthened System-Wide Resilience:** By subjecting the most interconnected and critical players to rigorous, realistic testing, the regulation aims to improve the resilience of the entire EU financial ecosystem and mitigate systemic risk from cyberattacks.

  - **Creation of a Market for High-End Security Services:** The mandate will likely fuel demand for certified, high-quality threat intelligence and red teaming providers, leading to market growth and consolidation in this specialised sector.

  - **Focus on Actionable Threat Intelligence:** The framework formally elevates the role of threat intelligence in defining security strategy. The entire testing process is predicated on simulating the *modus operandi* of actual adversaries, forcing a more proactive and forward-looking approach to defence.

### MiCA: European Commission adopts Delegated Regulation on RTS on liquidity management policy and procedures under MiCA

The European Commission adopted this Delegated Regulation to supplement MiCA, specifying the **minimum contents of the liquidity management policy and procedures** for issuers of asset-referenced tokens (ARTs) and e-money tokens (EMTs). This applies to significant issuers by default and can be extended to non-significant issuers by competent authorities. The core objective is to ensure issuers can meet redemption requests from token holders at any time, especially under stress conditions.

**Key Requirements:**

- **Robust Policies and Procedures:** Issuers must establish, document, and maintain comprehensive strategies for identifying, measuring, managing, and monitoring liquidity risk across various time horizons. These policies must be approved by the management body, which also sets the risk tolerance for each token.

- **Maintenance of Reserve Assets:** The policy must ensure that adequate levels of liquid reserve assets are maintained to meet redemption requests at all times. This includes ongoing monitoring of asset market value, creditworthiness, and concentration risk.

- **Overcollateralisation:** Issuers must assess the appropriateness of overcollateralisation, particularly when the referenced assets are highly volatile or are not part of the reserve, to ensure the stability of the reserve's value.

- **Intra-day Liquidity Management:** The framework requires robust management of intra-day liquidity risk, including identifying needs and having processes to meet them.

- **Contingency Planning and Early Warning Indicators:** Issuers must develop a liquidity contingency plan with calibrated early warning signals. These must include monitoring for maximum deviations between the market value of the token, the reserve assets, and the referenced assets to anticipate large-scale redemptions.

- **Liquidity Stress Testing:** The policy must include detailed procedures for conducting regular liquidity stress tests. This includes defining the risks covered, the parameters and scenarios used (including reverse stress testing), and documenting the outcomes and any remedial actions taken.

- **Segregation of Policies:** Liquidity management policies and procedures must be established and applied separately for *each* ART or EMT issued. They must also be kept separate from policies related to the issuer's other business activities.

- **Custodian Management:** Issuers must regularly monitor their custodians and establish internal limits to avoid concentration risk with any single custodian.

**Firm Implications**

This regulation imposes a sophisticated, bank-like liquidity risk management framework on ART and EMT issuers, with significant operational consequences.

- **Mandatory Sophistication in Risk Management:** Issuers can no longer rely on simple liquidity management. They must implement a dynamic, data-driven framework comparable to the Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP) used by banks. This requires significant investment in expertise, systems, and processes.

- **Board-Level Accountability:** The requirement for the management body to approve policies and set risk tolerance levels places direct, auditable responsibility on senior leadership for liquidity adequacy.

- **Proactive Stress Testing as a Core Function:** Liquidity stress testing is not a periodic, check-the-box exercise. It must be a regular, ongoing process, including reverse stress testing, with results directly feeding back into and modifying the firm's contingency plans and risk limits.

- **Increased Operational Costs:** The need for constant monitoring of asset values, custodian concentration, and early warning indicators, as well as the potential need for overcollateralisation, will increase operational complexity and costs.

- **No One-Size-Fits-All Approach:** The requirement to have separate, tailored liquidity policies for each token issued means firms with multiple offerings face a significant compliance multiplier. Each policy must be individually justified and maintained.

- **Heightened Supervisory Scrutiny:** The detailed documentation required for stress testing (parameters, assumptions, outcomes) provides supervisors with a powerful tool to scrutinise an issuer's resilience and to mandate stronger liquidity requirements if they deem the internal models or assumptions inadequate.

### MiCA: EBA opinion on PSD2 and MiCA overlap

EBA's opinion to address the regulatory conflict and uncertainty arising from the overlap between the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA) and the Payment Services Directive (PSD2). The core issue is that Electronic Money Tokens (EMTs)—a type of crypto-asset pegged to a fiat currency—are considered both "crypto-assets" under the Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) Regulation and "electronic money/funds" under the Payment Services Directive 2 (PSD2). This could force Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs) to seek a burdensome "dual authorisation" under both regimes.

The EBA's opinion provides a two-pronged solution:

1. **Short-Term Guidance (for National Competent Authorities - NCAs) until PSD3/PSR is adopted:**

    - **Which services require a PSD2 license?**

      - Transferring EMTs on behalf of clients.

      - Custody and administration of EMTs where the wallet allows transfers to and from third parties (this wallet is considered a "payment account").

    - **Which services DO NOT require a PSD2 license:**

      - The exchange of crypto-assets for funds or other crypto-assets where the CASP uses its capital.

      - Intermediating the purchase of any crypto-asset using EMTs.

    - **Supervisory Priorities & Forbearance:**

      - **Enforce These:** NCAs must prioritise the enforcement of Strong Customer Authentication (SCA) for wallet access and transfers, as well as their own cumulative funds requirements and fraud reporting.

      - **De-prioritise These:** NCAs are advised *not* to prioritise the supervision of complex PSD2 rules related to Open Banking, safeguarding (as MiCA's safekeeping rules apply), unique identifiers (such as IBANs), and specific information disclosures where it is technically challenging (e.g., exact network fees).

    - **Transition Period:** CASPs have until **March 1, 2026,** to obtain the necessary PSD2 authorisation.

2. **Long-Term Legislative Advice (for EU Lawmakers):**

    - **The Preferred Solution:** The EBA strongly recommends amending MiCA to incorporate key payment-related protections from the upcoming PSD3/PSR. This would establish a single, comprehensive regulatory framework for EMT services under MiCA, eliminating the need for a separate payment license.

    - **Rejected Solution:** The EBA firmly opposes simply excluding EMTs from payment regulations, as this would create an unlevel playing field, expose consumers to risk, and encourage regulatory arbitrage.

**Firm Implications for Stakeholders**

- **For Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs):**

  - **Action is Mandatory:** You must immediately assess if your EMT-related services (like transfers and custody for third parties) qualify as payment services under this guidance.

  - **Licensing Deadline:** If your services qualify, you have until **March 1, 2026,** to either obtain a Payment Institution (PI) license under PSD2 or partner with a licensed provider. Failing to do so will require you to cease these activities.

  - **Increased Capital Requirements:** Prepare for **Cumulative Capital Requirements**. You will need to hold capital that satisfies **both** MiCA and PSD2 requirements, which will increase your operational costs. For example, an entity could be required to have a minimum of €250,000 (€125,000 under MiCA + €125,000 under PSD2).

  - **Top Compliance Priority is Security:** Your most critical task is to implement **Strong Customer Authentication (SCA)** for accessing wallets and initiating transfers by the March 2026 deadline. The EBA is clear that there is no compromise on this security standard.

  - **Temporary Operational Relief:** You gain a reprieve from implementing the most challenging parts of PSD2, specifically Open Banking API access and rules that are ill-suited for blockchain technology (like fixed execution times and fees).

- **For Banks and Traditional Payment Institutions (PSPs):**

  - **Partnership Opportunities:** The requirement for CASPs to partner with licensed PSPs creates new business opportunities for established financial institutions.

  - **Competitive Landscape:** The EBA's long-term goal is to ensure a level playing field. This means that eventually, CASPs offering payment services will operate under consumer protection and security standards comparable to yours, preventing them from competing based on lower regulatory standards.

- **For Consumers:**

  - **Enhanced Security:** You will benefit from significantly higher security when using EMTs for payments. The mandatory application of SCA will reduce the risk of fraud and theft from unauthorised transactions.

  - **Greater Clarity and Trust:** This guidance clarifies the rules, leading to a more stable and reliable market for EMTs and fostering greater consumer confidence.

  - **Short-Term Protection Gaps:** For the next 2-3 years, your rights regarding EMTs will not be fully aligned with traditional payments, particularly in areas like fee transparency, liability for incorrect transfers, and access to services via third-party apps (Open Banking).

### MiCA: Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/1140 (27 Feb 2025) supplements Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 on standards for crypto-asset service records.

This Delegated Regulation supplements the main Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) Regulation (EU) 2023/1114. Its primary purpose is to establish detailed and uniform rules for Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs) on the records they must keep for all their services, activities, orders, and transactions. The overarching goal is to enable competent authorities to effectively supervise CASPs, enforce regulations, and monitor for market abuse.

**Key Record-Keeping Requirements:**

The regulation mandates that CASPs maintain comprehensive and standardised records across three main categories:

- **General Business and Client Records (Annexe 1):** CASPs must document their entire operational and compliance framework. This includes keeping records of:

  - All policies, procedures, and internal assessments of their effectiveness.

  - Client agreements, communications (including marketing), and consent forms.

  - The safekeeping of client crypto-assets and funds, including details of accounts and custody arrangements.

  - Handling of complaints, conflicts of interest, and personal transactions by staff.

  - Outsourcing agreements and details of outsourced functions.

  - Records specific to services offered, such as suitability assessments for advisory services or operating rules for trading platforms.

- **Granular Order and Transaction Records (Annexes 2 & 3):** This is the most technical part of the regulation, requiring CASPs to record precise details for every order and transaction in a standardised format. Key data points include:

  - **Party Identification:** Unique identification of the client (buyer/seller), the decision-maker for the trade (which can be a person or an algorithm), and the entity executing the trade.

  - **Asset Identification:** The specific crypto-asset must be identified using a standardised code.

  - **Trade Details:** Precise date and time (to the microsecond in UTC), quantity, price, order type, validity, and capacity in which the CASP acted (e.g., matched principal).

  - **Transmission Data:** If an order is transmitted between CASPs, the chain of transmission must be recorded.

- **On-Chain Data (Annexe 4):** CASPs must capture and link specific on-chain data to their off-chain transactional records. This includes:

  - Transaction hash.

  - Buyer and seller wallet addresses.

  - Smart contract addresses are involved.

  - Network fees and other blockchain-specific data.

**Standardisation and Identification:**

To ensure data is consistent and comparable across the EU, the regulation imposes strict standards for identification:

- **Legal Entities (Clients & CASPs):** Must be identified with a **Legal Entity Identifier (LEI)** compliant with the ISO 17442 standard. CASPs are obligated to ensure that their clients have an LEI, even if it means obtaining one on their behalf.

- **Natural Persons (Clients & Traders):** Must be identified by a unique code created by combining their country of nationality's ISO code with a specified national identifier (e.g., national ID card number, passport number), following a strict priority system.

- **Crypto-Assets:** Must be identified using a **Digital Token Identifier (DTI)** compliant with the ISO 24165 standard, or an equivalent approved by the European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA).

- **Algorithms:** Trading and execution algorithms must be assigned unique identifiers to track their activity.

**Data Retention and Accessibility:**

Records must be retained for a minimum of **five years**, and up to seven years if requested by a competent authority. They must be stored in a secure, immutable (tamper-proof) medium that is easily accessible to regulators and allows for efficient data analysis.

**Firm Implications**

This regulation marks a significant step in formalising the operational and compliance landscape for the crypto industry in the European Union, with profound implications for various stakeholders.

- **For Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs):**

  - **Massive Compliance and Operational Lift:** CASPs must invest heavily in sophisticated IT infrastructure and data governance frameworks. Simple data logging is insufficient; they need to build and maintain structured databases that conform to the dozens of specific fields and formats outlined in the Annexes. This represents a significant technical and financial challenge, especially for smaller entities.

  - **Increased Operating Costs:** The costs of implementing and maintaining these systems, managing vast amounts of data, training staff, and ensuring ongoing compliance (e.g., renewing LEIs for the firm and its clients) will be substantial. This could lead to market consolidation, as firms without sufficient resources may struggle to compete or choose to exit the market.

  - **Shift in Client Onboarding:** The requirement for CASPs to ensure their legal entity clients have an LEI will change client onboarding and lifecycle management processes. This places a direct data-gathering and administrative burden on the CASP.

  - **Heightened Scrutiny and Liability:** With such detailed records available to regulators, CASPs are under a microscope. Any failure to comply or data indicating market abuse or operational failings can be easily identified and acted upon. The regulation removes ambiguity and significantly increases accountability.

  - **End of the "Wild West":** The rules force a convergence with traditional finance (TradFi) standards. The mandate to use established identifiers, such as LEI and DTI, along with detailed reporting requirements, brings the crypto market into a mature, regulated framework, thereby eliminating regulatory arbitrage for EU-based firms.

- **For Regulators and Market Supervision:**

  - **Dramatically Enhanced Supervisory Capabilities:** Competent authorities will gain unprecedented, standardised insight into the crypto market. This enables data-driven supervision, allowing for large-scale, automated analysis of market activity across the EU.

  - **Effective Market Abuse Detection:** By requiring the identification of the specific person or algorithm behind every decision and tracking the full lifecycle of orders and transactions (including on-chain data), regulators are far better equipped to detect and investigate complex market manipulation and insider dealing schemes.

- **For the Broader Crypto-Asset Market:**

  - **Increased Institutionalisation and Trust:** While the compliance burden is high, the transparency and integrity it fosters are crucial for attracting institutional capital. The regulation enhances the legitimacy of the crypto market, making it a more trusted and stable environment for large-scale investment.

  - **Harmonisation within the EU:** The regulation creates a level playing field for all licensed CASPs in the EU, ensuring consistent standards and preventing a race to the bottom in terms of compliance.

  - **Pressure on Anonymity:** The explicit requirement to link on-chain wallet addresses and transaction hashes to identified off-chain entities (both natural persons and legal entities) reinforces the EU's firm stance against anonymity in regulated financial services. This solidifies the "travel rule" for crypto transactions and establishes a clear compliance boundary between regulated Central Asset Service Providers (CASPs) and the more anonymous aspects of decentralised finance (DeFi).

### MiCA: Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/1141 of 27 February 2025 supplements Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 by outlining conflict of interest policies for asset-referenced token issuers.

This Delegated Regulation supplements the main Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) Regulation by establishing specific and detailed requirements for the **conflicts of interest policies** that **issuers of asset-referenced tokens (ARTs) must implement**. ARTs are a type of stablecoin backed by a reserve of assets.

The core objective is to protect both the holders of the ARTs and the issuer's integrity by ensuring that potential conflicts are rigorously identified, prevented, managed, and disclosed.

**Key Requirements and Scope:**

The regulation requires ART issuers to create, implement, and maintain a formal, written conflicts of interest policy. This policy must be comprehensive and proportionate to the issuer's size, nature, and complexity.

1. **Identification of Conflicts:** Issuers must identify a wide range of potential conflicts that could harm either the **token holders** or the **issuer**. The regulation highlights several high-risk areas:

    - **Management of the Reserve of Assets:** Conflicts arising from how the reserve is invested, custodied, or managed, especially if it involves related parties.

    - **Transactions with Insiders:** Scrutiny of any transaction (e.g., issuance or redemption of tokens) between the issuer and its "connected persons" (management, employees, major shareholders, their relatives, etc.).

    - **Remuneration Policies:** Ensuring that compensation structures for staff and management do not create incentives that are detrimental to token holders.

    - **Relationships:** Identifying conflicts arising from personal, professional, or political relationships between connected persons and external parties.

    - **Third-Party Arrangements:** Managing conflicts with entities providing key services, such as custody of the reserve or distribution of the tokens.

2. **Policies and Procedures:** The management body of the issuer is ultimately responsible for the policy, which must include:

    - Transparent processes for preventing and managing identified conflicts, such as controlling information flow and ensuring independent decision-making.

    - Measures to prevent members of the management body from holding similar positions in competing ART issuers.

    - A requirement to keep detailed records of all identified conflicts and the mitigation measures taken, with a retention period of 5 years.

    - Effective internal and external communication channels and staff training.

3. **Independent Management:** Issuers must appoint a specific person or function responsible for managing conflicts of interest. This role must be independent from the business functions, have sufficient authority and resources, and be able to report directly to the management body.

4. **Disclosure:** While disclosure is mandatory, the regulation makes it clear that **disclosure is not a substitute for management**. It is a last resort for conflicts that cannot be prevented or mitigated. Issuers must publicly disclose on their website:

    - The general nature and sources of conflicts of interest.

    - The steps taken to minimise them.

    - This information must be kept up to date and provided in the official language of their home Member State, as well as a language "customary in the sphere of international finance" (i.e., English).

**Firm Implications**

This regulation imposes a stringent governance framework on ART issuers, bringing them significantly closer to the standards expected of traditional financial institutions, such as banks or fund managers.

- **For Issuers of Asset-Referenced Tokens (ARTs):**

  1. **Mandatory Governance Overhaul:** Issuers cannot operate with loose, informal structures. They are legally required to establish a robust, board-approved governance framework with a dedicated, independent compliance function for conflicts of interest. This is a significant operational and cultural shift for many crypto-native organisations.

  2. **Severe Constraints on Reserve Management:** The rules severely restrict how the reserve of assets can be managed. Issuers will face intense scrutiny if they invest the reserve in assets linked to themselves or their related parties. This forces a conservative and independent investment strategy for the reserve, prioritising the stability and redeemability of the token above all else.

  3. **Increased Liability for Management and Directors:** The regulation places direct responsibility on the management body for creating, implementing, and ensuring the effectiveness of the policy. This elevates the personal liability of senior executives and board members, who can be held accountable for any failings.

  4. **Business Model Limitations:** The policy scrutinises transactions with all "connected persons." This can complicate operations for issuers that are part of a larger, vertically integrated group that might also provide crypto-asset services (like exchanges or custody). All intra-group dealings must be demonstrably at arm's length and fair to token holders.

  5. **Higher Operational Costs and Complexity:** Implementing and maintaining these policies, including the required record-keeping, training, and independent oversight function, will significantly increase compliance costs and administrative burden.

- **For Token Holders and the Market:**

  1. **Greatly Enhanced Holder Protection:** This is the most significant favourable implication. The rules are designed to protect the value of the token by safeguarding the integrity of the reserve of assets. Holders can have greater confidence that the issuer is not misusing the reserve for its benefit or taking risks that could jeopardise the token's stability.

  2. **Establishes a "Gold Standard" for Stablecoins:** This regulation sets a high bar for governance in the stablecoin sector within the EU. It will differentiate highly-regulated ARTs from other, less transparent stablecoins, potentially making them more attractive to institutional and risk-averse users.

  3. **Increased Transparency, But Not as a Crutch:** While the disclosure rules provide valuable information, the regulation’s emphasis that disclosure is not sufficient to manage a conflict is crucial. It prevents issuers from simply disclosing away a serious conflict and forces them to actively avoid or manage it, which is a much stronger form of protection for holders.

### MiCA: Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/1142 of 27 February 2025 supplements Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 on standards for conflicts of interest policies, disclosure content, and methodology for crypto-asset service providers.

This Delegated Regulation supplements the main Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) Regulation. It establishes a detailed and legally binding framework for how all **Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs)**—such as exchanges, brokers, custodians, and advisors—must identify, prevent, manage, and disclose conflicts of interest.

The regulation’s primary goal is to protect clients and ensure market integrity by addressing the wide range of conflicts inherent in the provision of crypto-asset services.

**Key Requirements and Scope:**

The regulation requires every CASP to establish a formal, written conflicts of interest policy that is proportionate to the scale and nature of its services. The management body is directly responsible for its adoption, implementation, and regular review.

- **Identification of Conflicts:** CASPs must identify all actual or potential conflicts that could harm the interests of a client or the CASP itself. The regulation highlights explicitly risks arising from:

  - **Combined Business Functions:** When a CASP or its group performs multiple roles that have conflicting incentives (e.g., operating a trading platform while also engaging in proprietary trading or market-making).

  - **Remuneration:** Compensation policies for employees or management that could incentivise them to favour their own or the firm's interests over a client's.

  - **Personal Transactions:** The personal trading activities of employees, management, and their close relations ("connected persons").

  - **Inducements:** Receiving benefits from third parties that could compromise the CASP's duty to its clients.

  - **Placing Services:** Specific conflicts that arise when a CASP helps an issuer offer new tokens to the public (similar to underwriting).

  - **Group Structures:** Conflicts arising from the business activities of other entities within the same corporate group.

- **Prevention and Management Procedures:** The policy must include specific measures to address identified conflicts. The regulation mandates particular procedures, including:

  - **Information Barriers:** Preventing the exchange of sensitive information between departments with conflicting interests (e.g., between a proprietary trading desk and a client advisory desk).

  - **Separate Supervision:** Ensuring independent oversight of staff performing functions with conflicting objectives.

  - **Remuneration Controls:** Removing any direct link between the pay of employees in one function and the revenues of another conflicting function.

  - **Rules for Personal Trading:** Implementing strict procedures to monitor and control the personal crypto transactions of connected persons to prevent abuse.

  - **Independent Conflict Management Function:** Appointing a person or function with the necessary independence, authority, and resources to oversee the entire conflicts of interest framework, reporting directly to the management body.

- **Disclosure as a Last Resort:** While CASPs must disclose conflicts to their clients, the regulation explicitly states that **disclosure alone is insufficient as a mitigation strategy**. It is a measure of last resort to be used only when the firm’s organisational and administrative arrangements are not adequate to prevent a conflict from damaging a client's interests. Disclosures must be:

  - Detailed, specific, and precise.

  - Kept up-to-date and displayed prominently on the CASP's website.

  - Provided in all languages, the CASP uses to market its services.

**Firm Implications**

This regulation imposes the rigorous governance standards of traditional finance onto the crypto services industry, fundamentally challenging some of its most common business models.

- **For Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs):**

  - **Direct Challenge to the Vertically Integrated Model:** This is the most significant implication. The standard crypto exchange model—where a single entity acts as the platform, broker, custodian, market maker, and sometimes even trades on its behalf—comes under direct regulatory scrutiny. CASPs must now implement strict internal separations (information barriers, separate management) that functionally "unbundle" these conflicting activities. Failure to do so would make it impossible to manage conflicts in line with the rules.

  - **Mandatory Professionalisation of Compliance:** CASPs can no longer treat compliance as a secondary function. They are legally required to build a professionalised governance structure with an independent compliance function that has absolute authority, adequate resources, and a direct line to the board. This necessitates significant investment in experienced personnel and sophisticated monitoring systems.

  - **End of Unregulated Employee Trading:** The rules on personal account dealing mean the days of employees freely trading on their own or other platforms are over. CASPs must implement strict monitoring, pre-clearance, and reporting systems for employee trading, similar to those at investment banks. This represents a major cultural shift for the industry.

  - **Business Model Reassessment:** The high cost and complexity of managing these conflicts may force CASPs to rethink their strategies. Some may choose to specialise in specific services (e.g., pure custody or execution-only brokerage) rather than offering a complete, integrated suite, as the latter now carries a much heavier regulatory and operational burden.

- **For Clients and the Market:**

  - **Substantial Increase in Client Protection:** Clients receive robust new protections against practices that have plagued the crypto industry, such as platforms trading against them, front-running orders, or prioritising their proprietary interests. The rules force CASPs to act in the best interests of their clients.

  - **Unprecedented Transparency:** Clients will gain a much clearer understanding of a CASP's business model and its inherent conflicts. The requirement for a CASP to be explicit about the various "hats" it wears will empower clients to make more informed decisions about where they do business.

  - **Forced Maturity of the EU Crypto Market:** This regulation effectively outlaws the "casino"-style operating models that have led to major scandals and collapses globally. It forces a level of structural and ethical discipline that will weed out poorly managed operators and drive the professionalisation of the entire EU crypto ecosystem.

### MiCA: European Commission adopts Delegated Regulation on RTS on authorisation for issuers of ARTs under MiCA.

The European Commission has adopted a **Delegated Regulation (EU) C(2025) 3221 final** on June 5, 2025. This regulation supplements the broader Regulation (EU) 2023/1114 (Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, or MiCA) by **specifying the detailed information required for an application for authorisation to offer asset-referenced tokens (ARTs) to the public or to seek their admission to trading in the EU.**

**Summary of the Delegated Regulation**

This regulation, developed by the European Banking Authority (EBA) in collaboration with the European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) and the European Central Bank (ECB), outlines the comprehensive information that legal entities (excluding credit institutions) must provide when applying to issue Asset-Backed Securities (ARTs). The key areas of information required are:

- **Applicant Issuer Identification:** Full legal name, trading name, website, legal entity identifier (LEI), legal form, incorporation details, registered office, registration numbers, instruments of constitution, accounting year-end, and contact details for the application.

- **Programme of Operations (Business Model, Strategy, and Risk Profile):**

  - Main features of the ART: Name, type, mechanism of issuance (including smart contracts and payment methods), distribution channels, redemption mechanisms, DLT protocol, and interoperability.

  - Existing crypto-assets: Details of any other tokens issued by the applicant.

  - Other activities: Any other financial or non-financial activities and their interactions.

  - Group overview: If part of a group, details of its structure and activities.

  - Business environment: Analysis of market players, likely developments, and competitive position.

  - Overall business strategy: Strategic goals, key drivers, competitive advantages (e.g., DLT specificities), target customers, and geographical distribution.

  - Risk assessment: Comprehensive assessment of business, operational, ICT, cybersecurity, financial (liquidity, market, credit), third-party provider, and **money laundering/terrorist financing (ML/TF) risks.**

- **Financial Information on the Business Plan:**

  - Forecasts: Three-year financial forecasts (balance sheets, profit/loss, cash flow) under baseline and stress scenarios (especially for significant ARTs).

  - Assumptions: Credible and realistic planning assumptions, including expected token holders, transaction volumes, and profitability drivers.

  - Own Funds Requirements: Calculations for Regulatory Capital.

  - Capital evidence: Supporting evidence of issued, paid-up, and unpaid capital, including the legitimate origin of funds.

  - Reserve of assets: Forecast calculations of the amount and composition of the reserve.

  - Past financial information: Statutory financial statements for the last three years, indebtedness, security interests, and credit ratings.

- **Internal Governance Arrangements and Structural Organisation:**

  - Organisational chart: Operational structure, reporting lines, and responsibilities.

  - Management body: Terms of reference, roles, duties, and reporting lines.

  - Human and technical resources: Foreseen number, profile, skills, and expertise, adequacy for the business plan.

  - Data reporting: Procedures for accurate and timely data reporting.

  - Policies: Code of conduct, complaints handling (as per Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/293), and conflicts of interest (as per related delegated regulation).

  - Disclosure: Procedures to ensure compliance with disclosure requirements to token holders.

  - Third-party arrangements: Details of critical functions outsourced, including rationale, location, resources, control systems, business continuity plans, and contractual terms.

- **Internal Control Framework:**

  - Functions: Description of internal compliance, risk management, and internal audit functions, ensuring their independence and resources.

  - Risk management systems: Strategy for identifying, assessing, monitoring, mitigating, and reporting all risks, including ICT, reputational, legal, and ML/TF risks, and the risk appetite statement.

  - ICT Systems: Detailed technical documentation on ICT risk management framework, updated systems, security policy, and data reporting capabilities, demonstrating compliance with **Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (DORA).**

  - Business Continuity Plan: Mapping of essential data/functions, back-up/recovery systems, and key staff availability.

  - Proprietary DLT: For proprietary DLTs, details on legal title, operators, risk plans, technical/security audit reports, and transparency mechanisms.

  - AML/CTF compliance: For crypto-asset service providers involved, a detailed description of internal control mechanisms and a forward-looking assessment of compliance with AML/CTF obligations.

- **Liquidity Management, Reserve of Assets, and Redemption Rights:**

  - Reserve framework: A Comprehensive framework for the constitution, composition, management, and segregation of the reserve of assets.

  - Stabilisation mechanism: A Detailed policy describing how the ART's value will be stabilised.

  - External audit: Name of the external consultant for independent audits of the reserve.

  - Custody and investment policies: Detailed policies ensuring compliance with relevant MiCA articles.

  - Third-party arrangements: Details of contractual arrangements for operation, investment, and custody of the reserve, including legal and operational separation.

  - Redemption rights: Policies and procedures ensuring the respect of token holders' redemption rights.

  - Recovery and Redemption Plans: Outline of the recovery plan and the redemption plan.

- **Suitability of Management Body:**

  - Personal details: Full name, birth details, address, nationality, ID, and position details.

  - Curriculum Vitae: Education and professional experience, particularly in financial services, crypto-assets, DLT, IT, cybersecurity, and digital innovation.

  - Personal history: Absence of criminal record, penalties, refusals of authorisation, dismissals, and any prior suitability assessments by other authorities.

  - Conflicts of interest: Description of financial and non-financial interests that could create conflicts, and mitigation strategies.

  - Time commitment: Estimated time devoted, list of commercial mandates, and non-commercial duties.

  - Suitability assessment: Results of the applicant issuer's evaluation of each member and the collective suitability of the management body.

- **Shareholders with Qualifying Holdings:**

  - Holding structure: Chart showing capital breakdown and direct/indirect qualifying holdings.

  - Identity and reputation: Information and documents for each shareholder/member as per related delegated regulations.

  - Appointed management body members: Identity of management body members appointed by or nominated by such shareholders.

  - Holding details: Number, type, nominal value, premium, and security interests over holdings.

  - Financial information: As per related delegated regulations.

**Firm Implications**

This Delegated Regulation has significant implications for any firm looking to issue Asset-Referenced Tokens (ARTs) in the European Union:

- **Highly Detailed Application Process:** Firms must prepare for an exceptionally rigorous and detailed application process. The document mandates extensive disclosures across all facets of the business, from foundational legal and financial structures to operational specifics, risk management, and personnel suitability.

- **Increased Compliance Burden:** There's a substantial increase in the compliance burden. Firms need to ensure their internal structures, policies, and procedures align with the granular requirements, particularly concerning internal controls, risk management (including ML/TF), ICT resilience (DORA compliance), and management suitability.

- **Enhanced Due Diligence on Personnel and Shareholders:** The regulation requires in-depth background checks on members of the management body and significant shareholders, including verification of their criminal records, professional history, and potential conflicts of interest. This means firms must conduct thorough due diligence before proposing individuals for these roles.

- **Robust Financial Planning and Stress Testing:** Firms must present credible three-year financial forecasts, including stress scenarios, to demonstrate economic viability and ability to meet prudential requirements. This necessitates sophisticated financial modelling capabilities.

- **Transparency and Accountability for Reserve Assets:** Clear and detailed policies are required for the composition, management, segregation, custody, and investment of the reserve assets backing the ARTs. Firms must also commit to regular independent audits of these reserves.

- **Strong Governance and Internal Controls:** Emphasis is placed on robust internal governance arrangements, clear organisational structures, independent internal control functions (including compliance, risk management, and internal audit), and effective complaint handling mechanisms.

- **Importance of Third-Party Vendor Management:** If firms rely on third-party service providers for critical functions (e.g., DLT operation, reserve asset custody, distribution), they must provide extensive details on these arrangements, including rationale, oversight, and business continuity plans. Contracts with critical third-party providers must be robust enough to ensure continuity, even during redemption plans.

- **Data Protection Considerations:** The regulation acknowledges the collection of personal data and emphasises compliance with the GDPR and relevant data protection principles, such as data minimisation.

- **EU Establishment Requirement:** The regulation clarifies that only legal persons or undertakings established in the European Union (EU) are eligible to apply for authorisation. This means non-EU firms wishing to offer ARTs in the EU must establish a legal entity within the EU.

- **Forward-Looking Assessment for AML/CTF:** Firms must provide a forward-looking assessment of continuous compliance with AML/CTF obligations, particularly when involving crypto-asset service providers in their business model.

In essence, this Delegated Regulation significantly raises the bar for market entry for ART issuers in the EU, requiring a level of institutional maturity, transparency, and operational resilience comparable to that of traditional financial institutions. Firms will need to invest heavily in legal, compliance, economic, and technological expertise to navigate these requirements successfully.

### Digital Markets: ESMA report on DLT Pilot Regime

The ESMA (European Securities and Markets Authority) report on the functioning and review of the DLT (Distributed Ledger Technology) Pilot Regime, published on June 25, 2025, highlights the initial, albeit limited, uptake of DLT in EU financial markets. The regime aims to foster DLT development by offering temporary regulatory exemptions for DLT-based market infrastructures.

The report, mandated by Article 14 of the DLTR (Regulation (EU) 2022/858), assesses the functioning of the regime, including technical deployment, market performance, regulatory exemptions, and systemic risks.

**Key Findings:**

- **Limited Uptake but Encouraging Experimentation:** Only three infrastructures (CSD Prague, 21X AG, and 360X AG) have been authorised, with minimal live trading. However, the regime has stimulated experimentation in DLT-based trading, settlement, and compliance, particularly for smaller issuers and innovative asset types.

- **Operational and Legal Frictions:** The report identifies challenges, including a lack of interoperability with traditional financial market infrastructures (e.g., CSDs, RTGS systems) and limited access to central bank money for settlement.

- **Restrictive Thresholds:** The current thresholds for eligible financial instruments and aggregate market value are perceived as too restrictive, limiting broader participation from larger institutional players and well-established instruments.

- **Varying DLT Implementations:**

  - **CSD Prague:** Operates as a DLT Settlement System (DLT SS) using a permissioned, private DLT environment (R3 Corda Enterprise) for securities registration, settlement, and asset servicing. It focuses on domestic issuers, particularly SMEs.

  - **21X AG:** Operates as a DLT Trading and Settlement System (DLT TSS) on a public, permissionless blockchain (Polygon PoS) with permissioned access via smart contracts, integrating trading and settlement services for tokenised securities. It aims for real-time, low-cost settlement.

  - **360X AG:** Operates as a DLT MTF (Multilateral Trading Facility) but does not directly use DLT for its trading functions. Instead, it leverages Clearstream Banking AG's D7 platform (a private, permissioned DLT-based system) for the initial recording of DLT financial instruments. Settlement occurs through Clearstream's conventional infrastructure.

- **Risk Management:** All authorised DLT MIs have implemented formal risk management procedures, with varying approaches tailored to their DLT architecture. No investor losses or major system failures have been reported to date, though trading activity has been minimal.

- **Regulatory Arbitrage and Level Playing Field:** While exemptions are intended to foster innovation, they raise concerns about competitive neutrality with traditional market infrastructures. However, ESMA assesses the risk of material regulatory arbitrage as low due to the compensatory measures required by the DLTR.

- **Costs and Benefits:** DLT offers potential operational efficiencies, reduced settlement times, and new access channels for SMEs. However, high set-up costs, lack of integrated cash settlement mechanisms, and scalability constraints remain significant barriers.

**Recommendations:**

ESMA provides short-term and long-term recommendations to the European Commission:

- **Short-term:**

  - **Codify the Pilot Regime permanently and remove the maximum six-year license duration:** To provide regulatory certainty and incentivise long-term investment.

  - **Increase existing thresholds or introduce tiered, adjustable thresholds:** To encourage greater market participation, particularly from large institutional investors and issuers, and allow NCAs to tailor requirements based on risk profiles.

  - **Enhance investor protection and broaden the scope of eligible assets:** Harmonise retail investor protection arrangements and consider extending the scope to include more complex instruments for sophisticated investors, with appropriate MiFID II safeguards.

  - **Prioritise central bank money for settlement:** While permitting commercial bank money and EMTs, subject to stricter thresholds for EMTs due to additional risks.

- **Long-term:**

  - **Enable continuity of operations and scaling:** Allow DLT MIs to continue operations beyond initial thresholds if risks are appropriately mitigated.

  - **Establish a pathway toward a permanent DLT regulatory framework:** Integrate DLT-specific provisions into existing sectoral frameworks or create a dedicated permanent regime for DLT MIs, ensuring technological neutrality and "same risk, same rules" principles.

  - **Clarify the regulatory treatment of DLT TSS entities:** Potentially introduce a new regulatory category with functional requirements for combined trading and settlement entities, focusing on outcomes rather than traditional role-based distinctions.

**Firm Implications**

The ESMA report has several key implications for firms operating or considering operating within the DLT space in the EU:

- **Increased Regulatory Certainty (Potential):** If ESMA's recommendations are adopted, the permanent codification of the DLT Pilot Regime and removal of the six-year license limit would significantly reduce regulatory uncertainty. This would incentivise **long-term investment** in DLT solutions and allow firms to plan for sustained operations rather than a temporary test phase.

- **Opportunity for Scaled Operations:** Raising the current restrictive thresholds for market capitalisation and issue size would open up the DLT Pilot Regime to a much broader range of financial instruments and larger market participants. This means **firms could target a wider client base and handle higher volumes of DLT financial instruments**, making their DLT initiatives more commercially viable.

- **Tailored Regulation and Flexibility:** The proposal for tiered or adjustable thresholds, where NCAs can calibrate requirements based on a firm's specific risk profile, offers greater flexibility and adaptability. This could lead to a **more proportionate regulatory burden**, especially for smaller firms and startups, while ensuring that appropriate safeguards are in place for larger, more systemically essential operations.

- **Focus on Interoperability and Cash Settlement:** The report highlights the ongoing challenges with interoperability between DLT market infrastructures and traditional systems (CSDs, RTGS). Firms will need to **prioritise developing solutions for seamless integration** with existing financial infrastructure. Furthermore, the lack of central bank money for DLT settlement remains a key friction. Firms relying on commercial bank money or e-money tokens must be aware of the associated risks and potential for stricter thresholds.

- **Enhanced Investor Protection Requirements:** Regardless of threshold changes or expanded asset scope, firms must expect a continued, and potentially stricter, focus on investor protection. Suppose direct retail access or more complex instruments are permitted. In that case, firms will need to ensure that they conduct **robust and transparent onboarding, suitability, and appropriateness assessments that are aligned with MiFID II standards.**

- **Importance of Risk Management Frameworks:** While no major incidents have occurred, the report emphasises the ongoing need for robust risk management frameworks tailored to DLT's unique legal, technical, and operational risks. Firms must continue to **invest in cybersecurity, smart contract auditing, and clear operational continuity plans** to ensure their resilience and maintain a robust security posture.

- **Potential for New Business Models (DLT TSS):** The report's recognition of the DLT TSS, which combines trading and settlement, could pave the way for a new, dedicated regulatory category. Firms exploring **integrated, end-to-end DLT solutions** will find this development encouraging, as it could lead to more tailored and efficient regulatory oversight for such models.

- **Competition and Level Playing Field:** While ESMA believes the risk of significant regulatory arbitrage is low due to compensatory measures, firms should remain aware that the differences in regulatory burdens between DLT MIs and traditional infrastructures may still be a point of discussion. This could impact future **competitive strategies and investment decisions**.

In essence, the report signals a clear intent from ESMA to evolve the DLT Pilot Regime from a limited experiment to a more robust and permanent framework. Firms that proactively address the identified frictions and align their strategies with ESMA's recommendations are likely well-positioned for future growth in the tokenised financial instruments market.

### Digital Markets: European Parliament: US digital asset strategy and the European response

- Establishing a government task force to regulate cryptocurrency markets

However, to ensure this, the EU's crypto market regulation (MiCA) and the euro's status as a legal tender may need to be strengthened. The authors express scepticism that a retail digital euro, as currently planned, would significantly contribute to protecting Europe's monetary sovereignty. However, they acknowledge the benefits of wholesale central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) for cross-border payments.

**What it means for firms:**

- **Weakened US Monetary Control:** The widespread adoption of dollar-backed stablecoins could reduce the share of payment instruments in the traditional monetary policy transmission chain, making monetary control more challenging and policy transmission less predictable.

- **Jeopardised Dollar Unicity:** Stablecoins, even if pegged, oscillate in value, which could threaten the dollar's unicity as a unit of account and introduce financial instability due to the risk of runs.

- **Potential for Financial Instability:** Stablecoins, like Money Market Funds (MMFs), face liquidity transformation risks. If not tightly regulated and supervised like banks, their increased role could make the financial system more fragile and lead to systemic risk if sponsors emerge.

- **Limited Impact on Europe's Monetary Sovereignty (Direct):** The report argues that direct "dollarisation" of the Euro area due to stablecoins is highly unlikely, as the primary drivers for currency adoption are trade integration and monetary policy credibility, neither of which supports a shift to the dollar in the euro area

- **Need for EU Regulatory Strengthening (Indirect):** Although the direct impact on Europe's monetary sovereignty is deemed low, the report recommends strengthening the euro's legal tender status and amending MiCA to clarify the distinctions between legal tender instruments and privately issued instruments, such as stablecoins. This would help prevent the erosion of monetary control if euro-denominated stablecoins gain traction.

- **Scepticism on the Retail Digital Euro for Monetary Sovereignty:** The report does not view the digital euro (in its retail version, as currently planned) as a significant defence for Europe's monetary sovereignty against DAS or other risks, due to its limited scope, size, and uncertain commercial success.

- **Benefits of Wholesale CBDCs:** Wholesale CBDCs are seen as beneficial for improving cross-border payment infrastructure and anchoring the role of central banks in the digital trading of financial assets

### Digital Markets: Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 published

The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (DUA Act 2025) received Royal Assent on 19 June 2025 and introduces several key amendments to existing UK data protection legislation.

1. **Amendments to the UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018:**

    - **Scientific and Historical Research & Statistical Purposes:**

      - The DUA Act 2025 clarifies the definitions of "scientific research," "historical research," and "statistical purposes" as outlined in Article 4 of the UK GDPR.

      - "Scientific research" now explicitly includes technological development, fundamental research, applied research, and public health studies, which are scientifically describable.

      - "Historical research" now includes genealogical research.

      - "Statistical purposes" refers to processing for statistical surveys or results where the output is aggregate data (not personal data), and the data is not used for decisions about a particular data subject.

      - Consent for scientific research is broadened to allow for less specific purposes when complete identification of purposes is not possible at the time of consent, provided it aligns with ethical standards.

      - New provisions (Article 84B and 84C) are introduced requiring appropriate safeguards for processing personal data for research, archiving in the public interest, or statistical purposes (RAS purposes). 7These safeguards must prevent substantial damage or distress to data subjects and include technical and organisational measures for data minimisation, such as pseudonymisation.

    - **Lawfulness of Processing and Legitimate Interests:**

      - The UK GDPR Article 6 is amended to introduce a new lawful basis for processing: "processing is necessary for a recognised legitimate interest." This is further detailed in a new Annexe 1, which outlines specific conditions for meeting this requirement, including national security, public security, defence, emergencies, crime prevention, and safeguarding vulnerable individuals.

      - The Secretary of State can amend Annexe 1 through regulations, subject to an affirmative resolution procedure, taking into account the rights and freedoms of data subjects, particularly children.

    - **Purpose Limitation:**

      - The DUA Act 2025 modifies Article 5(1)(b) of the UK GDPR regarding purpose limitation, clarifying that further processing must be compatible with the original purpose for which the data was collected.

      - A new Article 8A outlines factors to consider when determining compatibility, including the link between purposes, the context of collection, the nature of processing, and the safeguards in place.

      - Processing for new purposes is considered compatible if the data subject consents, or if it's for scientific/historical research, archiving in the public interest, statistical purposes (by Article 84B), compliance demonstration, or to safeguard public objectives (listed in Article 23(1)(c) to (j)) and authorised by law.

    - **Automated Decision-Making:**

      - Article 22 of the UK GDPR is replaced with new Articles 22A, 22B, 22C, and 22D, which redefine and restrict automated individual decision-making, including profiling.

      - Significant decisions based solely on automated processing are restricted, especially if they involve special categories of personal data, unless explicit consent is given or it's contractually or legally necessary and falls under Article 9(2)(g).

      - Safeguards must be in place, providing data subjects with information about decisions, the ability to make representations, obtain human intervention, and contest decisions.

      - The Secretary of State can make regulations to define "meaningful human involvement" and "similarly significant effect" for automated decisions, and to impose additional safeguards.

    - **International Transfers:**

      - Chapter 5 of the UK GDPR (general processing) and Chapter 5 of Part 3 of the 2018 Act (law enforcement processing) are amended regarding transfers of personal data to third countries and international organisations.

      - New Articles 44A, 45A, 45B, and 45C (in the UK GDPR) and 74AA, 74AB, and 74B (in the 2018 Act) introduce a "data protection test" for approving transfers, ensuring the standard of protection is not "materially lower" than that in the UK.

      - The Secretary of State will monitor developments in third countries and international organisations to ensure continued adequacy.

    - **Information Commissioner's Role:**

      - The DUA Act 2025 abolishes the office of Information Commissioner and establishes a new body corporate called the "Information Commission."

      - The functions of the Information Commissioner are transferred to the Information Commissioner.

      - New duties are imposed on the Commissioner, including a principal objective to secure an appropriate level of protection for personal data and promote public trust, while also considering innovation, competition, and public security.

      - The Commissioner is required to prepare and publish a strategy for carrying out functions and consult with other regulators.

      - New provisions for codes of conduct for general and law enforcement processing are introduced, encouraging expert public bodies to create such codes.

2. **Amendments to the Privacy and Electronic Communications (EC Directive) Regulations 2003 (PECR):**

    - **Interpretation:**

      - PECR Regulation 2 is amended to clarify definitions of "call," "communication," and "direct marketing."

      - References to periods will now be interpreted in accordance with EU Regulation No. 1182/71.

    - **Personal Data Breach Notification:**

      - Regulation 5A is amended to require notification of a personal data breach to the Commissioner "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours after having become aware of it," with reasons for delay if missed.

    - **Storing Information (Cookies):**

      - Regulation 6 on storing information (like cookies) in terminal equipment is replaced with new Schedule A1.

      - The new Schedule details exceptions to the prohibition on storing or accessing information without consent, including for transmission over an electronic communications network, strictly necessary services, statistical purposes (with clear information and an opt-out option), and website appearance and functionality (with clear information and an opt-out option).

    - **Direct Marketing by Charities:**

      - Regulation 22 is amended to allow charities to send direct marketing electronic mail under certain conditions, including that the sole purpose is charitable, contact details are obtained during the expression of interest or support, and a simple opt-out mechanism is provided.

3. **Other Key Provisions:**

    - **National Underground Asset Register (NUAR):** Part 3 establishes and makes provisions for a National Underground Asset Register for England, Wales, and Northern Ireland. This includes duties for undertakers to provide information, access to NUAR data, and fees, as well as monetary penalties for non-compliance.

    - **Registers of Births and Deaths:** Part 4 amends the Births and Deaths Registration Act 1953, allowing registers to be kept in non-hard copy form and introducing requirements for signing registers electronically.

    - **Retention of Biometric Data:** Sections 126, 127, and 128 amend the Counter-Terrorism Act 2008 regarding the retention of biometric data, including for recordable-equivalent offences and data obtained from INTERPOL.

    - **Copyright and AI Systems:** Sections 135, 136, and 137 mandate an economic impact assessment and a report on the use of copyrighted works in the development of AI systems, considering policy options, technical measures, and licensing arrangements.

    - **Purported Intimate Images:** Section 138 introduces new offences related to creating or requesting the creation of "purported intimate images" of adults without consent.

### Digital Markets: Digital assets and (electronic) trade documents in private international law

The report highlights that modern technologies, particularly Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT) and digital assets such as cryptocurrency tokens, challenge the traditional territorial premise of private international law. This creates uncertainty regarding international jurisdiction (which country's court should hear a dispute), applicable law (which law should apply), and the recognition and enforcement of judgments across borders.

The paper identifies "omniterritoriality" as a core problem, where digital phenomena have simultaneous and equally valid connections to multiple jurisdictions, making it difficult to pinpoint a single "location" for legal purposes. While many DLT applications (e.g., permissioned systems, those with intermediaries) can be accommodated by existing rules, truly decentralised applications (like some DeFi protocols) pose fundamental challenges to the multilateralist approach to conflict of laws, which assumes a single "objective seat" for legal issues.

The Law Commission proposes:

- **New Power for Free-Standing Information Orders:** To address challenges in identifying unknown fraudsters and tracing misappropriated crypto-tokens in pseudo-anonymous DLT environments, the report proposes a new discretionary power for courts to grant "free-standing information orders." This would allow claimants to obtain necessary information (e.g., identity of potential defendants, location of assets) even if substantive proceedings are not yet fully formulated or intended for England and Wales. This is a response to the current situation where claimants often resort to "placeholder" claims and novel service methods (like NFTs) to get interim relief.

- **Clarifying Property and Tort Gateways:** For property claims, the report proposes that a crypto-token's "location" for jurisdictional purposes should be where it can be effectively controlled at the time proceedings are issued. For tort claims, damage is sustained where the victim was physically present at the time of the damage. Suppose damage results from being denied access to an online account accessible globally, and no single location can be reasonably identified. In that case, the default should be to sue the defendant in their "home court." These interpretations aim to provide clarity and align with underlying legal principles, rather than relying on "geographical fictions."

- **Reforming Section 72 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882:** The report criticises Section 72 as anachronistic.

  - **Section 72(2) (Contractual Obligations/Interpretation):** It proposes amending Section 72(2) to explicitly cover the substantive rights and obligations of parties to a bill of exchange, and to adopt **party autonomy** as the default rule for choice of law. In the absence of a choice, the acceptor's liability would be governed by the law of the place of payment, and secondary parties' liability by their habitual residence.

  - **Section 72(1) (Formal Validity):** It proposes a "menu" approach for formal validity, where a contract on a bill is upheld if it complies with the law governing its substance, the drawer's agreement, the acceptor's contract, or the place of payment. This aims for "pro-formal validity" and simplifies complexities.

  - **Section 72(3) (Duties of Holder):** It suggests reforming this section to distinguish between four sub-rules and to use connecting factors, such as the drawee's habitual residence or the place of payment, rather than the physical location of the bill.

- **Supranational Approach for Omniterritoriality (Long-term):** For truly omniterritorial phenomena where existing rules fail to identify a single applicable law, the report tentatively proposes adopting a "supranational approach." This would involve the courts of England and Wales developing and applying a special body of substantive law focused on the "just disposal of proceedings," taking into account factors like the parties' legitimate expectations, including coding protocols and blockchain conventions. This is seen as a way to avoid arbitrary outcomes and provide certainty where traditional methods are inadequate. However, statutory intervention for this is considered premature.

**Firm Implications**

- **Increased Access to Justice for Digital Asset Victims:** The proposed free-standing information orders could significantly improve victims' ability to investigate and pursue fraud and hacking cases involving digital assets, even when defendants are unknown or outside the jurisdiction.

- **More straightforward, More Predictable Jurisdiction Rules:** The proposed interpretations for property and tort gateways, emphasising practical control for digital assets and physical presence for tortious damage, aim to bring greater clarity and consistency to jurisdictional determinations in crypto-related disputes. This could reduce "muddling through" tactics seen in past cases.

- **Modernisation of Negotiable Instruments Law:** The proposed reforms to Section 72 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 would significantly update a century-old law, aligning it with modern principles of party autonomy in contract and current commercial realities, particularly in the context of electronic trade documents. This could enhance legal certainty for international trade transactions involving digital bills of exchange.

- **Potential for New Legal Frameworks for Decentralised Systems:** The exploration of a "supranational approach" for omniterritorial digital assets signals a recognition that existing legal paradigms are insufficient for truly decentralised environments. This could lead to the development of novel legal principles that prioritise the "just disposal of proceedings" and the legitimate expectations of participants, including the role of "code as a legally relevant fact."

- **Continued Evolution of English and Welsh Common Law:** The report highlights the flexibility of English and Welsh common law in adapting to new technologies, while also identifying areas where statutory reform or clear judicial guidance is needed.

- **International Harmonisation Remains a Goal:** Despite domestic reforms, the report acknowledges that complete clarity and effectiveness, especially for electronic trade documents, will ultimately require international cooperation and harmonisation of laws.

## Hong Kong

### Digital Markets: The government gazettes the Stablecoins Ordinance

The Hong Kong government has gazetted the Stablecoins Ordinance, which establishes a licensing regime for fiat-referenced stablecoin (FRS) issuers. This ordinance was passed by the Legislative Council on May 21, 2025, after being tabled in December 2024. The Stablecoins Ordinance will take effect on a date yet to be appointed by the Secretary for Financial Services and the Treasury.

Key implications of the Stablecoins Ordinance include:

- **Licensing Requirement:** Upon the ordinance's commencement, any entity issuing FRS in Hong Kong, or issuing FRS that references the Hong Kong Dollar (HKD) both within and outside Hong Kong, must obtain a license from the Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA).

- **Scope of Stablecoin Definition:** A "stablecoin" is defined as a cryptographically secured digital representation of value that is expressed as a unit of account or store of economic value, used or intended for use as a medium of exchange, transferable electronically, operated on a distributed ledger, and purports to maintain a stable value by referencing a single asset or a pool of assets. Certain digital representations, such as those issued by central banks or governments, or those already classified as securities, futures contracts, or deposits under other ordinances, are excluded from this definition.

- **"Specified Stablecoin" Definition:** A "specified stablecoin" is a stablecoin that maintains a stable value wholly or partly by referencing one or more official currencies, specified units of account, specified stores of economic value, or a combination thereof. The Monetary Authority may also specify other digital representations of value to be "specified stablecoins."

- **Regulated Stablecoin Activity:** This includes issuing a specified stablecoin in Hong Kong in the course of business, or issuing a specified stablecoin outside Hong Kong that references HKD. The Monetary Authority can also select other activities that fall under this definition, considering their materiality to Hong Kong's monetary or financial stability, its function as an international financial centre, and significant public interest matters.

- **Offences and Penalties:**

  - Carrying on regulated stablecoin activity or holding oneself out as doing so without a license (unless exempted) is an offence, with penalties including a fine of up to HKD 5,000,000 and 7 years' imprisonment on indictment.

  - Offering a specified stablecoin or holding out as doing so without proper authorisation is also an offence, with similar penalties.

  - Fraud and deception related to specified stablecoin transactions can result in fines of up to HKD 10,000,000 and a 10-year imprisonment. The court may also prohibit the convicted person from acquiring, disposing of, offering, subscribing for, or underwriting any specified stablecoin in Hong Kong for up to 5 years.

  - Inducing others to enter into agreements involving specified stablecoins through fraudulent or reckless misrepresentation is an offence, with penalties including a fine of up to HKD 1,000,000 and a term of imprisonment of up to 7 years.

- **Exemptions:** The Monetary Authority may grant exemptions from certain offences related to regulated stablecoin activities and the offering of specified stablecoins if the risks posed are deemed immaterial to holders and the financial system.

- **Licensing Conditions and Obligations:** The Monetary Authority can attach various conditions to a license, including requirements on reserve asset management, financial resources, business activities, and public disclosures. Licensees have duties to pay fees, display their license number, fulfil minimum criteria, and report significant changes or inability to meet obligations.

- **Revocation and Suspension of Licence:** The HKMA can revoke or suspend a licence under specific grounds outlined in Schedule 4, such as failure to fulfil the minimum criteria, insolvency, providing false information, or contravening licence conditions.

- **Control over Licensee's Ownership and Management:** The ordinance includes provisions for the HKMA's consent for controllers, chief executives, directors, managers, and stablecoin managers of licensees, and establishes restrictions and offences related to these.

- **HKMA's Powers:** The HKMA has the powers to require information and documents from licensees and designated stablecoin entities, examine books and accounts, require reports from skilled persons, and issue directions to ensure compliance.

- **Designation of Stablecoin Entities:** The HKMA can designate entities that issue specified stablecoins outside Hong Kong or provide services to stablecoin payment systems, particularly if these activities are material to Hong Kong's financial stability or public interest. Designated entities are subject to specific requirements regarding operating rules, risk management, and financial resources.

- **Transitional Arrangements:** The ordinance includes transitional provisions for entities that were already conducting regulated stablecoin activities before its commencement, allowing them to continue under certain conditions while applying for a license.

- **Amendments to Other Enactments:** The Stablecoins Ordinance also amends other ordinances, such as the Banking Ordinance, Deposit Protection Scheme Ordinance, and Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing Ordinance, to integrate stablecoin regulation into the existing financial regulatory framework.

### Digital Markets: HKMA publishes consultation paper on proposed AML/CFT requirements for regulated stablecoin activities

The Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) has released a consultation paper on proposed Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing (AML/CFT) requirements for stablecoin issuers licensed under the new Stablecoins Ordinance (SO). This paper is intended to be read in conjunction with the SO and the proposed Guideline on Supervision of Licensed Stablecoin Issuers. The HKMA is inviting written comments from market participants and interested parties until June 30, 2025.

**Key Implications and Proposed Requirements:**

- **Background and Scope:** The consultation paper follows the Hong Kong government's commitment to enhancing the virtual asset (VA) regulatory framework, as outlined in the passage of the Virtual Asset Service Providers Bill on 21 May 2025. "Stablecoin" is defined in the SO as a type of VA used as a medium of exchange, purporting to maintain a stable value by referencing an asset or basket of assets, excluding central bank digital currencies. "Regulated stablecoin activity" includes issuing specified stablecoins in Hong Kong or issuing HKD-referenced stablecoins globally, requiring an HKMA license.

- **Risk-Based Approach:** The HKMA emphasises a risk-based approach to AML/CFT, acknowledging the evolving nature of stablecoins and their potential for anonymity, global reach, and use in illicit financial layering. The transparency and traceability of on-chain transactions are noted as mitigating factors.

- **General AML/CFT Requirements:** Licensed stablecoin issuers (licensees) are proposed to implement effective AML/CFT policies and controls, covering institutional ML/TF risk assessments, governance, senior management oversight, internal audit, staff training, combating terrorist financing, financial sanctions, proliferation financing, suspicious transaction reporting, and record-keeping.

- **Stablecoin Issuance (Minting):**

  - Licensees must conduct customer due diligence (CDD) on customers, including those with business relationships and those conducting occasional transactions equal to or above \$8,000.

  - For custodial wallets, licensees must identify the custodial institution and perform due diligence measures similar to those imposed by the SFC on VATPs.

  - For unhosted wallets, additional controls are proposed, including enhanced monitoring of transfers, transferring stablecoins only to reliable unhosted wallets, and (where appropriate) imposing transaction limits.

  - The HKMA is seeking feedback on these proposed measures for both custodial and unhosted wallets.

- **Stablecoin Redemption:**

  - Licensees must conduct CDD on customers before processing redemption requests.

  - Similar to issuance, if a stablecoin holder uses a custodial wallet for redemption, the licensee must conduct due diligence on the custodial institution.

  - If an unhosted wallet is used for redemption, the licensee should implement the same additional controls as for issuance.

  - The HKMA is seeking feedback on these proposed measures for redemption.

- **Ongoing Monitoring:**

  - Licensees should implement risk-based transaction monitoring systems to detect illicit use of stablecoins at issuance and redemption.

  - This includes adopting technological solutions, such as blockchain analytics tools, to track transaction history and identify wallet addresses associated with illicit activities or sanctioned parties.

  - The HKMA is seeking feedback on the need for ongoing monitoring of illegal activities.

- **Stablecoin Transfers (Travel Rule):**

  - When a licensee carries out a stablecoin transfer that falls under the definition of a "virtual asset transfer" in the AMLO, they must comply with the special requirements for VA transfers (the Travel Rule).

  - The HKMA views licensees as potentially acting as ordering, intermediary, or beneficiary institutions in stablecoin transfers.

  - Feedback is requested on the extent to which licensees should comply with the Travel Rule.

- **Secondary Market Monitoring:**

  - Licensees must implement measures to prevent stablecoins from being used for illicit activities in the secondary market.

  - Possible measures include confining primary distribution and redemption to regulated financial institutions (FIs) and virtual asset service providers (VASPs), utilising blockchain analytics tools for ongoing screening of transactions and wallet addresses, blocking sanctioned or illicit wallet addresses, and allowing or adopting a closed-loop approach.

  - The HKMA is seeking feedback on the accountability of licensees for secondary market monitoring and the implementation of these measures.

- **Future AML/CFT Guidance:** The HKMA plans to develop supplementary AML/CFT guidelines for other digital asset activities (e.g., offering stablecoins, providing custodial services for digital assets) conducted by Authorised Institutions and licensed stablecoin issuers, aiming for a level playing field across financial institutions. This will involve further consultation later in 2025.

- **Securities Investor Protection Act of 1970 (SIPA):** Digital asset securities may not meet the definition of "security" under SIPA, meaning that in the event of a broker-dealer failure, SIPA protection likely would not apply, and holders would have only unsecured general creditor claims. This outcome is deemed inconsistent with investor expectations.

- **Control Location Applications:** The Staff have received inquiries about using an issuer or transfer agent as a "control location" for uncertificated securities where ownership records are published via DLT, but the DLT is not the authoritative record.

The Staff reiterate their support for innovation and their commitment to continued engagement with market participants to protect investors, maintain fair markets, and facilitate capital formation.

**Firm Implications**

Although this statement has been withdrawn, its original content provides insight into the SEC's and FINRA's historical concerns and considerations regarding digital asset custody for broker-dealers. The withdrawal itself may imply a new or evolving stance, but based on the content of the withdrawn statement, here are the firm implications it conveyed:

- **Heightened Scrutiny on Digital Asset Custody:** Broker-dealers engaging in or seeking to engage in the custody of digital asset securities face significant regulatory hurdles, primarily due to the unique characteristics of digital assets that challenge established customer protection frameworks.

- **Challenges in Meeting Customer Protection Rule (15c3-3):** The ability to demonstrate "possession or control" over digital asset securities is a significant concern. Issues such as proving exclusive control of private keys, the immutability of blockchain transactions, and the lack of recourse to reverse errors make compliance difficult.

- **Risk of Investor Loss Without SIPA Protection:** Many digital assets, even if deemed securities under federal law, may not qualify for SIPA protection. This exposes investors to the risk of being general unsecured creditors in the event of a broker-dealer failure, an outcome inconsistent with typical investor expectations for broker-dealer accounts.

- **Complexities for Recordkeeping and Auditing:** The nature of DLT makes it challenging for broker-dealers to evidence the existence of digital asset securities for regulatory books, records, and financial statements, complicating audits and compliance monitoring.

- **Non-Custodial Models are Preferred (Historically):** Business models where broker-dealers facilitate digital asset security transactions without taking custody were historically viewed with less concern by the Staff, provided other regulations are met.

- **Innovation and Dialogue Encouraged:** Despite the challenges, staff have historically encouraged innovation and active engagement with market participants to develop compliant custody solutions.

### FinCrime: FATF targeted update on implementation of standards on virtual assets and virtual asset service providers: June 2025

The June 2025 report provides the FATF's sixth review of how jurisdictions across its global network are implementing anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing (AML/CFT) standards for virtual assets (VAs) and VASPs, as required by Recommendation 15 (R.15).

**Key Findings on Implementation**

- **Overall Progress is Slow but Improving:** While the global implementation of R.15 has shown slight improvement since 2024, significant gaps remain. As of April 2025, 29% of assessed jurisdictions are now "largely compliant," up from 25% in 2024, and the proportion of "not compliant" jurisdictions has decreased from 25% to 21%. However, only one jurisdiction is fully "compliant".

- **Risk Assessments:** More jurisdictions have conducted ML/TF risk assessments for VAs and VASPs, with 76% of survey respondents reporting they have done so, an increase from 71% in 2024. However, a key challenge remains in using these assessments to implement adequate, risk-based supervision.

- **Regulatory Approaches:** Most jurisdictions (62%) have decided to permit and regulate VASPs. However, there is a steady increase in jurisdictions opting for a full or partial prohibition, reaching 20% of respondents by 2025. The FATF notes that effectively enforcing a ban is a significant challenge.

- **Licensing and Supervision:** While more jurisdictions are requiring VASPs to be licensed or registered, many of these frameworks are not yet operational. Ninety-six jurisdictions report having a licensing requirement in law, but only 76 have licensed or registered a VASP in practice. A key challenge is identifying and regulating offshore Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs).

- **The Travel Rule:** Progress has been made, with 73% of relevant jurisdictions (85 of 117) having passed legislation to implement the Travel Rule. However, this leaves a substantial global gap, and enforcement remains limited, with 59% of these jurisdictions having taken no enforcement action on Travel Rule compliance.

**Emerging Risks and Trends**

- **Stablecoins:** The use of stablecoins by illicit actors, including terrorist financiers and state-sponsored groups such as those from the DPRK, has continued to increase. Estimates suggest a majority of all illegal on-chain activity now involves stablecoins. Illicit actors often pair stablecoins with anonymity-enhancing tools and use them to layer funds through dormant accounts.

- **Theft and Scams:** The scale of VA theft and fraud remains significant. The report highlights the DPRK's theft of \$1.46 billion from VASP ByBit in 2025 as the most critical single VA theft. There is also a noted increase in the professionalisation of scams, including "scam-as-a-service" activity and investment scams like "pig butchering".

- **Decentralised Finance (DeFi):** Identifying who is in control of a DeFi arrangement remains a significant challenge for regulators. About half (48%) of advanced jurisdictions now require specific DeFi arrangements to be licensed as VASPs; however, most (75% of that group) have not successfully identified any entities that qualify.

**Implications for Firms (VASPs)**

The FATF's findings have significant implications for firms operating in the virtual asset sector. The overall message is that regulatory scrutiny is increasing, and firms must enhance their compliance frameworks to address both existing standards and emerging risks.

- **Expect Increased Regulatory and Supervisory Pressure:** The slow pace of global implementation has frustrated the FATF. Firms should anticipate more aggressive supervision and enforcement actions as jurisdictions work to improve their compliance ratings. The report's call for jurisdictions to "rapidly operationalise" the Travel Rule is a clear signal that patience is wearing thin.

- **The Travel Rule is a Priority:** The persistent gaps in Travel Rule implementation are a "serious concern" for the FATF. Firms must ensure they have a robust, effective, and fully operational Travel Rule solution in place. Regulators will likely focus inspections on this specific requirement.

- **Risk Management for Stablecoins is Critical:** Given that the majority of on-chain illicit activity now involves stablecoins, firms must implement heightened risk mitigation measures for these assets. This includes conducting enhanced due diligence on transactions involving stablecoins, particularly those paired with anonymity-enhancing services or those operating on high-risk networks.

- **Scrutiny of DeFi Protocols Will Intensify:** Firms offering services related to DeFi protocols should be prepared for greater scrutiny. The FATF is focused on identifying the "controlling" entities in these arrangements. Firms should assess their DeFi offerings and be ready to demonstrate how they manage the associated AML/CFT risks, even in a supposedly "decentralised" environment.

- **Navigating a Fragmented Global Landscape:** The inconsistent implementation of R.15 worldwide (as shown in Annexe A) creates a complex and fragmented compliance environment. Firms operating across borders must manage differing legal requirements and exercise caution when dealing with counterparties in jurisdictions with weak or non-existent AML/CFT regimes for VAs.

- **Cybersecurity and Fraud Prevention are Paramount:** The record-breaking theft by the DPRK and the rise of professional scam networks underscore the importance of robust security and fraud prevention systems. These are no longer just commercial risks but significant AML/CFT concerns, as the proceeds of these crimes are laundered through the VA ecosystem.

### FinCrime: FATF report on virtual assets and travel rule supervision

The Financial Action Task Force (FATF)'s June 2025 report outlines "Best Practices on Travel Rule Supervision" for Virtual Assets (VAs) and Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs). The core aim is to enhance the global financial system's protection against money laundering (ML), terrorist financing (TF), and proliferation financing (PF) by ensuring payment transparency for VA transfers, similar to traditional financial institutions.

The report highlights that despite the FATF's 2019 extension of its standards to VAs and VASPs (Recommendation 15), implementation, particularly of the Travel Rule, remains inconsistent across jurisdictions. The Travel Rule mandates VASPs and financial institutions to obtain, hold, and transmit specific originator and beneficiary information immediately and securely during VA transfers.

Key challenges to effective Travel Rule implementation and supervision include:

- **The "Sunrise Issue":** Uneven and inconsistent adoption of the Travel Rule globally, leading to difficulties for compliant VASPs interacting with non-compliant counterparts. While progress has been made (85 out of 163 surveyed jurisdictions now have legislation, up from 65 in 2024), this remains the "single largest obstacle."

- **Unhosted Wallets:** While not explicitly subject to AML/CFT controls, transfers between VASPs and unhosted wallets (and peer-to-peer transactions) pose ML/TF risks that require mitigation. Jurisdictions need to assess and address these risks proportionately.

- **Capacity, Risk Understanding, and Training:** A global shortage of technical expertise and supervisory capacity within regulatory bodies, coupled with a lack of common understanding between authorities and VASPs on Travel Rule risks and compliance readiness.

- **Travel Rule Tools: Interoperability and Deficiencies:** A lack of interoperability between various Travel Rule compliance tools creates friction and complicates full compliance for VASPs. Some tools also have inherent gaps or deficiencies.

To address these challenges, the report identifies several good practices for jurisdictions, categorized by supervisory lifecycle stages:

- **Pre-implementation Phase:** Gaining a robust understanding of the VASP population (size, risk, compliance capabilities) through surveys and engagement before enforcing compliance. This includes setting clear regulatory expectations.

- **Thematic Reviews:** Using thematic reviews, even before full enforcement, to understand VASPs' preparedness and address vulnerabilities.

- **Setting Clear Regulatory Expectations:** Issuing clear, actionable, and up-to-date guidance, including on challenging areas like unhosted wallets and the Sunrise Issue (e.g., through lists of equivalent jurisdictions).

- **Building a Risk-Based Supervisory Lifecycle:**

  - **Travel Rule Compliance as a Licensing/Registration Condition:** Requiring VASPs to demonstrate Travel Rule compliance *before* commencing operations (e.g., via live demonstrations, regulatory sandboxes, or early onsite reviews).

  - **Use of Third Parties:** Leveraging external consultants or auditors to support AML/CFT functions, such as independent audits of VASP policies and controls, while maintaining ultimate supervisory responsibility.

  - **Overcoming Capacity and Training Gaps:** Establishing specialized teams, providing continuous training, and utilizing blockchain analytics tools for transaction data testing.

  - **Ensuring Up-to-Date Risk Understanding:** Systematizing risk understanding through ongoing reporting, monitoring, and enforcement structures.

  - **Remedial Actions and Sanctions:** Applying proportionate sanctions for breaches, including publishing enforcement actions and case studies to educate the sector.

- **Engagement with Private Sector:** Maintaining ongoing dialogue, information sharing, and capacity-building efforts through public-private partnerships and dedicated working groups to improve understanding of risks and foster compliance.

**Firm Implications**

The FATF's report on Travel Rule supervision carries several significant implications for VASPs, financial institutions, and regulators globally:

- **For Virtual Asset Service Providers (VASPs)**

  - **Heightened Compliance Expectation:** The report underscores a clear expectation for **proactive and robust Travel Rule compliance.** This is no longer a future consideration but an immediate operational necessity.

  - **Pre-emptive Action for "Sunrise Issue":** VASPs must actively manage the "Sunrise Issue" by assessing counterparty jurisdictions' Travel Rule implementation status. This might involve restricting transactions with non-compliant VASPs or implementing enhanced due diligence.

  - **Due Diligence on Travel Rule Solutions:** VASPs are explicitly responsible for the effectiveness of their chosen Travel Rule solutions. This means conducting thorough due diligence on solution providers, considering interoperability, and understanding any tool deficiencies. Relying solely on a vendor without internal validation is a significant risk.

  - **Investment in Capacity and Training:** Expect increased pressure to invest in internal training for staff on ML/TF risks in the VA sector and the specifics of Travel Rule obligations. A common understanding with supervisors is crucial.

  - **Managing Unhosted Wallet Risks:** VASPs will need to implement clear policies and procedures for transfers involving unhosted wallets, including collecting comparable information from customers and potentially verifying ownership.

  - **Potential for Stricter Licensing/Registration:** In jurisdictions adopting the "best practices," demonstrating Travel Rule compliance may become a prerequisite for obtaining or maintaining a license. This implies earlier and more in-depth scrutiny of a VASP's technical and operational capabilities.

  - **Increased Scrutiny and Enforcement:** As supervisory regimes mature, VASPs should anticipate more frequent and rigorous inspections, including thematic reviews and independent testing of transaction data using tools like blockchain analytics. The report indicates a growing trend of enforcement actions for non-compliance, with findings potentially being publicly disclosed.

  - **Engagement with Regulators:** Proactive engagement with regulatory bodies and industry associations is not just beneficial but increasingly expected. This helps VASPs stay informed, provide feedback on challenges, and contribute to the development of effective standards.

- **For Financial Institutions (FIs)**

  - **Similar Obligations for VA Transfers:** Financial institutions that engage in virtual asset transfers on behalf of customers are reminded that the same Travel Rule obligations apply to them as they do to VASPs. This necessitates understanding VA-specific nuances of the Travel Rule.

  - **Risk Assessment for VA Activities:** FIs should include VA-related activities in their overall risk assessments, considering the unique ML/TF risks associated with virtual assets, especially in cross-border transactions and interactions with VASPs.

- **For Regulators and Supervisors**

  - **Urgency in Implementation:** The FATF reinforces the urgency for all jurisdictions to implement the Travel Rule. Lagging behind not only creates vulnerabilities but also complicates compliance for other jurisdictions.

  - **Focus on Practical Guidance:** There's a clear call for regulators to provide practical, actionable, and up-to-date guidance to VASPs, informed by real-world challenges and supervisory findings.

  - **Risk-Based Supervision:** Regulators are encouraged to adopt a truly risk-based approach to VASP supervision, allocating resources to higher-risk firms and activities.

  - **Enhancing Supervisory Capacity:** Investing in specialized teams, training, and tools (like blockchain analytics) is paramount for effective VASP supervision.

  - **Leveraging Licensing for Compliance:** Integrating Travel Rule compliance as a condition for licensing or registration is highlighted as an effective way to mitigate risks at the outset.

  - **Collaboration and Information Sharing:** Fostering public-private partnerships and facilitating information exchange with the VASP sector is crucial for both understanding risks and guiding compliance.

  - **Enforcement and Deterrence:** While initial focus might be on education, regulators are expected to move towards proportionate enforcement actions for significant or repeated failures, and to publish these findings to deter noncompliance across the sector.
