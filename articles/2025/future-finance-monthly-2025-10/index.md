## Executive Summary

#### European Union: Implementation, Systemic Risk, and Harmonisation

The EU is intensely focused on implementing MiCA and DORA, establishing the Anti-Money Laundering Authority (AMLA), and managing emerging systemic risks in the crypto sector.<sup>1</sup>

- **Systemic Crypto Risks "Mounting":** The European Systemic Risk Board (ESRB) issued a stark warning that financial stability risks from crypto-assets are increasing, partly fueled by new US policies. The ESRB identified three critical vulnerabilities: a "stablecoin loophole" in MiCAR involving third-country multi-issuer schemes (e.g., USDC) that could trigger redemption runs on EU entities; extreme concentration in custody services (the top three custodians manage 71% of CIP assets); and regulatory gaps for large Multi-Function Groups (MFGs). The ESRB recommends immediate enhanced supervisory dialogue for MFGs and a future legislative review of MiCAR.

- **Strict MiCA Enforcement:** A Significant conflict emerged between the EBA and the European Commission regarding stablecoin reserves. The EBA formally rejected the Commission’s attempts to loosen liquidity requirements for Asset-Referenced Tokens (ARTs) and to classify Money Market Funds (MMFs) as automatically safe. The EBA is insisting on strict, bank-like liquidity standards to ensure investor protection and prevent regulatory arbitrage.

- **The Rise of AMLA:** The EBA finalised the technical foundation for AMLA, establishing a unified AML/CFT rulebook. This includes standardised risk assessments, harmonised Customer Due Diligence (CDD), and the methodology for selecting firms for direct AMLA supervision. Operationally, firms face a massive requirement to remediate all existing customer files to the new standards (with a 5-year transition period for non-high-risk clients). Crucially, the 2026 calendar year is the first data reference period for selection.

- **DORA and Cyber Priorities:** Cyber risk and the implementation of DORA are top supervisory priorities for 2026 across the EBA and ESMA. This includes the first complete cycle of direct oversight for Critical Third-Party Providers (CTPPs).

- **Regulatory Simplification:** In a notable shift, regulators are prioritising efficiency. The European Commission paused 115 "non-essential" Level 2 legislative acts for at least two years.<sup>7</sup> The EBA is aiming for a 25% reduction in reporting costs, and ESMA is driving the simplification of transactional (MiFIR, EMIR, SFTR) and funds reporting (AIFMD/UCITS).

#### United Kingdom: Proportionality and Innovation

The UK is balancing the embedding of the Consumer Duty with pro-innovation initiatives in digital markets and streamlining AML supervision.

- **Consumer Duty Recalibration:** The Consumer Duty remains central to the FCA’s strategy; however, the regulator acknowledged "disproportionate compliance costs" for wholesale firms. A four-point action plan will clarify supervisory expectations, consult on updating client categorisation, and, significantly, propose removing non-UK customers from the Duty’s scope entirely.

- **Digital Markets and Tokenisation:** The FCA is actively consulting (CP25/28) on a framework to support fund tokenisation, including a "Blueprint Model" for DLT-based registers and a new "Direct to Fund" (D2F) dealing model.

- **Emerging Technology and Resilience:** The Bank of England outlined its strategy to "shape, not slow" innovation in AI, DLT, and Quantum Computing. This involves adapting the RTGS system for DLT interoperability and establishing a regime for systemic stablecoins (consultation expected Q4 2025). Regulators emphasised the need for firms to test destructive cyber scenarios and to plan for long-term "quantum readiness."

- **AML Consolidation:** The UK Government plans to consolidate the fragmented AML/CTF supervision regime under a Single Professional Services Supervisor (SPSS), a role to be assumed by the FCA.

#### International Developments and Global Trends

Global regulators are converging on standards for digital assets while navigating the challenges of new technologies and the fragmented implementation of these standards.

- **Stablecoin Regulation and Compliance:** The global landscape for stablecoins is rapidly solidifying (US GENIUS Act, Hong Kong, Japan, Australia), but remains fragmented regarding reserve requirements and the permissibility of yields. A critical compliance trend, highlighted by Chainalysis, is the regulatory expectation (e.g., by the HKMA) for issuers to use on-chain analytics to monitor secondary market activity for AML/CFT risks.

- **IOSCO Implementation:** An IOSCO thematic review found significant but varied progress in implementing Crypto and Digital Asset (CDA) recommendations, particularly regarding custody. Challenges persist in managing conflicts of interest in vertically integrated CASPs and ensuring practical cross-border cooperation.

- **AI and DLT Convergence (AIxDLT):** The convergence of AI and DLT offers powerful use cases, such as AI-powered oracles and DLT-verified AI data provenance. However, this creates complex legal and liability challenges by overlapping immature regulatory frameworks (e.g., AI Act, MiCA, GDPR).

- **Bitcoin as a Reserve Asset:** A Deutsche Bank report argues that Bitcoin is maturing into a viable macro-hedge, supported by its negative correlation with the USD. Driven by de-dollarisation trends, the report predicts Bitcoin could coexist with gold as a central bank reserve asset by 2030.

## Europe

### Digital Assets: ESRB - Crypto-assets and decentralised finance: Report on stablecoins, crypto-investment products and multi-function groups

The European Systemic Risk Board (ESRB) issues a stark warning in its report, stating that financial stability risks from crypto-assets are “mounting” in 2025. This increase is mainly fueled by new “pro-crypto” US policies (such as the GENIUS Act), which are rapidly expanding the market (especially for USD-denominated stablecoins) and strengthening the US dollar’s dominance in the crypto ecosystem.

The report focuses on three key areas of risk:

- **Stablecoins:** The report identifies a critical “stablecoin loophole” in the EU’s Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCAR). This loophole involves “third-country multi-issuer” schemes (e.g., USDC), where an EU-regulated entity and a non-EU entity issue the same fungible token. The reserves are split geographically. The ESRB warns that a run on the non-EU token (which is not MiCAR-compliant) could trigger a massive redemption run on the EU entity, as global holders seek to redeem in the EU, where consumer protections (such as the ban on redemption fees) are stronger. This could quickly drain the EU entity’s reserves and propagate instability within the EU.

- **Crypto-Investment Products (CIPs):** The market for CIPs (like ETPs and ETFs) has grown significantly, reaching USD 235 billion. While this increases investor exposure, the primary systemic risk identified is the extreme concentration in custody services. The top three custodians manage approximately 71% of the assets for physically-backed CIPs. The failure of a single custodian could have a cascading, destabilising effect across the entire crypto market.

- **Multi-Function Groups (MFGs):** These are large crypto “conglomerates” (like Binance or the collapsed FTX) that bundle multiple services such as issuance, exchange, and custody. The report highlights that these groups pose significant risks from opaque governance and severe conflicts of interest. Crucially, most of these non-bank MFGs fall into a regulatory gap, as they are not subject to existing EU group-wide supervisory frameworks (like banking consolidation rules), making it impossible for supervisors to monitor group-wide risks.

**Firm Implications**

- **For Stablecoin Issuers:** Firms using the “multi-issuer” model are now under intense regulatory scrutiny. They must prepare for enhanced supervisory measures, higher liquidity and capital requirements, and the strong possibility of a future legislative review to close this “loophole.”

- **For Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs):**

  - **Stricter Enforcement:** CASPs should expect stricter enforcement against non-compliant stablecoins (such as USDT/Tether). The report explicitly suggests that authorities could ban CASPs from offering any services (including custody) related to these tokens.

  - **Concentration Risk:** Firms (such as CIP issuers) that rely heavily on a single dominant custodian (e.g., Coinbase, BitGo) face significant, identifiable counterparty and operational risks. Supervisors will increase their focus on these concentration risks.

  - **MFG Supervision:** Large, non-bank MFGs (such as exchanges) currently supervised at the entity level must prepare for a new, more intrusive layer of group-wide supervision. This will likely involve new group-level reporting requirements and enhanced supervisory dialogue to assess the interconnectedness and overall financial health of the group.

- **For Traditional Financial Institutions:** Banks and asset managers entering the crypto space (e.g., as CIP issuers or custodians) will face heightened monitoring of their interlinkages with the crypto sector and their exposure to the identified custody and stablecoin risks.

- **For EU Investors:** The report confirms that EU holders of “multi-issuer” stablecoins are exposed to global risks, as their EU-based reserves could be depleted by a run originating outside the EU. They also remain vulnerable to international market shocks from non-compliant stablecoins, such as USDT.

**Next Steps and Timeline**

The report outlines a clear, multi-stage timeline for regulatory action based on its findings from October 2025.

- **Immediate Term (Short Term):**

  - **MFGs:** The ESRB strongly encourages the EBA, ESMA, NCAs, and the ECB to immediately establish structures for “enhanced supervisory dialogue” and coordination to monitor the group-wide risks (liquidity, leverage, interconnectedness) of dominant, non-bank MFGs (Categories 1 and 2a).

  - **Stablecoins:** Authorities are urged to apply existing MiCAR provisions “to the fullest extent practicable” to mitigate the multi-issuer risks now, even before new legislation is passed.

- **Medium Term:**

  - **MiCAR Review:** The report formally recommends that the European Commission and Parliament conduct a review of MiCAR to strengthen the policy framework.

  - **MFGs:** This review must introduce formal group-level reporting requirements and formalise supervisory cooperation mechanisms for non-bank MFGs.

  - **Stablecoins:** The legislative review must explicitly address and close the third-country multi-issuer stablecoin loophole.

- **Long Term (Under Review):**

  - The report deems it “premature” to consider a full-scale resolution framework (i.e., orderly wind-down powers) for non-bank MFGs. However, this will be kept under review as the sector’s interconnectedness with traditional finance grows.

### FinCrime: EBA technical advice on priority RTS and guidelines under AMLA Regulation, AML Regulation and MLD6

This report contains the European Banking Authority’s (EBA) final advice to the European Commission on six key mandates that will form the technical foundation of the new EU Anti-Money Laundering Authority (AMLA). This advice, presented in the form of four draft Regulatory Technical Standards (RTS) and two technical advice papers, aims to establish a unified, harmonised AML/CFT rulebook.

The primary goal is to move away from the current fragmented system, where 27 different national transpositions of AML Directives create regulatory gaps and compliance burdens for cross-border firms. AMLA will now take the EBA’s proposals forward to establish a unified framework with directly applicable rules.

The six mandates covered are:

1. **RTS on Entity Risk Assessment:** A standard methodology for all EU supervisors to assess the inherent and residual ML/TF risk profile of obliged entities (OEs).

2. **RTS on Selection for Direct Supervision:** The methodology AMLA will use to identify and select high-risk, cross-border financial institutions for direct supervision.

3. **RTS on Customer Due Diligence (CDD):** Detailed, harmonised requirements for Standard, Simplified (SDD), and Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD).

4. **RTS on Sanctions and Measures:** A common framework for classifying the gravity of breaches and applying pecuniary sanctions, administrative measures, and new Periodic Penalty Payments (PePPs).

5. **Advice on Fines:** Guidance on establishing the “base amounts” for pecuniary fines.

6. **Advice on Group-wide Policies:** Minimum standards for group-wide AML/CFT policies, particularly concerning information sharing (i.e., creating a “single customer view”).

**Firm Implications**

These proposals will have significant, firm-wide operational and strategic impacts on all obliged entities, particularly credit and financial institutions.

1. **Risk Assessment & Data Reporting**

    - **Standardised Risk Methodology:** Firms will be subject to a single, EU-wide risk assessment methodology. For cross-border groups, this ends the burden of reporting different risk data in various formats to multiple national supervisors.

    - **New Data Requirements:** Firms must prepare to collect and report a new, particular set of data points (detailed in Annexe 1) covering inherent risk (customers, products, geographies) and AML/CFT controls. The EBA reduced the final data points by 15% after consultation; however, firms must still prepare for significant IT and data governance initiatives.

    - **Frequency:** Most firms will have their risk profile formally reviewed **annually**. Smaller or low-risk entities may be reviewed only **once every three years**.

2. **Selection for Direct AMLA Supervision**

    - **Clear Eligibility Criteria:** Firms now have a clear threshold for eligibility for direct AMLA supervision. A firm is considered “operating” in another Member State (triggering eligibility if active in 6+ states) if it has:

      - More than **20,000 customers** reside in that state; **OR**

      - More than **€50,000,000** in annual transaction value generated by customers in that state.

    - **Selection Process:** Eligible firms will be assessed using the new standardised risk methodology. Those classified as “high risk” at the group level will be selected for the first wave of direct AMLA supervision.

3. **Customer Due Diligence (CDD)**

    - **Massive Remediation Project:** This is the most operationally heavy implication. Firms must update **all existing customer files** to meet the new, detailed RTS requirements.

    - **5-Year Transition Period:** The EBA proposes a pragmatic timeline for this. Upon the application date, firms must use a risk-based approach to:

      0. Prioritise updating all **high-risk** customer files first.

      1. Update all other **non-high-risk** existing customer files within a **5-year transition period**.

    <!-- -->

    - **Harmonised Rules:** The new RTS will create a single, detailed standard for what information must be collected and verified for CDD, SDD, and EDD, ending national discrepancies.

<!-- -->

1. **Sanctions and Enforcement**

    - **Consistent Penalties:** The new framework classifies breaches into four categories of gravity. Breaches classified as **Category 3 or 4** will be automatically deemed “serious, repeated or systematic” and will trigger pecuniary sanctions.

    - **New Enforcement Tool:** Firms will face **Periodic Penalty Payments (PePPs)**. This is a new tool that supervisors can use to impose an ongoing daily, weekly, or monthly fine to compel a firm to comply with an administrative measure (e.g., rectify a control failure) that it has ignored.

2. **Group-wide Policies & Information Sharing**

    - **Mandated Single Customer View:** The advice lays the groundwork for requiring groups to share customer information (identity, risk, and suspicions/STRs) among all branches and subsidiaries.

    - **IT & Data Governance Impact:** This will require firms to build the technical and legal infrastructure to share personal and sensitive data across borders (within the group) to manage group-wide risk effectively.

**Next Steps & Timeline**

The EBA has handed this advice to AMLA, which will now finalise the rules. Firms must act immediately, as the reference period for the first selection is already underway.

- **January 1, 2026:** AMLA’s deadline to submit the final draft RTS on the methodology for selecting entities for direct supervision.

- **July 10, 2026:** AMLA’s deadline to submit the final draft RTS for entity risk profiles, CDD, and sanctions, as well as guidelines on fines.

- **CRITICAL PERIOD (2026):** The **entire calendar year of 2026** is the first data reference period. Firms must ensure they are collecting the data points outlined in Annexe 1 *now*, as this 2026 data will be used to determine the first selection.

- **July 1, 2027:** AMLA will officially **begin the first selection process** for direct supervision, using the 2026 data.

- **July 10, 2027:** The new AML/CFT package (AMLR, AMLD6, and these RTS) is expected to **apply** across the EU fully.

- **July 2027 – July 2032:** The **5-year transition period** for firms to remediate all existing non-high-risk customer CDD files.

### FinCrime: EBA report on tackling money laundering and terrorist financing risks in cryptoasset services

This EBA report explains how some crypto-asset businesses exploited regulatory gaps to avoid AML/CFT oversight. It discusses the new, stronger EU framework under MiCA, offering guidance for authorities to ensure effective implementation and prevent repeat failures. The report highlights ML/TF risks from the crypto sector, worsened by a fragmented regulatory landscape in the EU. Some Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs) used various strategies to evade supervision:

- **Unauthorised Operations:** Providing services in EU member states without the required national license or registration.

- **Forum Shopping:** Exploiting differences in national regulations by applying for licenses in jurisdictions perceived to have less stringent AML/CFT requirements.

- **Abuse of Exemptions:** Improperly using the “reverse solicitation” exemption to service EU clients without authorisation.

- **Weak Internal Controls:** Demonstrating recurring deficiencies in AML/CFT policies, risk assessments, outsourcing arrangements, and compliance resourcing.

- **Opaque Structures:** Using complex and unclear beneficial ownership and governance structures to hide accountability and control.

- **Risky Partnerships:** Leveraging multi-entity arrangements with other high-risk firms to maintain market presence after facing regulatory action.

The report highlights that the new harmonised framework, focused on MiCA and an improved AML/CFT package (coming into effect in December 2024), establishes essential safeguards. These include an EU-wide authorisation and passporting system, stricter governance and transparency standards, and increased supervisory powers. However, the success of this new regime relies entirely on consistent and vigilant implementation by national Competent Authorities (CAs).

**Firm Implications**

For Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs) & Issuers:

- **End of Regulatory Arbitrage:** The harmonised MiCA framework eliminates the ability to “forum shop” for lenient regulators within the EU. A single authorisation allows for passporting rights, but it comes with uniformly high standards.

- **Scrutiny of Legacy Issues:** Firms with a history of AML/CFT weaknesses will face intense scrutiny during the MiCA authorisation process. Authorisation will not be granted until past issues are fully resolved to EU standards.

- **Robust Compliance is Non-Negotiable:** Generic, group-level AML/CFT policies are insufficient. Frameworks must be tailored to EU regulations, with clear accountability, sufficient local resources for compliance functions, and effective oversight of any outsourced activities.

- **Complete Transparency Required:** Opaque ownership and complex group structures will be heavily scrutinised. CAs are empowered to look beyond formal ownership to assess indirect control and influence; firms must provide clear and consistent information to support this assessment.

- **Strict Enforcement:** Non-compliance can lead to severe penalties, including the withdrawal of authorisation to operate anywhere in the EU.

**Next Steps and Timeline**

- **December 31, 2024:** MiCA and the enhanced AML/CFT legislative framework came into force.

- **End of 2025:** The EBA will transfer its standalone AML/CFT powers to the newly established Anti-Money Laundering Authority (AMLA).

- **July 1, 2026:** The transitional “grandfathering” period ends. All CASPs must have obtained MiCA authorisation to continue operating in the EU.

- **2027:** The 6th Anti-Money Laundering Directive (AMLD6) will apply, further strengthening rules on group-wide supervision.

### FinCrime: European Commission letter to ESAs and AMLA on deprioritising certain Level 2 financial services legislation

The European Commission is streamlining EU financial regulations. Between 2019 and 2024, it was authorised to produce 430 detailed “Level 2” acts. In response to stakeholder concerns, 115 of these are deemed “non-essential” and will be paused for at least two years. This aims to reduce regulatory burden, improve efficiency, and enhance clarity for the financial industry.

**Firm Implications**

- **For Financial Institutions (Banks, Insurers, Investment Firms, etc.):** This provides immediate regulatory relief. Firms will not have to dedicate resources to preparing for and implementing these 115 specific pieces of legislation in the short term. This reduces compliance costs and administrative complexity, allowing them to focus on implementing the essential core regulations.

- **For European Supervisory Authorities (ESAs & AMLA):** The EBA, ESMA, EIOPA, and the new AMLA are instructed to pause their work on drafting the technical standards related to the de-prioritised acts. This allows them to reallocate resources to more critical supervisory and regulatory priorities. They are also responsible for ensuring that market participants are aware of this decision.

- **For the Regulatory Landscape:** This signals a notable policy shift toward simplification and efficiency. It provides the market with certainty that a significant portion of potential future regulation is now on hold and may ultimately be removed entirely from the legal framework.

**Next Steps & Timeline**

The Commission will implement a straightforward two-step approach:

- **Immediate Freeze (Now - Oct 2027):** The Commission commits not to adopt any of the 115 non-essential Level 2 acts before October 1, 2027. This creates a definite two-year pause.

- **Legislative Cleanup (Within the next two years):** For essential rules that the Commission is legally required to issue, it will propose amending or repealing the original enabling legislation as part of upcoming reviews of the main (Level 1) legislation. Many of these reviews are scheduled for this two-year window.

- **Public Communication (Immediate):** The ESAs and AMLA are instructed to publish this letter and its annexe on their websites immediately to ensure all stakeholders are informed of this decision.

- **At the end of the two years,** the Commission will either proceed to adopt the measures or formally remove the requirements from the law.

### MiCA: EBA opinions on Commission amendments to draft RTS on prudential matters under MiCA

The European Banking Authority (EBA) has issued a formal negative opinion, rejecting significant changes proposed by the European Commission to the rules governing the liquidity of reserves for Asset-Referenced Tokens (ARTs) under MiCA.

The core disagreement centres on ARTs backed by **non-currency assets**, such as commodities (e.g., a gold-backed stablecoin).

- **The Commission’s Proposal:** The Commission sought to simplify the rules by removing the EBA’s strict requirements that issuers hold a large portion of their reserves in cash or assets that can be sold within 1 to 5 days. The EBA believes that this change would incorrectly permit issuers to invest the proceeds from token sales directly in illiquid assets, such as physical gold.

- **The EBA’s Rejection:** The EBA argues this creates unacceptable liquidity risk. Since token holders who pay in cash have the right to redeem for money, investing the reserve in illiquid assets could prevent an issuer from meeting redemption requests during a major cash stress event, potentially leading to a collapse. The EBA states that this is inconsistent with MiCA’s prudential goals, creates an uneven playing field with banks (who are prohibited from treating commodities as liquid assets), and invites regulatory arbitrage.

The EBA has accepted the Commission’s minor, non-substantive editorial changes but has re-submitted the technical standards, reinstating its original, stricter liquidity requirements.

**Firm Implications**

- **For ART Issuers:** This creates significant regulatory uncertainty.

  - If the EBA’s view prevails, issuers of commodity-backed tokens will face strict and operationally restrictive liquidity rules. They will be required to hold a large portion of their reserves in highly liquid financial instruments (such as cash or short-term government bonds). They will primarily be unable to invest cash proceeds in the underlying commodity itself. This prioritises safety but increases management complexity and potential costs.

  - If the Commission’s view were to prevail, issuers would have more flexibility to manage their reserves, but this now appears unlikely given the EBA’s strong, formal opposition.

- **For Banks and Traditional Financial Institutions:** The EBA’s position is a significant defence against regulatory arbitrage. It prevents a scenario where the crypto sector could operate under looser liquidity standards than the banking sector for similar activities, potentially leading to destabilising effects.

- **For Investors:** The EBA’s stance is designed to protect token holders. It ensures that the issuer has enough cash or near-cash assets on hand to honour redemptions at any time, significantly reducing the risk of losses from an issuer’s inability to sell its reserve assets quickly.

**Next Steps & Timeline**

- **October 9, 2025:** The EBA formally sent its negative opinion and the amended draft Regulatory Technical Standards (RTS) back to the European Commission.

- **Q4 2025 (Expected):** The ball is now in the European Commission’s court. Having received a formal negative opinion, the Commission must reconsider its amendments. While it can technically overrule the EBA, it would need to provide a compelling public justification for doing so, contrary to the specific technical and legal advice of the experts. More likely, the Commission will accept the EBA’s revised text.

- **Late 2025 / Early 2026:** The Commission will formally adopt the final version of the Delegated Regulation.

- **Entry into Force:** The final rules will become legally binding and directly applicable in all EU Member States 20 days after their publication in the Official Journal of the European Union.

### MiCA: EBA’s Opinion, the implications for firms, and the expected next steps and timeline

The European Banking Authority (EBA) has formally rejected key amendments proposed by the European Commission to the types of safe assets that can back stablecoins (Asset-Referenced Tokens and E-money Tokens) under MiCA.

The disagreement centres on two main points:

1. **Money Market Funds (MMFs) are Not Automatically Safe:** The Commission wanted to classify all MMFs as “highly liquid financial instruments” (HLFI). The EBA strongly objects, arguing that MMFs have proven unreliable during periods of market stress (such as the COVID-19 crisis) and may face liquidity problems.

2. **Preventing Regulatory Arbitrage:** The EBA highlights a significant inconsistency. Under existing banking rules (the Liquidity Coverage Ratio or LCR), banks are not allowed to count MMFs as top-tier liquid assets. Allowing token issuers to do so would create an uneven playing field and a loophole that could enable the crypto sector to operate under looser safety standards than traditional banks.

The EBA has re-submitted the technical standards with its original, more conservative rules, effectively telling the Commission that its proposed changes introduce unacceptable risks to the financial system.

**Firm Implications**

- **For Stablecoin Issuers (ARTs & EMTs):** This decision severely restricts the investment options for the asset reserve.

  - **No MMF Shortcut:** Issuers cannot simply place a large portion of their reserves into MMFs and consider them sufficiently liquid. They will be forced to use a more limited list of ultra-safe assets, such as top-tier government bonds, that meet the same strict criteria applied to banks.

  - **Increased Operational Complexity:** Managing the reserve will be more complex and potentially less profitable, as the range of eligible, yield-generating instruments is narrower.

  - **Stricter Concentration and “Look-Through” Rules:** Issuers will be required to adhere to more stringent limits on how much they can invest with a single asset manager and will be required to “look through” collective investment funds to analyse the underlying assets, thereby preventing hidden risk concentrations.

- **For Banks and Traditional Financial Institutions:** The EBA’s stance is a significant win. It upholds the principle of a level playing field, ensuring that new crypto-asset firms cannot assume liquidity risks that are forbidden to established banks. This prevents a potential race to the bottom on prudential standards.

- **For Investors:** This is a crucial measure for investor protection. The EBA is prioritising the actual safety and redeemability of stablecoins, ensuring that the assets backing them can be sold quickly for cash without losses, even during a market crisis. This reduces the risk of a stablecoin “breaking the buck.”

**Next Steps & Timeline**

- **October 9, 2025:** The EBA has sent its formal negative opinion and the revised draft Regulatory Technical Standards (RTS) back to the European Commission.

- **Q4 2025 (Expected):** The European Commission must now react. Receiving such a strong, public, and technically grounded rejection from its expert authority makes it politically difficult to ignore. The most likely outcome is that the Commission will concede and adopt the EBA’s stricter rules.

- **Late 2025 to early 2026:** The Commission will formally adopt the final Delegated Regulation, which will contain the technical standards.

- **Entry into Force:** The final rules will become legally binding and directly applicable across all EU Member States 20 days after their publication in the Official Journal of the European Union.

### MiCA: Delegated Regulation on RTS on liquidity management policy and procedures under MiCA published in OJ

Published in the Official Journal on 3 October 2025, this Commission Delegated Regulation details rules under the Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) framework. It specifies minimum content requirements for liquidity management policies for asset-referenced tokens (ARTs) and e-money tokens (EMTs), ensuring that issuers can meet redemption requests, particularly during periods of market stress. The regulation requires robust liquidity risk strategies, contingency plans with early warning systems, regular stress tests, including reverse stress tests, and segregation of liquidity policies for each token from other business activities. It also emphasises the importance of mitigating concentration risk with custodian-held reserves.

**Firm Implications**

- **Significant Compliance Uplift:** Issuers must create, document, and maintain highly detailed and granular procedures for liquidity management. This is a crucial operational and compliance task that extends far beyond a high-level policy statement.

- **Mandatory, Granular Risk Management:** Firms are required to actively monitor a range of specific risks, including intra-day liquidity needs, custodian concentration, and the creditworthiness of reserve assets. They must establish and monitor specific internal limits and “early warning signals,” such as deviations between the token’s market value and the value of the reserve assets.

- **Segregation Adds Complexity:** The requirement to establish a separate liquidity management policy for each ART or EMT offered adds significant operational complexity. A single, overarching policy for all issued tokens is explicitly forbidden.

- **Stress Testing Becomes a Core Activity:** Firms must design and regularly execute complex liquidity stress tests and reverse stress tests to ensure robust financial planning and decision-making. The parameters, assumptions, and outcomes of these tests must be documented and will be subject to supervisory review.

- **Direct Accountability for Management:** The regulation explicitly places responsibility on the issuer’s management body (e.g., the board of directors) to approve the liquidity policies and set the firm’s risk tolerance levels.

- **Potential Need for Overcollateralization:** Issuers must formally assess whether holding more assets in reserve than the value of tokens in circulation is appropriate, particularly when the referenced assets are highly volatile.

**Next Steps and Timeline**

- **For Affected Firms:**

  - They should immediately develop or update their liquidity management policies to meet the detailed requirements outlined in Articles 2 through 5 of the regulation.

  - Present these policies to their management body for formal approval and to set risk tolerance levels.

  - Implement the required monitoring systems to detect early warning signals, intra-day liquidity risks, and custodian concentration risks.

  - Design the methodology for their first round of liquidity stress testing and reverse stress testing.

  - Ensure complete segregation of policies is established for each token issued and from all other business activities.

- **For National Competent Authorities:**

  - Incorporate these technical standards into their supervisory frameworks for ART and EMT issuers.

  - Clarify for any non-significant issuers in their jurisdiction whether they will be required to comply with these rules.

  - Prepare to review and assess the detailed policy documents and stress test results that issuers will submit.

  - **October 23, 2025:** The regulation takes effect (20 days after publication).

  - **Effective immediately from the Entry into Force:** This regulation is binding and directly applicable in all EU Member States as of October 23, 2025. Firms covered by its scope must comply with this date.

### MiCA: ESMA: New MiCAR Q&As

ESMA has clarified how regulators should distinguish between three key crypto-asset services under MiCA:

1. **Exchange Services:** The CASP acts as a counterparty, using its own capital to buy or sell crypto-assets from a client at a firm price.

2. **Execution of Orders:** The CASP acts as an agent for the client to conclude a purchase or sale, with a duty to obtain the best possible result (best execution).

3. **Reception & Transmission of Orders (RTO):** The CASP acts as a conduit, simply receiving a client’s order and passing it to a third party for execution.

The core of the guidance is that regulators (NCAs) must not rely on a firm’s marketing labels or legal terms. Instead, they must conduct a “fact-specific analysis” of the “operational reality” and “order fulfilment flow.”

A critical “grey area” is addressed: a CASP acting as a client’s counterparty (akin to an “Exchange”) may still be providing “Execution” services and thus be subject to best execution rules. The deciding factor is the client’s reasonable expectation.

Crucially, ESMA states that in any case of doubt, especially with retail clients, regulators should **presume** the CASP is providing **Execution** services—imposing the stricter best execution requirements.

**Firm Implications**

- **Business Model Clarity is Non-Negotiable:** A CASP cannot simply call itself an “Exchange” to avoid best execution rules. If the “operational reality” or client perception is that the CASP is acting as an agent to find a price, it will be regulated as an “Execution” service.

- **“Presumption of Execution” for Retail:** This is the most significant implication. If a CASP’s service for retail clients is ambiguous, regulators will default to classifying it as “Execution.” This forces the CASP to establish a “best execution policy,” a complex, high-stakes compliance burden.

- **Operational Reality Over Legal Text:** Regulators are explicitly instructed to ignore marketing labels and review the actual order flow. This means a CASP’s entire tech stack, order routing, and pricing mechanism will be subject to scrutiny.

- **High Bar for “Exchange” Service:** To be considered only an “Exchange” (and avoid best execution rules), a CASP must make it undeniably clear to clients (especially retail) that it is offering only a firm, proprietary price and is not acting as their agent. This must be reflected in its commercial policy, user interface, and pricing display.

- **Supervisory Risk:** CASPs should expect NCAs to challenge their service classifications during the authorisation process and in subsequent supervision. Misclassification is a significant compliance risk.

**Next Steps and Timeline**

- **Immediate Internal Review:** CASPs must immediately conduct a “fact-specific analysis” of their order fulfilment flows, comparing the reality of their operations against the three definitions provided by ESMA.

- **Client Perception Audit:** Review all marketing, user agreements, and the platform’s UI/UX. Ask the question: “Would a ‘reasonable person’ in our client’s position expect us to act as their agent (Execution) or as a simple counterparty (Exchange)?”

- **Remediate Ambiguity (The “Doubt” Test):**

  - If any ambiguity exists for retail services, the CASP must make a strategic choice:

    1. **Comply:** Accept the "Execution" classification and build/implement a complete MiCA-compliant best execution framework.

    2. **Redesign:** Redesign the service, user terms, and UI to remove all ambiguity, explicitly stating that the firm is a counterparty, not an agent.

- **Document Everything:** Prepare detailed documentation of this analysis, the rationale for the firm's service classification, and any changes made. This will be required for discussions with NCAs.

- **Update Authorisation Applications:** CASPs in the process of MiCA authorisation must ensure their applications fully reflect this guidance and provide a robust justification for their service classification.

- **Timeline:**

  - **30 December 2024:** The MiCA rules for CASPs, including these service definitions, entered into application.

  - **14 October 2025 (Date of ESMA Answer):** This guidance is practical immediately. It clarifies existing rules, not a new rule.

  - **Immediate (Q4 2025 onwards):** NCAs will use this interpretation *now* when assessing new CASP authorisation applications and conducting initial supervisory reviews of already-authorised firms.

  - **2026 (Ongoing Supervision):** This interpretation will be a core supervisory priority for NCAs as they move into full-scope MiCA supervision. Firms can expect this to be a primary area of focus during any regulatory audit.

### DORA: Official translations of ESMA guidelines on outsourcing to cloud service providers by non-DORA depositaries

These new ESMA guidelines establish a consistent framework for specific AIF and UCITS depositaries (those not covered by the DORA Regulation) that outsource functions to Cloud Service Providers (CSPs). The rules are designed to help these firms identify, manage, and monitor the risks throughout the lifecycle of a cloud outsourcing arrangement.

The guidelines are based on nine key principles, with a clear distinction between requirements for “critical or important functions” and non-critical ones. Core requirements for firms include:

- Establishing a formal governance framework and maintaining a detailed register of all cloud arrangements.

- Conducting extensive due diligence and risk assessments before outsourcing.

- Ensuring written contracts contain specific, mandatory clauses on security, audit rights, data location, and sub-outsourcing.

- Developing and testing robust exit strategies to ensure they can terminate an arrangement without disrupting business.

- Guaranteeing that the firm and its regulators have effective access and audit rights over the CSP.

- Notifying their competent authority promptly before outsourcing any critical function.

**Firm Implications**

- **Formal Governance is Mandatory:** Firms must treat cloud outsourcing as a high-stakes activity, not just an IT decision. This requires a formal strategy, a straightforward assignment of senior management responsibility, and the creation of a detailed and up-to-date register of all cloud outsourcing arrangements.

- **Increased Due Diligence Burden:** The pre-outsourcing analysis is extensive. Firms must assess a wide range of operational, legal, compliance, and concentration risks. This demands significant internal expertise and resources before any contract is signed.

- **More arduous Contract Negotiations:** Firms must now ensure their legal agreements with CSPs include particular clauses. This includes the right to terminate, full access, and audit rights for the firm and regulators, along with strict conditions on any sub-outsourcing, which may be challenging to negotiate with large, global CSPs.

- **Exit Planning is a Must:** For any critical function outsourced to the cloud, firms must have a comprehensive, documented, and tested exit strategy. This involves identifying alternative solutions and creating transition plans to ensure they can move their data and operations smoothly if needed.

- **Accountability for the Entire Supply Chain:** The firm remains fully responsible, even if its CSP sub-contracts a function. The guidelines require firms to have the right to object to sub-outsourcing and to ensure their primary CSP properly oversees any third parties involved.

**Next Steps and Timeline**

- **For Firms:**

  - Immediately review all planned, new, and renewing cloud outsourcing arrangements to ensure they comply with the nine guidelines.

  - Update internal governance frameworks, policies, and risk management processes to align with the new requirements.

  - Create or update the mandatory register of cloud arrangements, clearly distinguishing between critical and non-critical functions.

  - Begin renegotiating contracts for any arrangements being renewed or amended to include the required clauses.

  - Establish a formal process for notifying their national competent authority of any planned outsourcing of critical functions.

- **For National Competent Authorities (Regulators):**

  - Incorporate these guidelines into their national supervisory frameworks.

  - Notify ESMA of their intention to comply with the guidelines.

  - Begin supervising the affected firms to ensure they are adhering to these new standards, with a particular focus on arrangements for critical functions.

- **Timeline:**

  - **September 30, 2025:** The guidelines are published and take effect.

  - **Effective immediately (from Sept 30, 2025):** The guidelines apply to all cloud outsourcing arrangements entered into, renewed, or amended on or after this date.

  - **By November 30, 2025 (within two months of publication):** National competent authorities must notify ESMA whether they comply with the guidelines.

  - **Ongoing:** Firms are expected to comply immediately with all new, renewed, or amended arrangements.

### CBDC: ECB Working Paper No 3111: Central bank money as a catalyst for fungibility

This paper defines the precise technical conditions required for any stablecoin to be genuinely "fungible” (i.e., interchangeable at par) and maintain the “singleness of money.” It argues that this is only possible if anchored to central bank money.

Three Technical Conditions for Fungibility:

1. **Settlement Finality:** The transaction must be irrevocable and unconditional. This is a challenge for DLTs that offer probabilistic finality (e.g., Proof-of-Work) versus the deterministic finality of central bank-run RTGS systems.

2. **Interoperability:** The system must be interoperable both horizontally (with other stablecoins) and vertically (with the traditional financial system, requiring on/off-ramps to central bank money).

3. **Seamless Convertibility:** The holder must have a credible and seamless legal right to redeem the stablecoin 1:1 at par for the "ultimate” settlement asset: central bank money.

**Firm Implications**

- The paper’s logic strongly favours stablecoin models with robust, transparent reserves (e.g., tokenised e-money funds or fully off-chain collateralised tokens).

- It explicitly concludes that **algorithmic stablecoins are not fungible** because they fundamentally lack the third condition (seamless convertibility into a reserve asset).

- Issuers must design their reserve and redemption mechanisms to be both legally and operationally robust enough to serve as a credible proxy for central bank money.

**Next Steps and Timeline**

- **Next Steps:** Stablecoin issuers and financial institutions must prioritise the “seamless convertibility” mechanism in their product design to meet future regulatory expectations.

- **Timeline:** As a 2025 working paper, this reflects the ECB’s current policy thinking.

### CBDC: Digital euro innovation platform: Outcome report (ECB)

This September 2025 report details the findings from the ECB’s "visionaries” (conceptual) and "pioneers” (technical) workstreams (Feb-May 2025). The key takeaway is the ECB’s architectural vision for the digital euro as a “platform.” The Eurosystem will provide the core settlement back-end. At the same time, private-sector Payment Service Providers (PSPs) will be empowered to build and monetise innovative, value-added services on top of it.

**Firm Implications**

- **A "Platform” Architecture:** The ECB is not building a monolithic, user-facing app. It is building the core infrastructure and APIs. This creates a massive opportunity for PSPs, FinTechs, and banks to act as the interface and service layer for the digital euro.

- **“Conditional Payments” (Not “Programmable Money” ):** This is the key technical concept. The ECB explicitly rejects “programmable money” (where the token itself has rules). Instead, it proposes “conditional payments”:

  - **Eurosystem (Back-End):** Provides the core API for “reservation of funds,” enabling a PSP to lock a user’s funds temporarily.

  - **PSP (Front-End):** Builds the “conditionality layer” (the business logic). Example: A PSP’s app tells the ECB back-end to “reserve €50.” The PSP’s logic then tracks a shipping API. When the parcel is delivered, the PSP’s logic tells the ECB back-end to “release the €50” to the merchant.

- **Managing Holding Limits:** The “pioneers” workstream successfully tested “waterfall” (defunding) and “reverse waterfall” (funding) mechanisms. These APIs will automatically move funds between a user’s digital euro wallet and their commercial bank account to enforce holding limits without disrupting the user experience.

**Next Steps and Timeline**

- **Next Steps:**

  - PSPs, FinTechs, and banks should immediately shift their R&D focus to designing value-added services (e.g., conditional payment logic, e-receipt integration) that build upon the proposed Eurosystem back-end.

  - Firms should gain hands-on experience by participating in all future “pioneers” and innovation workstreams to understand the technical implementation of the APIs.

  - Merchants and PSPs should develop strategies to leverage the digital euro’s pan-European reach to scale their new solutions across the entire euro area.

- **Key Timeline:**

  - **November 1, 2023:** The Eurosystem’s two-year “preparation phase” begins.

  - **February - May 2025:** The “visionaries” and “pioneers” workstreams are conducted.

  - **September 2025:** This outcome report is published, clarifying the technical architecture.

### Digital Assets Bank of Japan Working Paper No. 25-E-12: Private Law Frameworks for Tokenised Assets

**Comparative Legal Constructs:**

- **Switzerland (DLT Act):** Creates a new, bespoke legal category: the **“ledger-based security” (Registerwertrecht)**. The right is *legally constituted by* and *transferred only via* the DLT ledger itself.

- **Germany (eWpG):** *Extends existing law*. It creates **“electronic securities” (elektronisches Wertpapier)** and **“crypto securities” (Kryptowertpapier)** and legally *deems* them to be “property” (Sache) under the German Civil Code, applying established property law to them.

- **France (Monetary/Financial Code):** Amends existing law to allow securities to be recorded in a **“shared electronic registration system” (DEEP)**, legally recognising the DLT as a valid register.

- **United States (UCC Article 12):** Introduces a new, technology-neutral concept: **“control” over a “controllable electronic record” (CER)**. This is a *factual test* (power to avail, prevent others, transfer) that, if met, grants the holder legal property rights and “negotiability,” similar to the legal status of “possession” for a physical object.

**What it means for firms:**

- Firms in Europe and the US have a high degree of legal certainty.

- The paper contrasts this with **Japan**, which *lacks* a general law. Firms in Japan must employ complex, technical interpretations of existing laws (e.g., the **Companies Act** for uncertificated bonds, the **Trust Act** for beneficial interests) to structure tokens, thereby creating legal friction and uncertainty.

**Next Steps:** Firms are building on this legal certainty (e.g., Project Helvetia in CH, RSN in the US). Japan must decide whether to clarify its private law via new legislation or refined legal interpretation.

**Timeline:** Dated October 2025, this is a current analysis of legislation from 2023 to 2025.

### Digital Assets: DFSA Consultation Paper 168: Enhancements to the Regulation of Crypto Tokens

This paper proposes significant technical updates to the DFSA’s Crypto Token regime to align with international standards (e.g., IOSCO) and market feedback. The core change is a shift in the suitability assessment model.

**Key Technical Proposals:**

- **New Suitability Model:** This is the main proposal. It moves away from the current DFSA-managed “Recognised Crypto Token” list. Instead, it introduces a **firm-led “Suitable Crypto Token” model**. Firms must now conduct their own due diligence, “assess, evidence, and publish” the suitability of tokens they wish to use, based on criteria like transparency, liquidity, and technology.

- **Exception for Fiat Tokens:Fiat Crypto Tokens (stablecoins) are *excluded*** from the firm-led model. The DFSA will retain central responsibility for assessing and approving these, and will publish its own list of approved fiat tokens.

- **Collective Investment Funds:** The paper proposes removing restrictions that inadvertently capture mainstream funds (e.g., S&P 500 ETFs) if any constituent company holds crypto. The proposal *exempts* funds that track a *non-crypto* index, clarifying the rules and opening the door for new, flexible crypto-focused fund structures (like VCs or hedge funds) in the DIFC.

**What it means for firms:**

- Firms must build a new, robust **internal token assessment and governance framework** to comply with the “Suitable Crypto Token” model. This includes documenting and evidencing every decision to use a non-fiat token.

- Fund managers gain significant new flexibility. The removal of the restrictive thresholds allows for the creation of dedicated crypto-mandate funds that were previously unviable.

**Next Steps:** The next step is to receive industry feedback on the proposed rules.

**Timeline:** The deadline for submitting feedback on Consultation Paper 168 is **31 October 2025**.

### Digital Assets: FSI Briefs: Stablecoin-related yields (Oct 2025)

This brief examines how Cryptoasset Service Providers (CASPs) offer yields on payment stablecoins (which aren’t inherently yield-bearing) through mechanisms like re-lending, margin pools, DeFi facilitation, or loyalty programs. Regulatory approaches vary: the EU and HK ban issuers and CASPs from paying interest, while Singapore restricts retail access. The regulatory landscape is fragmented, creating compliance burdens for cross-border firms and potential arbitrage opportunities. Firms offering these products expose themselves and their users to significant risks (including liquidity, counterparty, operational, and legal risks) without traditional safety nets. Multifunction platforms face scrutiny over conflicts of interest.

- **Next Steps:**

  - **Regulators:** Develop coherent frameworks covering both issuers and CASPs. Address regulatory gaps and arbitrage risks through international coordination. Consider measures such as retail access restrictions, enhanced CASP prudential/conduct oversight, conflict-of-interest rules, mandatory disclosures, precise classification (differentiating between payment and investment), and cross-border supervision. US agencies need to issue regulations implementing the GENIUS Act.

  - **Firms:** Issuers must comply with interest payment bans. CASPs must navigate differing jurisdictional rules on yield products, potentially segmenting offerings by client type (retail/professional) or location. Enhance risk management for yield-generating activities. Enhance transparency and disclosure regarding risks and the yield of generation. Review internal structures to mitigate conflicts of interest.

### Digital Assets: CIGI Paper No. 331: How Will Stablecoins Integrate with the Financial System?

This paper analyses stablecoins as a form of “programmable financial infrastructure.” It dissects the main stablecoin archetypes and maps their adoption against potential geopolitical scenarios.

- **Technical Archetypes Analysed:**

  - **Fully Reserved:** A “narrow bank” model with 1:1 backing by high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) like short-term treasuries.

  - **Deposit Tokens:** Bank-issued, fractionally-backed tokens (e.g., JPMD) that are digital representations of traditional bank deposits.

  - **Tokenised Money Market Funds:** Regulated as securities, these are tokenised shares in a fund that holds yield-bearing assets (e.g., BlackRock’s BUIDL).

  - **Algorithmic Stablecoins:** High-risk models (e.g., the failed Terra/LUNA) that use on-chain mechanisms rather than reserves to maintain a peg.

- **Key Design Dimensions:** The paper emphasises that viability hinges on three technical/legal dimensions: **Reserve Integrity** (the quality and transparency of backing), **Consumer Protection** (explicit legal claims and bankruptcy remoteness), and **Compliance** (programmable AML/CFT).

- **What it means for firms:**

  - The paper frames stablecoins as a *neutral settlement asset* and *programmable monetary primitive*.

  - They can “unbundle” legacy card networks (which bundle authentication, risk, and settlement) by separating *final, instant settlement* (the stablecoin’s role) from *authentication* (the wallet/app’s role), thereby turning a complex, multi-party process into a simple two-party trade.

  - They act as a *bridge* between disconnected domestic fast payment systems (e.g., FedNow, Pix, e-CNY).

- **Next Steps:** Firms must develop strategies based on *which archetype* they will use (or compete against) and monitor the “race” between private, permissionless innovation and public, top-down CBDC projects.

- **Timeline:** The paper is dated August 2025, positioning this as an immediate and ongoing strategic challenge.

### Digital Markets: The Road to Crypto Regulation Part 2: Stablecoins (Chainalysis)

This August 2025 report analyses the global regulatory landscape for stablecoins, positioning them at an “inflexion point.” The market is evolving from a crypto-native trading tool to a key infrastructure for solving real-world inefficiencies, such as cross-border settlement. This evolution is attracting significant interest from traditional finance (TradFi), but its future path is now being dictated entirely by a new, fragmented patchwork of global regulations.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **Regulatory Fragmentation:** Firms face significant operational complexity. For example, the EU’s MiCA requires 30% of reserves to be held in bank deposits, while Japan’s 2023 regime requires reserves to be held onshore, primarily in domestic bank deposits. The UK and Hong Kong have different rules on reserve asset maturity and liquidity. These differences directly impact issuer profitability and business models.

  - **The Next Frontier: AML/CFT Ecosystem Monitoring:** Regulators are moving beyond simple intermediary-based AML/CFT. The Hong Kong Monetary Authority’s (HKMA) AML guidelines (August 2025) explicitly require issuers to utilise “blockchain analytics tools” to monitor “secondary market activity” and screen transactions/addresses *beyond* their direct counterparties.

  - **Mandatory On-Chain Analytics:** This new expectation requires issuers to adopt a “crawl-walk-run” approach to risk management:

    1. **Crawl:** Utilise dashboards (such as Chainalysis Sentinel) to review historical ecosystem activity.

    2. **Walk:** Create and review real-time alerts for high-risk transfers (e.g., to illicit addresses) and manually freeze funds as needed.

    3. **Run:** Integrate via API to automatically add addresses to blocklists and freeze funds.

- **Next Steps:**

  - Stablecoin issuers must build a compliance matrix to navigate conflicting jurisdictional requirements on reserve composition, redemption timelines (e.g., one business day in HK vs. 5 in Singapore), and custody.

  - Firms must immediately invest in and operationalise on-chain Know Your Transaction (KYT) and analytics tools (like Chainalysis Reactor and Sentinel) to meet the new regulatory standard of ecosystem-wide monitoring for illicit finance.

  - Monitor for “passporting” or mutual recognition agreements between jurisdictions with “comparable” regimes (a concept in the US GENIUS Act) to streamline cross-border operations.

### Digital Markets: The Convergence of AI and Distributed Ledger Technology (Clifford Chance & Deutsche Bank)

This September 2025 paper explores the powerful symbiotic convergence of AI and DLT (“AIxDLT”). It outlines four key use cases, demonstrating how AI can enhance DLT’s capabilities (e.g., smarter oracles) and how DLT can address AI’s fundamental challenges (e.g., data provenance). However, this convergence creates significant new legal and regulatory challenges by forcing immature, separate legal frameworks to overlap.

- **What it means for firms:**

  - **New Use Cases:**

    - **AI enhances DLT:** 1) **AI-Augmented Smart Contract Development:** Using AI for code generation, vulnerability analysis, and formal verification. 2) **AI-Powered Oracles:** AI agents can analyse complex, unstructured real-world data (news, video, IoT feeds) to verify conditions and trigger “smarter” smart contracts, solving the traditional oracle problem of limited data inputs.

    - **DLT enhances AI:** 3) **DLT for AI Training Data:** DLT can create an immutable, auditable trail for AI training data, proving its provenance and combating data-poisoning attacks. 4) **Agentic AI:** DLT provides a financial “infrastructure” for autonomous AI agents to own digital assets and transact peer-to-peer, enabling new models of economic activity.

  - **Complex Legal & Liability Overlap:** Firms must navigate a “spaghetti” of regulations. An AIxDLT application could simultaneously be subject to:

    - **AI Act (EU):** For rules on AI risk, transparency, and data quality.

    - **MiCA (EU):** For rules on the crypto-assets being transacted.

    - **GDPR:** For rules on personal data used by either the AI or DLT.

    - **Liability:** Creates novel questions. If an autonomous AI agent, operating on a DLT, makes a faulty decision causing financial loss, who is liable? The AI developer, the oracle provider, the DLT operator, or the agent itself?

- **Next Steps:**

  - Firms must create cross-functional teams (including legal, compliance, and tech) to analyse specific regulatory overlaps *before* launching an AIxDLT product.

  - Technology teams should prioritise pilot programs for high-potential use cases, such as DLT-based data auditing for AI models or AI-driven oracles for DeFi protocols.

  - Industry bodies must collaborate with regulators to develop clear guidelines and standards for this converged landscape, thereby avoiding the stifling of innovation.

- **Key Timeline:** This report is forward-looking and describes the state of innovation as of September 2025.
